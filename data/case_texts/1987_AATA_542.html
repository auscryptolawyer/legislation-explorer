NT85/200-201 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 542; (1987) 18 ATR 3275; 87 ATC 347 (2 March 1987)

Last Updated: 14 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - assessable income - emigrant - entitled to pension from Yugoslavia - pension paid to former wife in Yugoslavia -income derived - whether pension assignable - whether so assigned - whether exemption s23(q) applies - whether deduction of "self contribution" amounts to a tax.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss19, 23(q) (repealed)

Social Security Act 1947 s144.

Case 49 (1963) 11 CTBR (NS)

Case 122, Case R68, 84 ATC 482

DECISION AND REASONS

Re : NT85/200-201

Applicant

And : COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

NT85/200-201

AAT Decision No 3258

Tribunal : Mr BJ McMahon (Senior Member)

Date : 2 March 1987

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NT85/200 - 201

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: NT85/200 - 201

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant was formerly a resident of Yugoslavia. While there, he and his former wife were divorced. He became liable to pay some amount in respect of her maintenance. It is not clear whether this liability arose as a result of a court order or by way of agreement.

He was entitled to a pension from the Yugoslav Government which he has described as an invalid pension granted to him because of a heart condition.

Before he left Yugoslavia to emigrate to this country, he made arrangements for that pension to be paid to his former wife in satisfaction of his maintenance obligations. In consideration of these arrangements being entered into, his former wife signed a waiver agreement at the Australian Embassy in Vienna, indicating that she had no objection to his traveling to Australia.

The amount of the pension is 34.356 dinars per month which is approximately $A113.00 gross. Subject to a deduction which will be referred to later, it continues to be paid to the applicant's former wife.

The respondent has included the amount of this pension as part of the applicant's taxable income. The applicant (who was not represented) considers that he should not pay tax on monies that he does not receive and has objected to the inclusion of this amount in his taxable income. The disallowance of that objection is the subject matter of the present review.

The matters to be considered raise difficult question of law. Their resolution has not been made easier by the incomplete state of the evidence. I should say at the outset that the Commissioner's officers have gone to great lengths to assist the applicant to obtain and present evidence and submissions. Nevertheless, the documentation is not complete and I must do the best I can with what has been put before me.

Firstly, it is well settled that tax is imposed at the point of derivation of income. It is also well settled that income may be derived without having been received. If this were the applicant's only ground for objection, he must fail without further ado. There are, however, two arguments that possibly could assist him which I will now examine.

He might argue that he had validly assigned his right to future receipts of instalments of his pension to his former wife. The document by which he initiated this arrangement was not available. I am, therefore, unable to say whether it would amount to an assignment or not. It seems to me that the proper law governing the transaction would be the law of Yugoslavia. One can only say that it would be unlikely, having regard to the nature of invalid pensions, that one would find under the law of that country that they were assignable. Certainly, similar pensions are not assignable under Australian law. Section 144 of the Social Security Act 1947 provides that such a pension "shall be absolutely inalienable". Even if they were assignable in Yugoslavia, the lack of documentation does not allow me to say whether or not an assignment was properly effected.

A translation of a statement by the applicant's former wife was tendered. It does no more, however, than confirm that she receives the pension in question. If she is aware of the difficulties encountered in establishing the nature of her rights to the pension, she does not advert to them in her statement.

There was also a translated certificate from the "Self Managing Interest Community of Pension and Invalid Insurance of the Workers of Croatia". This did no more than certify that the applicant (emphasis added) was receiving the pension in question and certifying that the information was issued for court proceedings purposes.... "as since 28/12/1971 he authorised [his former wife] to receive his pension".

A statement from the Yugoslav Consul in Sydney also certified that the applicant (emphasis added) "is entitled to a minor amount as pension benefit".

All the evidence before me would indicate that so far as the Yugoslav authorities are concerned, it is the applicant who remains entitled to the pension, even though at his direction, it is paid elsewhere.

In my view, the arrangements, so far as they can be clearly established, amount to no more than a mandate. This operates as each receivable arises. It operates to preclude any actual receipt of the receivable by the applicant, but it does not prevent a constructive receipt by him of the amount. He has appropriated the receivable in the act of assignment.

If any further reinforcement of this view were needed, it is to be found in section 19 of the Income Tax Assessment Act which is in the following terms:

"19 Income or money shall be deemed to have been derived by a person although it is not actually paid over to him but is reinvested, accumulated, capitalized, carried to any reserve, sinking fund or insurance fund however designated, or otherwise dealt with on his behalf or as he directs."

The second argument that might have been available to the applicant in order to support his claim for exemption of the pension from income tax, would have been based on the former section 23(q) (since repealed). This ground was not referred to in his objection, but the respondent's representative agreed that it should be canvassed. Had it become necessary, I would have given leave pursuant to powers that are now vested in this Tribunal to amend the objection so that reference could be made to the argument.

Section 23(q), previously exempted from tax in Australia income "not exempt from tax in the country where the income is derived" provided the Commissioner is satisfied that the tax has been or will be paid.

The pension is, in fact, subject to a deduction equivalent to $A3.00 per month. This is referred to by the Yugoslav Consul as "Samodoprinos" which he translates as "self contribution". A translation of part of a payments slip apparently completed by computerised accounting was tendered together with a translation. It was issued by an organisation whose acronym is "SIZMIORH" which the translator has expanded to read "Self Managing Interest Community of Pension and Invalid Insurance of the Workers of Croatia at Split" (a town in Yugoslavia).

On the hearing of the application, the applicant thought that the amounts were deducted by the Municipal Council of Split to finance local works. It would appear more likely from the documents that the amount is deducted by this self managing interest community. It is possible, of course, that the amounts deducted find their way to the Municipal Council to help finance public works.

Publications were made available to explain what was meant by a self managing interest community. A copy of the Yugoslavian Constitution was tendered, together with a copy of an extract from a book entitled "Form and Content of Social Property" by Professor AG Chloros, professor of comparative law at the University of London. These documents point to the unique features of economic regulation within the Yugoslav community. The formation of decentralised self managing interest communities appears to be widespread and their use in the management of the Yugoslav economy appears to be well understood.

In the chapter on Yugoslavia, in a publication of the International Bureau of Fiscal Documentation, Professor Jelcic of the University of Zagreb gives a sketch of the taxation structure operating within Yugoslavia. He confirms that apart from general taxes levied on citizens, there are classes of payments described by him as "contributions received by self managing communities of interest'". These contributions are utilised to finance the collective needs of the recipient community and its members. Self managing communities of interest can be formed, for example, in the field of water supply or in the field of road facilities. The present contributions would appear to fall within the field he has described as self managing communities of interest in the field of communal activities. Dealing with them, Professor Jelcic says:

"The collective needs arising on the part of self managing communities of interest' are financed using the contributions accumulated for communal construction by the various socio-political communities, OAL's and work organisations, and from certain other economic resources."

So far as I have been able to establish the facts from the evidence presented before me, it would seem likely that the deduction from the applicant's pension consists of a contribution to the Self Managing Community of Interest described above.

The question then arises whether such payments are a tax to which ex-Australian income must be subject before it could qualify for exemption under the former section 23(q).

It was held in (1963) 11 CTBR (NS) Case 49 that taxes on income imposed by the States of New York and Colorado fell within that concept. In other words, the tax referred to in the sub-section is not necessarily a national tax. However, it seems to me that the deductions that I have described do not at all amount to a tax. They are more akin to the social insurance payments made to Saudi Arabian authorities that were considered in (1984), Case R68, 84 ATC 482. It seems to me that the tax contemplated by this sub-section is a compulsory levy or imposition by a sovereign state. Even if the levy was imposed by the Split municipal authorities it would not amount to a tax in this sense. A levy by a co-operative organisation furthermore would not have the character of a tax, even though that co-operative organisation was formed within the context of the legal organisation of a State. Such an acknowledgement seems to be implied in the certificate of the Yugoslav Consul when he said:

"The above amount [referring to the pension] is subject to payment of 'saraotoprinos' ("self contribution") which is equivalent of tax payments on income." (emphasis added)

The deductions may be similar to taxation in the sense that they are deducted at source. That of itself, however, would not characterise them as tax for the purpose of the sub-section.

It has not been demonstrated, therefore, that the pension in question was not exempt from tax in the country in which it was paid. This argument for exemption, therefore, will not be available to the applicant.

I conclude that the decision under review should be affirmed. Quite apart from the legalities that must lead me to this conclusion, it seems to me not unjust that this should be so. The applicant had an obligation to his former wife. If he had not satisfied it by redirecting his pension, he would have had to satisfy it by periodical payments made from Australia. He would not have been entitled to a deduction for these payments. Justice and fairness are often strangers to taxation law. In this case, however, I consider that they walk hand in hand.
