REASONS FOR DECISION

25 March 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant (CA) is a retired Chartered Accountant (born 1917) who, prior to his retirement, was a member of a nationally prominent firm of Chartered Accountants (NPF). For many years before joining NPF he conducted a practice on his own account in a regional city. He was married with one child and I am satisfied that he was the controlling influence in the management of the family's financial affairs. By the 1970's, as he approached 25 years in practice, CA was in partnership with others. In earlier years he had provided most of the money needed to be provided by the partners in order to fund the operations of the business. In 1971, when new partners were introduced to the practice, it was decided to re-arrange financial support for the practice by establishing a company (Finco) to provide for the financial needs of the practice. Finco thereafter provided finance by way of loans at interest: a facility previously provided by the partners through their capital accounts and loan funds. To enable Finco to advance the required monies, Finco had to be provided with funds and that was achieved when Finco borrowed from other companies associated with partners in the firm. One entity to lend at interest to Finco was Famco, a company established to serve the interests of CA and his family. Famco in turn borrowed from members of the family of CA and from a "family settlement". CA lent his available funds to Famco interest free and, to the limits of his credit with Famco, drew on those funds at will using Famco as his Banker. At 30 June 1974 Famco owed CA $58,775.

  1. From 30 June 1974 the practice previously conducted by the local firm was taken over by NPF and all that remained for the former firm to do was to wind up its affairs. That involved the collection of fees due on accounts rendered but unpaid; receiving its share of fees from work-in-progress; and finalising work in hand for clients who had chosen not to have their affairs taken over by NPF. NPF handled the work involved in collection of outstanding fees (a process which took more than two years) and work-in-progress. The task of finalising work in hand for those clients whose work was not being transferred with the practice was physically carried out by NPF but with accounts being rendered in the name of the old firm. All work in the latter field was finalised within a few months and certainly well before 30 June 1975.
  2. When the accounts of the old partnership were taken and such distributions to partners had been made as the partners determined, the funds remaining were insufficient to enable the partnership to repay Finco the monies it had borrowed. To overcome that difficulty CA borrowed monies from Famco and in October 1976 lent that money ($19,452) to the old partnership. The old partnership then repaid Finco the monies it had borrowed at interest; and Finco in turn was able to repay Famco. In the same year CA also borrowed from Famco at interest to invest in NPF. In all, he borrowed $25,000 from Famco at interest. He repaid $10,000 principal in the year ended 30 June 1977. In relation to those borrowings he claimed deductions for interest paid. The Commissioner allowed deductions pursuant to s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") in sums of $499 in the first year and $399 thereafter. In doing so the Commissioner acted on the basis that only the interest related to the advance of monies to NPF was deductible.

4. Having resided for many years in the regional city; having optimism for its future, based (inter alia) upon Government promoted schemes for its development; and having confidence in a personal friend who was the leading estate agent in the city in the commercial and industrial field, CA was persuaded that a particular parcel of land would prove to be a sound and profitable investment. As a result he arranged for Famco to purchase the site in July 1975 for $112,000. At the time of purchase no arrangements had been made for the property to be tenanted. However, I accept the evidence of CA that he believed that there were sound letting prospects available even in the short term. The site was held under two titles. Lot 2 was a rectangular allotment with a street frontage. It was not developed. Lot i was a much larger allotment. It comprised a site with a street frontage. It adjoined Lot 1, but it extended to a greater depth than Lot 1 and it extended in an L configuration across the rear boundary of Lot 2. The only development on Lot 1 was in the rear corner of Lot 1 remote from Lot 2. That development comprised an iron-clad shed with a concrete floor. It enclosed approximately 560 square metres. It was suited to light industrial usage.

5. The expectations of CA that the property could be immediately let proved to be unfounded and it was said that this became a matter of some concern to his wife (Mrs. CA), who had not favoured the purchase. She was the principal creditor of Famco. (Although she was the principal creditor of Famco, the child was the principal shareholder). It was then decided that CA would become a monthly tenant of the entire property at a rent of $10,400 p.a. plus all outgoings. (I am not persuaded that the principal reason for the letting was to allay such concerns as the wife had. I am satisfied that CA was too capable an accountant not to be influenced by the advantages to the family of him claiming as tax deductions the losses being incurred for the time being.) There was no formal lease and the only record of the matter is a Minute of resolution of 8 August 1975 recorded in the Minute Book: of Famco. That Minute was not the result of any formally conducted meeting, but simply composed as a result of the Directors (CA; his wife; and two partners of CA, whom he acknowledged to be merely his 'nominees') assenting after being consulted by CA. CA commenced paying rent at the rate of $10,400 per annum, and, using the services of his estate agent friend, tried to procure suitable tenants. Success was slow in coming and no rent had been received by 30 June 1976. It was only in about in February 1977 that a tenant was found for that portion of the site on which the shed suited to light industry was sited; and CA commenced to receive rent. There was no formal lease or agreement for lease and in consequence the limits of the area of exclusive occupancy over which the tenant had possessory rights were not precisely defined by any document. Nor were they established by other evidence. By June 1977 CA had received rent amounting to $1,175 and incurred outgoings on account of rent for the year of $7,900. No other expenses were claimed. CA acknowledged the rent to be assessable income and claimed all of the rent as a deduction.

6. Towards the end of the calendar year 1976, before the last-mentioned tenancy commenced, the agent had located prospective tenants (Alpha and Co.) who were willing to contemplate a long-term tenancy of Lot 2. Their interest was conditional upon the site being developed with suitable structures. As a result, if the tenancy was to eventuate, it was necessary that further funds be invested in a construction program developing the lands of Famco. Negotiations ensued and agreement in principle was reached on the basis that there was to be a long-term tenancy at full market rental with Alpha and Co. (A lengthy type-written Minute dated 14 December, 1977 (sic) recorded the essence of the proposal which by then had long since been implemented with some variations to the original proposal).

7. Negotiations took place with a view to arranging finance and effecting construction. CA approached his Bankers. They were not prepared to lend to Famco without the security (inter alia) of CA's guarantee, but CA considered the consequences which would ensue in the event of default by the tenants would be too severe for him. He would not be able to claim tax deductions on paying out any amounts called on the guarantee. However, through NPF he had met a client of that firm, who was a financial broker. CA approached the broker and, out of their discussions, there emerged the possibility of borrowing from one of NPF's audit clients, but such a borrowing was not acceptable under the self-imposed code of conduct of NPF. However, an alternative to "borrowing" found favour with the relevant national partner of NPF. The proposal which found approval was that funding would be made available through the client in terms of a "commercial lease". (That was the evidence of CA. It was not challenged and I accept it).

8. The result was a series of transactions. First, CA surrendered his tenancy of Lot 2 as informally as the tenancy had been established. He did so to enable new legal relationships to come into effect. The rent payable by CA for the residue of the lands was reduced to $6,000 per annum. (Having regard to the rent received by CA and the rents claimed by him, it seems that the reduction took effect from the commencement of the shed tenancy, rather than that of Alpha and Co. That could explain why rent to 30 June 1977 was $7,900.) As a result of the surrender by CA, Famco was entitled in possession to its interest in Lot 2 as holder of an estate in fee simple. Thereupon:-

(a) Famco by a "first lease" granted an estate as lessee to Mrs. CA for a term of 10 years and 6 months commencing 1 January 1977 and terminating 30 June 1987 at a total rent of $10,060 for the term;

(b) by a "second lease" Mrs CA granted an estate as lessee for a term of 9 years 364 days commencing on 30 June 1977 and terminating on 29 June 1987 at a total rent of $10,000 for the term payable by quarterly payments in arrears of $250 each. Under that lease the lessee (whom I shall refer to as "Nominees") was a company within a group of one of the major Australian trading banks;

(c) by a "third lease". Nominees granted an estate as lessee to CA for a term of 9 years 363 days commencing on 30 June 1977 and terminating on 28 June 1987 "at a total rent for the term of $150,652"; and

(d) by a "fourth lease", CA granted an estate to Alpha and Co. for a term of 9 years 331 days commencing on 1 August 1977 and terminating on 27 June 1987, at an initial yearly rent of $18,200 payable by equal monthly installments in advance.

(The first three of those leases, including the first under which the estate was to commence six months earlier than under the other two, were executed on 30 June, 1977. The fourth lease was executed on 5 October 1977).

9. Common to all of the leases except the fourth is reference to the "completion of the warehouse complex". The concept, although referred to in the first lease, is only defined in the later leases. The complex was defined in those leases to "mean and include the warehouse and showroom complex described in the plans and specifications initiated (sic) by the parties hereto"; and reference to "completion of the warehouse complex" was to mean the date of the last to occur of (inter alia) "the date upon which (Nominees) obtains a valuation of the warehouse complex at a sum of not less than $160,000". Provision was made in the second and third leases for rescission in the event of non-completion of the complex by 30 June 1977. No such provision appears in the last lease: a circumstance probably explained by the fact that the "complex" was already completed and occupied by Alpha & Co. before that lease was executed. The characteristic which most strikingly distinguishes the third lease from the first and second leases is the far greater particularity with which the obligations of the lessee are defined, although the stipulations are basically the same. The fourth lease follows a different format from the other three leases, but with the exception of provision for rent escalation and provisions as to consideration payable, there is no material distinction between them. However, it is noteworthy that, although CA only held his estate to 28 June 1987 without right or option to extend the term, by the fourth lease he conferred on Alpha & Co. an option to extend their term for a further 5 years.

  1. The first three leases were not the only documents executed on 30 June 1977. On that day Famco executed a deed of guarantee and indemnity in favour of Nominees, to protect it against breach or default by CA under the terms of his lease.
  2. Further, by another agreement entered into that day between Mrs CA and the Nominees, it was provided that:-

"In consideration of the grant by (the wife of CA) to (Nominees) of a lease of the property (as previously mentioned) (Nominees) shall pay to (the wife of CA) upon the date of the execution of the said lease by way of premium for the grant of the said lease the sum of $100,000."

  1. As to the matter of payment obligations. Alpha & Co. had no obligation to make any payment prior to 1 August 1977. On that date they became due to pay $1,516.66 to CA. That obligation was to continue for 24 months, after which date the rent was liable to be increased by agreement or, in default of agreement, in accordance with a determination of "current market value" to be made by an independent nominee of the President of the Australian Institute of Valuers. Similar reviews were to take place each two years thereafter (cf. below).
  2. Although CA was not scheduled to receive income pursuant to the lease of Lot 2 until 1 August 1977 his obligations to pay rent required him to pay $40,000 to Nominees "on the date of completion of the warehouse complex" and thereafter to effect 40 quarterly payments of rent in arrears. The first, second and third of those quarterly payments were to be of $3,170; the fourth, $13,170; the fifth, sixth and seventh, $2,750; the eighth, $12,750; and the remainder to be of $2,096 each; a total of $150,652.
  3. In turn. Nominees was to pay Mrs. CA a total of $10,000 by quarterly payments in arrears of $250 each and, pursuant to the premium agreement, $100,000 on 30 June 1977. Mrs. CA was to pay Famco $10 per month in advance up to "date of completion of the warehouse complex"; and thereafter quarterly payments in arrears of $250.

31. It is also of some significance that the lease from Famco to Mrs. CA did not oblige her to construct the complex. Nor was she obliged to do so by the terms of either the lease to Nominees or the premium agreement whereby she was to receive $100,000 from Nominees in consideration of the grant of that lease to Nominees. However, express provision was made in the lease to Nominees of 30 June 1977 entitling Nominees to rescind the lease ab initio if the complex was not completed by 30 June 1977 (sic) in which case it was provided that "neither party shall be liable to pay to the other any sum for damages, unpaid rental costs or expenses or have any rights against the other, either at law or in equity or have any right to recover from the other any monies paid (under the lease)". There was no similar provision in the premium agreement, probably for the good reason that it was not executed until 30 June 1977 either.

  1. I am not only satisfied that CA was the dominant influence in relation to the financial aspects of his family's affairs but I am also satisfied that he brought to that task his own professional skills as an accountant: an exercise in which his abilities were enhanced by his capacity to draw on the expertise within NPF. I am satisfied that in all that occurred, whether in deciding that Famco would purchase the realty; or that CA would become monthly tenant of the whole property at a substantial rent when no other tenants were available who would generate income; or in implementing the arrangements involving Nominees - tax considerations, and in particular income tax considerations (as distinct from other taxes such as Stamp Duty), were an important factor and influence. That is not to suggest that it is improper in any sense that he or others should have been so influenced.
  2. It was 1943 when Viscount Simon in Latilla v IRC C (1943) A.C. 377 at 3813 expressed his criticism of "those (who) enjoy the benefits of residence in this country without sharing in the appropriate burden of British taxation". That comment prompted Jordan, C.J. to say in the Estate of Vicars C(1945) 45 S.R.N.S.W. 85 at 933 "No doubt those who, like Viscount Simon, regard it as a patriotic duty to pay the largest amount of taxes lawfully exactable will so arrange their transactions as to attract a maximum of liability (although without, it is to be hoped, going to the length of a timely suicide, as foreshadowed by Lord Atkinson), and those who, like Lord Macnaghten and Lord Atkinson recognize no such duty, will order their affairs so as to incur liability for no more than is legally necessary. We are not, however, concerned with the desirability or morality of the course taken in the present case, but only with its legal operation and legal consequences. It is now well settled that the substance, in law, of a transaction can be determined only by ascertaining what the transaction really was". As I understand it, the latter view is now accepted as the correct view.

34. For that reason I am of opinion that in relation to matters of income taxation, citizens are not obliged in their capacity as taxpayers or as citizens to subordinate the interests of their families to the Revenue, or to be unthinking about tax considerations in their commercial undertakings, or that they should forego utilizing their own skills in their own dealings or avail of the right to themselves of the skills of others in order to only incur such tax liabilities as the Parliament, acting with the advice of its advisers and in exercise of its legislative powers, provides for by law. In appraising the evidence of CA in these matters I am satisfied that CA did use his own professional skills, aided by the circumstance of his membership of NPF, to advance the interests of his family in priority to those of the Revenue.

35. I am satisfied that, having done so, he has given undue importance in his thinking to the written record of things done as if the recording constituted the happening of things recorded. Subject to those considerations, I am satisfied that:-

(a) CA was completely honest in his evidence. I observe that he was quite candid and open, not only in acknowledging his awareness of the tax significance of the things done, but also in the presentation of the records for Famco, Mrs. CA and himself;

(b) CA quite frankly conceded that income tax was a significant consideration for him, although asserting that it was not the only consideration influencing the matters he undertook;

(c) CA acknowledged that the "commercial lease" concept was devised as an alternative to borrowing; that the "amount" initially made available by Nominees. in practical terms was $60,000 - rather than the $100,000 paid by Nominees to Mrs. CA - because, contemporaneously with payment of the premium, Nominees received payment of rent in the sum of $40,000 from CA; that the return on investment sought by Nominees was calculated by reference to the amounts invested by Nominees; and that the surrender of the lease by CA was arranged on the basis that Nominees secured its required return on investment in an amount appropriate to the duration of its investment;
  • (d) the arrangement enabled the building to be constructed at a cost of the order of $100,000; and
  • (e) although he had hoped to profit long-term from the arrangement, in the short-term he expected "tax losses" which he could set-off against his professional income:- all to be counter-balanced in due course by augmentation of his post-retirement income - expected to be reduced following his planned retirement in June 1980. (It was not suggested that he might have planned to terminate his role in favour of others after claiming the tax reductions available from the early net losses.)

36. In appraising the claims touching the tenancies it is appropriate to distinguish several distinct phases:-

(a) 12 July 1975 - 1 August 1975: In this period Famco was owner of the site and in possession of the whole of it. No claim arises for consideration.

(b) From 1 August 1975 to commencement of first tenancy: I accept that from 1 August 1975 CA became a monthly tenant of the whole property on a rental of $10,400 per annum plus outgoings. The evidence satisfies me that at some date, probably about February/March 1976, CA was able to let the developed portion of the property to a third party with whom he was dealing at arm's length. As to the period prior to the commencement of that tenancy, I am not satisfied that CA had any objective of deriving assessable income by way of rent such as would increase his taxable income as a consequence of letting the property. indeed in his evidence he acknowledged that at that time he had no wish to increase his taxable income, then principally gained from the pursuit of his profession. I accept that. It would have been quite remarkable if a person in his circumstances had preferred to add rent to his professional income when it could have been derived by associated entities and persons in the interests of the family and, in being so derived, would be subject to lower rates of tax. That is confirmed by considering the advantages which were to be had by the family, and which were sought by CA in the interests of the family, when he assumed liability for a substantial rent as a monthly tenant at a time when no suitable tenant was available for any of the property at a reasonable rent; and when CA believed that he would be entitled to deduct all rent and other expenses incurred by him in consequence of his tenancy. The Commissioner has allowed a deduction of $1,965 - I am not persuaded that that is in any way inadequate. To that extent the claim will fail.

(c) From commencement of that tenancy to 30 June 1976: In this period CA derived $1,965 by way of rent from the tenant of the developed portion of the property. I am satisfied that in this period his dominant objective was to reduce the loss arising from the holding of the whole property by letting the only portion of it for which a tenant could then be found. To the extent to which that portion was let, I am satisfied that an appropriate portion of the losses and outgoings incurred are deductible. The total losses and outgoings claimed for the year amount to $7,900. The Commissioner has allowed a deduction for the year of $1,965. CA, who bears the onus of persuading me that the amount allowed by the Commissioner should be greater, has not persuaded me that the allowance was inadequate. To that extent, the application fails.

(d) From 1 July 1976 until 30 June 1980 (excluding Lot 2 and from 30 June 1977): I make the same findings

- as under the previous sub-paragraph. I am not persuaded that any greater amount than the amounts allowed should have been allowed.

(e) From 30 June 1977 until 30 June 1980 in relation to

Lot 2: To that subject and the difficult questions

it poses I now turn.

37. Upon taking an overview of the matter in relation to Lot 2, CA determined upon a course of action which would involve the derivation of assessable income over the next ten years by deriving rental income from a property to be let by him. To succeed in that objective it was necessary for him to secure the capacity to let the premises. To acquire the premises he entered into a head lease as tenant of Nominees. He did so on terms which would provide him with a profit to be derived over the period of ten years - assuming that all parties concerned carried out their obligations: whether to make possession of the premises available, or to pay whatever was necessary to ensure continued entitlement to possession. It was proposed that the premises would be let in a developed state providing facilities required by Alpha and Co. Before the rights and obligations could be assumed viz a viz Alpha and Co., it was necessary that the premises be developed at substantial cost and so made available as part of the property to be leased by Nominees to CA.

38. That was made possible because Famco, as the holder of the estate in fee simple, was prepared to suffer Mrs. CA to develop its properties; Mrs. CA was prepared to develop the property and to let it at a nominal rental to Nominees subject to payment by Nominees of a substantial premium and sub-letting to CA; Nominees was prepared to let the property to CA on terms which would ensure to Nominees a satisfactory return upon its total investment by way of premium and rent; to achieve that Nominees would let the property for ten years to CA with the "rent" to be paid in irregular amounts by regular installments; and in turn the premises would be sub-let by CA to Alpha and Co. at a rent (greater in aggregate than that payable by CA to Nominees) which would be payable by regular and equal installments, save only for such increments as would arise by reason of biennial rent reviews.

  1. At this point it is appropriate to observe that Counsel for the Commissioner did not rely upon the provisions of s.260 of the Act although, in a later case (V83 88 A.T.C. 325) , Counsel sought to challenge the view that s.260 of the Act cannot apply to defeat or reduce any deduction otherwise truly allowable under s.51 [cf. Cecil Brothers Pty. Limited v. F.C.T. (1964) 111 C.L.R. 430D. No such contention was made in these proceedings.
  2. In appraising the tax significance of what CA did it is appropriate to remember that it is what he. did which is a matter of primary concern. The circumstance that what he did might have been made possible or desirable by the tax status of others (e.g. possibly the tax exempt status of Nominees); or of the non-income character of moneys received by others (e.g. the premium received by Mrs. CA) must not be allowed to obscure that. Nor, on the other hand, are such matters to be treated as wholly irrelevant. As Brennan, J. said in Ure v. F. C. of T. 81 A.T.C. 4100 at p.4104:-

"... where a question arises as to the purpose for which money is laid out by a taxpayer, it is erroneous to exclude as irrelevant evidence of the use of that money, albeit by others, in conformity with arrangements made by the taxpayer. That is not to deny the separate identity of those who use the borrowed money, nor to attribute vicariously to the taxpayer the activities of others. It is simply to take some relevant factors into account in determining the purposes for which money is laid out. Those purposes do not depend upon the state of the taxpayer's mind but upon what the taxpayer in the circumstances of the case is ascertained to have done in using and arranging for the use of the borrowed moneys."

41. However, if for the moment one may put aside the consequences which flowed for others (for example, the enhancement of the value of the freehold owned by Famco), it can be seen that the test propounded by a Full Bench of the High Court of Australia in Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. F.C. of T and Tongkah Compound N.L. v. F.C.of T. [(1949) [1949] HCA 15; 78 C.L.R. 47 at pp.56-57 - approved by a Full Bench of the Federal Court in Magna Alloys & Research Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 80 A.T.C. 4542 at p.4545] was satisfied.

The High Court had said:-

"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' mean in the course of gaining or producing such income...

In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income."

42. That is not sufficient to wholly dispose of the matter because it might be that the evidence establishes the co-existence of a "purpose" other than a purpose of producing assessable income with the result that it may be necessary that an apportionment of the "losses and outgoings" be effected in accordance with the principles laid down in the Ronpibon decision. That was the situation which arose in Ure (ante). As Brennan, J. said in that case at p.4104:-

"... a question arises as to whether it can truly be said that the incurring of interest charges at rates of 7.5%, 8.5%, 10% and 12.5% per annum is incidental to the gaining of income by way of interest at the rate of 1% per annum. The answer to that question does not turn directly upon the disparity in interest rates, but upon an examination of the purposes for which the money was laid out. The disparity in interest rates is itself eloquent to suggest the existence of purposes ulterior to the earning of interest at the rate of 1% per annum, and the evidence confirms the existence of further purposes. The loans of the borrowed moneys made by Mr. Ure were calculated to achieve the further purposes of providing some income to Mrs. Ure, of enhancing the profits of Listohan, of providing Mr. Ure and his family with the Epping residence at a nominal rental, and of enabling the Joan Honeybourne Trust to take the benefit of any increment in the capital value of the Epping property and to enjoy the rents and profits of that property when it became available for letting at a commercial rental."

His Honour went on to say in the circumstances of that case that the purposes of the borrowing -

"do not depend upon the state of the taxpayer's mind but upon what the taxpayer in the circumstances of the case is ascertained to have done in using and arranging for the use of the borrowed moneys.

The purposes for which money is laid out is an issue of fact, turning upon the objective circumstances which human experience would judge to be relevant to the issue. In the present case, there is an air of unreality about the proposition that the borrowed moneys were laid out wholly for the purpose of earning a return of 1% per annum. Rather is it right to say that the purpose for which the borrowed moneys were laid out included all the purposes earlier mentioned, only one of which was to earn a return of 1% per annum."

43. In that case the "air of unreality" lay in the circumstance that there was no prospect at all of overall commercial, financial advantage to the taxpayer from the loan transaction. That cannot be said for this taxpayer in relation to Lot 2. He stood in prospect over a decade of an overall commercial, financial advantage. But neither is that finding sufficient to determine the matter because, as has been illustrated in F.C. of T. v. Ilbery 81 A.T.C. 4661 (as to interest) and F.C. of T. v. Creer 86 A.T.C. 4318 (as to rent) it does not always follow that amounts paid as "interest" or as "rent" will be considered as allowable deductions. In Ilbery the taxpayer borrowed $20,000 repayable at the expiration of thirty years with interest at the rate of 14% per annum. Immediately following the borrowing, the taxpayer handed to his lender $14,000 paid in exercise of an option to prepay interest for a period of five years. In exercise of a right to do so, the lender then executed a deed whereby the loan was assigned to a company of which the taxpayer and his wife were directors and shareholders. The transaction was entered into at a time when the applicant was purchasing real estate for letting purposes with the purchase price being substantially funded by borrowings from a bank and a finance company. In disallowing the claim, Toohey, J. said (at p.4668):-

"At the time of the prepayment of interest the taxpayer had not acquired any property from which he hoped to derive income. There was no relevant income-earning undertaking or business in existence. That is not necessarily fatal to an argument that an outgoing incurred is 'incidental and relevant' to gaining or producing the assessable income. But what is under consideration here is a prepayment of interest made at a time when no relevant income-producing activity had begun and not made for any purpose connected with such an activity. The money was not outlaid to gain income; it was not incidental or relevant to the gaining of income."

As a result, his Honour concluded -

"... that the sum of $14,000 represented by the prepayment of interest was not an outgoing incurred in the course of gaining or producing the taxpayer's assessable income..."

That decision can be distinguished by the Applicant at least in form in that he derived $7 in income during the year of income ended 30 June 1977 (as asserted in his income tax return and not challenged) so that the payment of the initial $40,000 was contemporaneous with the commencement of the income-generating letting to Alpha and Co. However, I prefer to consider more substantial matters.

44. The Commissioner's argument is better supported by the decision in Creer. In that case a solicitor decided to invest in residential property by leasing it from its owner and then sub-letting it to tenants. He also had in mind that his family company would purchase the property subject to the head lease to him. He took leases of the properties which provided for a five year term and for payment of the total rent for the five year term by payments in advance of one installment each in each of three consecutive years. The first installment was payable on 30 June and comprised 80% of the total rent for the five year period. Immediately following the granting of the leases, the taxpayer's family company purchased the units from the owner subject to the taxpayer's tenancy. In considering the question whether the payment of $18,260 in three instalments as provided by the lease amounted to a "periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate to payments" or was more correctly "a final provisional payment so as to secure its future use or enjoyment" [cf. Dixon, J. in Sun Newspapers Limited; Associated Newspapers Limited v. F. C. of T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337 at p.363], Fisher, J. said (at p.4324):-

"Gibbs A.C.J. referred in the S.A. Battery Makers case (supra) at ATC p. 4417; C.L.R. p. 654 to Dixon J.'s classic statement and at ATC p. 4417; C.L.R. p. 655 he said as follows:

'However the present case cannot be resolved simply by applying these tests. The real problem in the case is not to determine the character of the advantage sought, once it has been identified, but to decide what was the advantage sought by the taxpayer by making the payments. If the only advantage sought was the right to possession under the lease, and what was called 'rent' really answered that description, clearly the outgoings were entirely of a revenue nature. If on the other hand one advantage sought by the outgoings was the acquisition of a capital asset (the land and buildings), the fact that the payments were called 'rent' and were made periodically, would not necessarily prevent them from being in part outgoings of a capital nature - see Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 68; (1953) 89 C.L.R. 428; Poole and Dight v. F.C. of T. 70 ATC 4047; [1970] HCA 25; (1970) 122 C.L.R. 427; and A.P.A. Fixed Investment Trust Company Ltd. v. F.C. of T. (1948) 8 A.T.D. 369 at p. 371.'

I have again emphasized the significant words.

His Honour acknowledged that to call something 'rent' does not conclude the matter, but that it was necessary to determine whether what was called 'rent' really answered that description. Likewise as he said "the fact that the payments were called 'rent' and were made periodically, would not necessarily prevent them from being in part outgoings of a capital nature". Nor in my opinion does it conclude the matter to find that that which the taxpayer sought was the right to possession under a lease. I read the Acting Chief Justice as saying that it is only if that which is sought is a leasehold interest and also that which is paid is properly called rent, then and then only is the outgoing necessarily on revenue account.

The test adopted by Dixon J. in Sun Newspapers (supra) is equally helpful, namely to determine whether the amount is a 'capitalized sum payable by instalments' or 'rent accruing de die in diem for the use of the thing'. The word 'capitalized' has dictionary meanings which are presently apt, namely 'converted into capital' or 'computed present value of income'.

In my opinion, on the true construction of the lease aided by the evidence as the manner in which the total payment was calculated, it is apparent that the amount called 'total rent' is in the words used by Sir Owen Dixon 'a capitalized sum' which was made payable as to 80% forthwith and thereafter by two instalments each of 10% on the anniversaries of the first payment."

The claim was disallowed.

45. The final decision of a Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia to be considered in this context is that of F.C. of T. v. Gwynvill Properties Pty. Limited 86 A.T.C. 4512. The Commissioner relied upon that decision in argument but, at the time this matter was heard, the High Court of Australia had yet to pronounce upon an application for special leave to appeal. The application was later refused. That being so, despite the strong dissent of Fisher, J., the decision of the majority in that case must be given full respect and effect.

46. In Gwynvill a company had borrowed in the course of its business. In doing so it assumed an obligation to pay interest quarterly in advance and the first such payment of interest was made on that basis. It was obliged to pay future quarterly instalments in advance but, if called upon to do so, to prepay five years interest in advance. The borrowing was for twenty-five years and the principal sum was $2,400,000. The lender called for the immediate payment of interest for the five year term so that, within the first of the relevant years of income, the taxpayer had paid the first installment of interest payable quarterly in advance ($69,271); and on the next business day and within the first year of income a further $1,500,000 for interest in respect of the next five year period. Jackson, J. pointed out at p.4527:-

"It is no doubt true to say that for some purposes (compare Commr of Stamp Duties (N.S.W.) v. Baystone Investments Pty. Ltd. 78 ATC 4448; (1978) 1 N.S.W.L.R. 441) the amount of the loan should be treated as being $2,400,000 and not as being that sum reduced by the pre-payments of interest. Again, it is true to say that the respondent made the pre-payments of interest from money which it had kept separate from the $2,400,000. Neither of these matters, however, requires one to regard the amount actually available to the respondent in consequence of the 'outside loan' as being $2,400,000 when in fact the net amount immediately available as the 'outside loan' was $830,729, i.e. the $2,400,000 reduced by the payments of $69,271 and $1,500,000... (That being so) the outgoings appear reasonably capable only of being seen as an attempt to obtain a large tax deduction for the borrower in the year ended 31 July 1978 at a net cost to the group of some $86,000 odd.

In these circumstances I am of the view that the whole of the payments of $69,271 and $1,500,000 did not satisfy the requirements of the second limb of sec.

51(1). Nor in my view could they be regarded as being in toto 'losses and outgoings ... incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' in terms of the first limb of sec. 51(1). The outgoings in toto were simply incurred in seeking to gain an allowable deduction. Compare F.C. of T. v. Ilbery..."

47. Neaves,-J. at p.4523, in considering the 'essential character' of the payments in question, drew attention to five features and then proceeded to say:-

"Taking these matters into consideration, I am unable to conclude that the transaction as a whole is explicable as being appropriate to achieve the espoused business end of supplementing from an outside source the funds available to the taxpayer from its own resources and those of the Franklin's Group. Whatever else the transaction may have achieved, it did not achieve that result and was not capable of doing so. If the purpose of the taxpayer was to obtain such funds, the transaction was not apt to achieve that purpose."

48. The findings so made by their Honours were of course findings of fact, such as Brennan, J. had spoken of in Magna Alloys (ante). Being but findings of fact, they cannot determine the assessment to be made of the evidence in this case or of the characterization of the transactions in question. In particular, it is to be observed that this was not an instance of receiving an amount by way of loan and almost immediately returning in the guise of interest an amount equal to a large percentage of the "principal" (cf. my reasons for decision in Case T37 86 A.T.C. 309 at p.318). However, more particularly, the claim of the Applicant relates to rent. That being so the use to the Applicant of the leased property was not diminished in the way the amount of money available to be used was reduced by the interest payment.

CONCLUSION; LOT 2

49. When one comes to assess the evidence in this case in relation to Lot 2, leased by the Applicant from Nominees, I am satisfied that the Applicant was about a long-term income-producing purpose in incurring the liabilities he did. Further, I am satisfied that he has met all the requirements of s.51 of the Act in that regard with the exception of expenses "of capital, or of a capital nature". In my view the expenses he incurred in establishing the entirety of the arrangement for the ten year lease did constitute expenses of capital or of a capital nature. In particular, I am so satisfied in relation to the initial payment of "rent", notwithstanding that the payment and the obligation giving rise to it referred to it as "rent". That payment did not have an naturally recurring character. Nor did the amounts of -

$4,270 - Legal Costs and' Stamp Duty 1977;

$3,000 - Legal Costs of Lessor 1977;

$3,000 - Costs Re Lease 1978;

$5,000 - Agent's Commission 1978.

It follows in my view, that only the other expenses claimed ($24,167 - 1978; $21,475 - 1979; and $2,880 - 1980) are allowable as deductions. For completeness, I add that the failure long-term to generate a "profit" and the premature bringing to an end of the Applicant's income-earning project with associated costs does not alter that.

CONCLUSION: LOT 1

50. As I have said, I am not persuaded in relation to Lot 1. I am not persuaded that in relation to that Lot the relevant "purpose" of the Applicant in holding the property as a tenant and so incurring expense was only a "purpose" of deriving assessable income. In my view, the circumstances were comparable to those in Ure (ante). The only difference is that, instead of conferring a positive financial advantage on others (the provision of loan moneys very cheaply: Ure) CA sought to advantage others by protecting them from loss. I am not persuaded that any greater deductions should be allowed than have been allowed.

CONCLUSIONS: INTEREST

  1. There remains a question as to deductibility of interest paid to Famco in relation to the professional practice. I have not been persuaded by the evidence presented to me that the liability was incurred to Famco for interest arose out of the application of borrowed moneys to any income-producing purpose. Accordingly, applying the principles already cited from Ronpibon (ante), I am not satisfied that any deduction is allowable.
  2. Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that the decisions of the Commissioner be varied to reduce taxable income by the sums of $24,167, $21,475 and $2,880 in the years of income ended 30 June 1978, 1979 and 1980 and that, in all other respects, the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections under review be affirmed.
