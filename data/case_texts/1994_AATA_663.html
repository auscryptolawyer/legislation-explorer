Stokes and Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] AATA 663 (8 November 1994)

Last Updated: 22 September 2008



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

SUPERANNUATION - preservation of superannuation benefits by a resigning eligible employee - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise late election - whether recognition of late election is just and equitable in all the circumstances



Superannuation Act 1976, ss 137, 157



Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

Commissioner for Superannuation and Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation (Federal Court of Australia, 246/1994, 4 May 1994)

Miller and Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported, AAT Decision No 9666, 11 August 1994)



AAT Decision No 9825

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: MARK EDWARD STOKES



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION



Tribunal: Dr DJ Grimes, Senior Member

Mr NJ Attwood, Member

Mr HG Julian, Member



  1. This is an application for review of a decision by the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 28 April 1994 confirming a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner dated 16 May 1993 under subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”), not to direct that the election made by the applicant on 7 June 1991 under subsection 137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that subsection.

  2. The applicant appeared at the hearing, and gave oral evidence before the Tribunal. The respondent was represented by Mr Simon Adams. The Tribunal had before it documents produced by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and further material lodged at the hearing.

  3. The general facts before the Tribunal are not in dispute. The applicant was born on 17 March 1959 and was educated to Higher School Certificate level. He joined the Commonwealth Public Service in 1979. He was initially employed in the Department of Health where he worked for 18 months as a clerk in the Personnel Section. He later joined the Department of Finance in the computer section and is at present Acting Assistant Director of the Local Area Network.

  4. In May 1985 the applicant took recreation leave and leave without pay in order to travel. He had applied for 3 weeks leave without pay and was due to resume work on 27 July 1985. In evidence the applicant said he found there was work available in England and decided to resign and work overseas. He was able to work in England, having obtained a 2 year work permit before leaving Australia.

  5. The applicant wrote a letter of resignation to the Department dated 11 July 1985, resigning with effect from 27 July 1985. This letter was received by the Department on 22 July 1985. A second letter dated 27 July 1985, confirming this resignation was sent by the applicant in the belief the first had not been delivered.

  6. A Form S2A was forwarded to the Department and signed by the applicant and witnessed by his father (who was an adult contributor to the Superannuation Fund) dated 23 May 1985 i.e. 2 days before the applicant left Australia. The applicant gave evidence that this form was in fact completed by him in London in July 1985 and returned to Australia for his father to witness. The form was backdated to May 1985 to give the impression his father had witnessed it. The applicant said he had done this as he believed nobody in England would be eligible as a witness.

  7. The applicant’s letter of resignation stated:
“ 11.7.85

The Computer Centre Manager

Dept. of Finance,


As I have decided to remain in Europe, I wish to tender my resignation from the A.P.S., with effect at the expiration of my leave (27 July 1985).


Could you forward all monies owing to me to:


21 Kavel St

Torrens 2607


I have enjoyed working very much at Finance and take this decision with some regrets. However, I look forward to seeing you on my return, perhaps in a few years. Could you please pass my regards on to all the staff.


Yours sincerely

M Stokes”


  1. The S2A form was headed:

“Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributors/ Lump Sum Benefits or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights.”


  1. The relevant section of the form was worded:

“ I, Mark Edward Stokes of 21 Kavel St, Torrens 2607 formerly employed by Dept. of Finance in the State of A.C.T. as Senior Operator Grade 2.


apply for a refund of accumulated contributions

Hereby * apply for payment of lump sum benefits

elect for preservation of superannuation rights


* Delete whichever is inapplicable”


The words “apply for payment of lump sum benefits”, “elect for preservation of superannuation rights” were scored out.



  1. The applicant stated that he gave no consideration to any alternative other than to apply for a refund of his contributions. He believed this was his only option and had never known of anyone resigning from the service to do otherwise. He believed he thought the alternatives on the form were there because it was a multipurpose form and only the first option applied to him.

  2. In evidence the applicant said that nothing in his service in the Personnel Section of the Health Department made him believe there was any other option available to him. No counselling or advice was provided to him but he conceded this was not possible when overseas. The accumulated contributions were placed in a term deposit. Evidence was given that the money was not used until he attended Canberra University on his return to Australia when he purchased a motor vehicle to travel to and from campus.

  3. The applicant gave evidence that he returned to Australia in August 1986 and rejoined the Department of Finance again becoming an “eligible employee” for the purposes of the Act on 8 September 1986.

  4. It was the applicant’s evidence that he attended no induction courses on the two occasions he joined the Department of Finance because of pressure at work. He added that when he resigned in 1985 nothing in writing was sent to him to indicate preservation of benefits was an option. The first knowledge he had of the option of preservation or late election was from a newspaper article and on receipt of this knowledge he immediately took steps to apply.

  5. The applicant agreed he would have received information statements sent out by the respondent, in 1987 and 1988 containing references to the preservation of contributions on resignation. He did not recall receiving this information and did not believe it would have put him on notice to enquire about his own situation as it would not have been clear that this was relevant to him.

  6. The applicant gave evidence that his decision to take a refund of his contributions was influenced by the fact that this was the only option he had seen others take while working in Personnel and the only option discussed by his colleagues at work.

  7. The difference to the applicant’s superannuation entitlements should his request for late election be granted is not great and illustrated in the following table:

Pension % of Salary Age 55 Age 60 Age 65

With Preservation 38.16 46.91 52.5

Without Preservation 35.64 45.56 51.88



  1. The applicant noted that these figures illustrate the younger his age of retirement the greater the disparity between the benefit with and without a late election.

  2. The applicant gave evidence that in the present atmosphere of privatisation by Government and the contracting out of Government work he feared he may be made redundant and produced evidence about the possibility of contracting out the work of his section. The applicant conceded, however, that no program of redundancies had been developed and that with his expertise it was likely that he would be offered other work in the Department before being made redundant. He also conceded that his fears of early redundancy were purely speculation and had arisen since his application for late election.

  3. The respondent noted that the application for late election in this case was 5 years and 10 months after the expiration of the period prescribed by section 137 of the Act. It was submitted that the main reason given by the applicant for this delay - a lack of knowledge of the option to preserve his right at the time of resignation and the assumption that he only had the option to obtain a refund of his accumulated contributions - did not constitute a reasonable excuse for his actions.

  4. The respondent also noted that form S2A, although superseded, clearly puts the reader on notice that he should make further enquiries as to whether the options on the form may apply to him. The applicant had previously worked in the personnel section of the Department of Health and had knowledge of the process and the desirability to seek further information when in doubt but did not do so. The claim by the applicant that because he was in England at the time, had no fixed address or occupation and was more concerned with immediate problems and therefore did not make enquiries, meant that he could not reasonably be expected to seek the information required, was not a valid reason for not applying the legislation. The respondent pointed out that the applicant was in England voluntarily; was difficult to contact and indeed the department could only contact him through his parent’s address. The fact that in filling out form S2A he filled the form in with a date prior to leaving Australia could have indicated that he had made his mind up to resign before going.

  5. The respondent noted out that since returning to the workforce, information concerning the preservation of benefits has been circulated to the departmental officers which also should have alerted the applicant to his rights.

  6. The respondent argued that the applicant’s contention that it was reasonable for him to make a decision based on what he had seen other colleagues do when working in Personnel, that is in making the election for refund and not preservation, was not relevant.

  7. The relevant legislation is contained in sections 137 and 157 of the Act. It provides:

"s137

(1) A person who -

  • (a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply, and upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

  • (b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.



(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

  • (a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and

  • (b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."

S157

(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

The Division referred to in section 137 is Division 3 of part IX and refers to “preservation of rights of persons ceasing to be eligible employees”.



24. In deciding the question of whether or not to exercise the discretion under s157(1) the Tribunal has recourse to the principles laid down in a number of previous AAT decisions. (See Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870 and Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307). It is widely accepted that guidance for the test of whether it is desirable in all the circumstances of the case to allow a late election is found in the decision of the Federal Court in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344. Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation and Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239 considered there to be no reason for applying different considerations to the exercise of the discretion in s 157 and that to extend a procedural time limit in litigation. In each case, he stated, the question was whether it is fair and just that the party be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had they acted within the prescribed statutory time limit.

25. The Tribunal considers that the power is not unrestricted in its terms as it specifies that the Commissioner must be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised. ...". The Tribunal is also mindful that a wide discretion is curtailed by the general purpose and policy for which the discretion is granted. The Tribunal in Re Lander considered that the discretion conferred by s 157(1) was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". In Liddle it was said that the word "desirable" strongly suggested a positive connotation, that is something "worthy of achievement" rather then "merely advisable" and that "objectively good reasons" must exist which justify non-compliance with a statutory time limit. The applicant in essence claimed that the facts of his case amounted to objectively good reasons which therefore made the decision to allow a late election desirable.



  1. More recently the comments of Davies J and Cooper J in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation (Federal Court of Australia, 246/1994, 4 May 1994), and Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman (supra), cited in Miller and Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported, AAT Decision No 9666, 11 August 1994) are noted.

Davies J:



”Many such provisions, including s.157, are not concerned with rights. If an act is not performed within the prescribed time, there is no right to have it performed. But there is a discretion to permit time to be extended should it be appropriate in the circumstances to do so. In the exercise of this discretion, it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time. Section 157 merely requires that the Commissioner ‘be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised’. ‘All the circumstances’ include all relevant circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised.



In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s.137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances need not be of a medical character. In Mr. Chalk’s case, the change in circumstance was that he resigned from Telecom intending to establish himself in an entirely new career as a farmer but was unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again took up employment with Telecom, after a lapse of two years.



The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded.”



Cooper J:



“The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration. In the instant case the Tribunal found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him under the Act and that neither Telecom nor the Commissioner advised him of the available choices. The Tribunal correctly treated as a fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the failure of Telecom and the Commissioner to correctly inform the appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault. Rather, as explained by Davies J. in his reasons, the section merely requires that the Commissioner ‘be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised’. Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified by Davies J. as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the discretionary power in section 157 to treat an election as within time was enacted.”



Von Doussa J.



“In s.157(1) ‘desirable’ is not referable to a state of mind - whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late election is ‘desirable’ is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning ‘something worthy of achievement’ does not imply other than an objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the end, whether the recognition of a late election is ‘something worthy of achievement’ must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.”



  1. The Tribunal notes the recent findings of the Federal Court and in determining the question before it, has carefully considered all the evidence presented to it and notes in particular, the following evidence. That the applicant worked in the Personnel Section of the Department of Health before transferring to the Department of Finance. That before travelling overseas on leave in 1985 the applicant obtained a two year work permit for the United Kingdom, indicating the likelihood that he would seek and gain work in the United Kingdom and therefore leave the Department, yet he took no action to inform himself of the alternatives available to him should he resign before leaving for overseas.

  2. When he resigned from the Department the applicant gave no indication that he would be seeking re-appointment, made no enquiries as to any alternatives he may have had regarding superannuation and made a choice in filling out the form S2A to request a refund of his contributions, ignoring the alternatives provided. The Tribunal is of the opinion that sufficient information was available to the applicant regarding superannuation options when he resigned to alert him to seek further information. That he did not seek to clarify the situation at the time was due to his own actions.

  3. On return to the Department of Finance the applicant made no enquiries of the Department’s Personnel section or of the Retirement Benefits Office as to any rights he may have for consideration of his previous service, nor did he attend an induction course. The applicant was not alerted to the existence of the possibility of preservation of superannuation rights through superannuation circulars in 1987 and 1988 and could not recall reading the information contained in them.

  4. The applicant obtained a refund of his contributions and received the benefit of the interest rate obtained from a term deposit until he made use of the funds to purchase a car on returning to Australia. The request for late election was more than 5 years after the period prescribed by the legislation.

  5. On retirement at the normal retirement ages of 60 or 65 the increase in pension payable to the applicant is less than one percent of salary if late election is permitted. In any case the retirement benefit is not inconsiderable.

  6. The applicant contended that accepting the advice of colleagues and the actions of others observed when he worked in Personnel, was a reasonable basis for making his election to take a refund of contributions. The Tribunal does not agree, noting the decision in Chalk that no reasonable person should rely on advice of that nature, and if they do so, they bear the consequence.

  7. The Tribunal notes that prima facie the rules should be followed for the requirements of the administration of the fund. The exercise of the discussion provided for in section 157 of the legislation should in the words of Davies J in Chalk “be extended should it be appropriate in the circumstances to do so” having regard to all the circumstances of the case.

  8. In considering the all the relevant circumstances of this case the Tribunal could find nothing which suggests the application of the normal rules relating to the time limit out of which the election should have been made should not be followed. The exercise of the discretion allowed under section 157 is neither justifiable nor desirable on the facts. On the evidence before it, the Tribunal is not of the opinion that to exercise the discretion contained in section 157 of the Act would be fair in order to do justice between the applicant and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.

  9. Accordingly, the decision not to treat the election made by the applicant as if it had been made in the prescribed period is affirmed.
