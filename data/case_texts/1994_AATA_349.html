Harts Pty Ltd and Steven Irvine Hart and Tax Agents' Board of Queensland [1994] AATA 349 (23 November 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

HARTS PTY LTD AND STEVEN IRVINE HART v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF

QUEENSLAND

No. Q93/666

AAT No. 9860

Number of pages - 18

Freedom of Information

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

K L BEDDOE (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of Information - Application by tax agent - access to written complaints and notes in respect of oral complaints - documents irrelevant to claim - further documents required to be filed - relevant notices to be sent to third party - interim decision only

Freedom of Information - Disclosure prohibited under Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - information in respect of affairs of another - deletion would render documents meaningless

Freedom of Information - Disclosure could reasonably have substantial adverse effect on proper and efficient conduct of operations of agency - contrary policy of Tax Agents Board not to deal with allegations that cannot be put in detail to agent - public interest requires investigation of allegations

Freedom of Information - Disclosure of identity of confidential source of information - not evident on face of documents

Freedom ofInformation - Disclosure would found action for breach of confidence - limitation of disclosure to purpose for which information given - no implied pledge of confidentiality - breach of duty does not alter operation of Act - implied consent

DECISION

K L BEDDOE By undated requests under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the Act") the applicants applied to the Australian Taxation Office for access to all written complaints and diary notes or memoranda regarding oral complaints as to the conduct of the applicants which had been received by the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland and/or the Australian Taxation Office. I infer that those applications were lodged in the Taxation Office on or about 2 August 1993 and were subsequently transferred to the respondent Tax Agents' Board of Queensland. By a letter dated 23 August 1993 the Secretary of the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland advised the applicants, through their solicitors, that access to the documents was not granted and the basis for the exemption was said to be paragraph 37(1)(b) of the Act. By a further letter dated 7 October 1993, once again addressed to the applicant's solicitors, that decision was affirmed in the same terms. By an application dated 19 October 1993, the applicants applied to this Tribunal for review of the decisions of the Secretary of the Tax Agents' Board. I was told from the bar table that the Australian Taxation Office had been advised of these proceedings but had elected not to seek to be joined as a party.

2. Since making the decisions under review the respondent has changed its stance quite substantially and now relies upon a number of provisions in the Act to justify the claim for exemption of the documents.

3. Subsection 3(1) of the Act provides that the object of the Act is to extend as far as possible the right of the Australian community to access to information in the possession of the Commonwealth Government by:

"(a) making available to the public information about the operations

departments and public authorities and, in particular, ensuring that

rules and practices affecting members of the public in their dealings

with departments and public authorities are readily available to persons

affected by those rules and practices; and

(b) creating a general right of access to information in documentary form

in the possession of public authorities, limited only by exceptions and

exemptions necessary for the protection of essential public interest and

the private and business affairs of persons in respect of whom

information is collected and held by departments and public authorities;

and

(c) . . . "

Subsection 3(2) provides that it is the intention of the Parliament that the provisions of the Act shall be interpreted so as to further the object set out in subsection 3(1) and any discretions conferred by the Act shall be exercised as far as possible so as to facilitate and promote, promptly and at the lowest reasonable cost, the disclosure of information.

4. Paragraph 37(1)(b) of the Act provides that a document is an exempt document if its disclosure under the Act would or could reasonably be expected to disclose or enable a person to ascertain, the existence or identity of a confidential source of information, or the non-existence of a confidential source of information, in relation to the enforcement or administration of the law.

5. Subsection 38(1) provides for exemption of documents where disclosure of the document or the information contained in the document is prohibited under a provision of an enactment specified in Schedule 3. Schedule 3 of the Act includes subsection 16(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

6. Subsection 16(2) provides that an officer shall not either directly or indirectly, except in the performance of any duty as an officer, and either while he is, or after he ceases to be an officer, make a record of, or divulge or communicate to any person any information respecting the affairs of another person acquired by the officer as set out in the definition of "officer" in subsection 16(1). The definition of officer is stated to be, unless the contrary intention appears, a person who is or has been appointed or employed by the Commonwealth or by a State and by reason of that appointment or employment, or in the course of that employment, may acquire or has acquired information respecting the affairs of another person, disclosed or obtained under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act or of any previous laws of the Commonwealth relating to income tax.

7. Paragraph 40(1)(d) of the Act provides, subject to subsection 40(2), that a document is an exempt document if its disclosure under the Act would, or could reasonably be expected to have a substantial adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct of the operations of an agency. Subsection 40(2) provides that section 40 does not apply to a document in respect of a matter in the document, the disclosure of which under the Act would, on balance, be in the public interest.

8. Insofar as it is relevant to this application, section 41 provides for exemption of a document if its disclosure under the Act would involve the unreasonable disclosure of personal information about any person including a deceased person. Subsection 41(2) provides that the provisions of subsection 41(1) do not have effect in relation to a request by a person for access to a document by reason only of the inclusion in the document of matter relating to that person. Insofar as the section deals with information provided by a qualified person (as defined) acting in his or her capacity as a qualified person, those provisions are not relevant to the present matter before the Tribunal.

9. The respondent also relies on subsection 45(1) of the Act which provides that a document is an exempt document if its disclosure under the Act would found an action, by a person other than the Commonwealth, for breach of confidence. It was not suggested that the exclusions in subsection 45(2) of the Act apply on the facts of this case.

12. Exhibit 4 in these proceedings is a copy of each of the documents which are still in dispute before the Tribunal and in relation to which an order under section 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 applies.

13. Part VIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is a scheme for registration of tax agents. Division II of that Part provides for the creation of a Tax Agents' Board in each State and also for the registration and re-registration of tax agents. An essential feature in the decision of the Board to register or to re-register a tax agent is whether the natural person or, in the case of a company, the nominee of the company, is a fit and proper person within the terms of section 251BC of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. That section sets out the criteria to determine whether a person is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns. Besides the specific tests the section also requires, in effect, that the person is a fit and proper person according to ordinary concepts. The phrase "fit and proper person" takes its meaning from the context in which it appears (Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 94 ALR 11 at 56). The general meaning of the phrase is clearly set out in the judgment of the High Court in Hughes and Vale Proprietary Limited and Anor v. The State of New South Wales and Others (No 2) [1955] HCA 28; (1955) 93 CLR 127 at pages 156-7. It is not necessary that I do more here than recognise the responsibility of the Board to ensure on an application for re-registration as a tax agent, that the person continues to be a fit and proper person within the terms of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and in the case of a company, that the nominee of the company is a fit and proper person, also within the terms of the said Act.

14. It was in that context that the respondent Board conducted an investigation between June and November 1992 in relation to applications for re-registration as tax agents by the applicants in these proceedings. The re-registration of both the applicants was delayed as a result of that investigation but I presume that the applicants had the benefit of subsection 251JC(4) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 until such time as the Board was to serve a Notice of Refusal to Re-register on the applicant. As it transpired, both the applicants were eventually granted re-registration as tax agents.

15. The present claim under the Act by the applicants has its genesis in a letter dated 17 August 1992 by the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland, addressed to the solicitors for the applicants. The letter refers to an interview in relation to certain allegations which are detailed in the letter involving a company by the name of Harwell Investments Pty Ltd trading as Trans Commercial Insulated Truck Bodies. The letter makes it clear that the interview is to be conducted on a formal basis with evidence being taken on oath.

16. It seems that the respondent Board receives complaints from time to time about registered tax agents. Those complaints apparently come from taxpayers, other regulatory authorities, and other tax agents. The policy of the Board is that when a complaint is received about a registered tax agent the Board will investigate the complaint to ascertain whether any action on its part is warranted. As a first step in the process of investigating the complaint the Board will require the consent of the complainant to the Board making known the allegations against the agent to the agent. It seems that if the complainant does not wish the Board to make those allegations known to the tax agent in respect of whom the complaint is made, then the Board does not pursue the matter any further. In other words, it seems to be the policy of the Board not to deal with allegations that it cannot put in detail to the agent against whom the allegation is made.

17. Paragraph 10 of the affidavit of Barry James Rienecker reads as follows:

"In a general sense the documents which are presently in issue

are documents which are concerned with complaints made by

various people and entities about the Applicants. In

determining that the documents in question were exempt

documents I took the view, which I still hold, that it is an

important part of the function of the Board to be able to

receive complaints and to treat those in confidence unless the

consent of the complainant to release the details of the

complaint has been received. The Board has received legal

advice, which it has acted upon, to the effect that the consent

of complainants must be obtained before details of a complaint

may be disclosed to the tax agent concerned. It was, and is,

my view that if it were to become public knowledge that the

fact and details of complaints made about tax agents were

released to those tax agents without the consent of those

complainants then people would be less willing to complain."

18. It will be apparent that there is an essential paradox in the Board's policy. It says that it takes no action in relation to complaints made in relation to tax agents unless the complainant consents to the allegation being put to the tax agent and then, on the other hand, says that if it were to become public knowledge that the facts and details of complaints made about tax agents were released to those tax agents, without the consent of those complainants, then people would be less willing to complain. The Board, apparently, has not explained to people who make allegations that it takes no action in respect of those allegations unless the person making the complaint consents to the allegation being put to the registered tax agent. It would seem, as a matter of common logic, that if complainants were told that no action would be taken on their complaints unless they consented to the allegations being made known to the agent concerned, that those persons who did not wish the allegations to be made known to the agent, would simply cease to make the allegations to the Tax Agents' Board. That seems to me to be the clear effect of Mr Rienecker's affidavit (Exhibit 1). The apparent inconsistency in the Board's position in these proceedings is heightened by the affidavit of Phillip Couchman, which is marked as Exhibit 2. In paragraph 3 of that affidavit, after referring to the proposed affidavit of Mr Rienecker which is exhibited to the affidavit of Mr Couchman, Mr Couchman goes on to say:

"On 15 June 1994, I personally spoke to either the

complainant, or a representative of the complainant in each

of the cases of complainant numbers 6, 11 and 12. Each of

the persons to whom I spoke confirmed to me that:

(a) the complaint made had been made in expectation that it

would be kept confidential and the identity of the

complainant would not be made known to the applicants; and

(b) that the complainant requires the Board to maintain that

confidence."

No evidence was given to explain why it was necessary for Mr Couchman to contact those complainants on 15 June 1994, which is a time years after the complaints were made. I should add that the complainants numbered 6, 11 and 12 are in fact the complainants numbers 3, 8 and 9 as set out in Exhibit 3 which is set out in full above. It seems to me on balance that I should accept Mr Hastie's submission that the evidence of Mr Couchman in his affidavit is merely hearsay on hearsay and I am therefore unable to find that there is any basis to support a view that those complaints were received by the respondent Board in confidence. I think I should infer from the fact that Mr Couchman found it necessary to ring those complainants several years after the complaint was received to ascertain whether or not those complaints had been put to the Board in confidence suggests to me that the Board did not understand them to have been in confidence and, in fact, acting in accordance with its policy, proceeded to take action in relation to the complaints.

19. The documents relating to complainant 4 make it clear that that complainant was asking for the Tax Agents' Board to take action against the applicants and also because of a second letter received by the Board from the same complainant, it is apparent that the Board was taking action because the letter which is document 19 requested the Board to postpone action in relation to the complaint. The complaint by complainant number 9, which is document 60, is a detailed complaint which clearly infers in its last paragraph that the respondent Board would be taking some action, and there is nothing in the letter to suggest that the material has been given to the Board in confidence. Document 61, which is the complaint by complainant number 10, was actually made to the Australian Taxation Office and included a request that the material contained in the letter be advised to the Tax Agents' Board with an indication that the complainant was not satisfied that the applicants are competent to hold a Tax Agent number. It may reasonably be inferred from that letter that this complainant was also expecting the Board to take action, and once again there was nothing in the letter which suggests that the material was sent to the Taxation Office in confidence and certainly the Taxation Office was asked to pass the details on to the respondent Board. Taking all these factors into account, I am satisfied that I should not give any weight to Mr Couchman's affidavit that the material was supplied by the complainants in confidence. In the result, I am satisfied that Mr Couchman's affidavit does not assist the Tribunal in these proceedings. In coming to that conclusion I have taken into account the fact that Mr Hastie did not call Mr Couchman for cross-examination, and I have taken into account the submissions of Mr Hack in relation to that. My particular reason for not giving any weight to the evidence of Mr Couchman is the fundamental inconsistency between the evidence of Mr Rienecker and the evidence of Mr Couchman. That fundamental inconsistency was raised by the Tribunal during the course of the hearing. If, as Mr Hack says, the information is received by the respondent under an implied pledge of confidentiality, which he says is implicit in all the material, then in the light of Mr Rienecker's evidence, that the Board would not act on such information, there seems to be a real problem with the evidence which has not been dealt with in the course of these proceedings.

20. The respondent relies upon the operation of section 16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 to justify the claim for exemption under section 38 of the Act. Mr Hack also drew attention to document 62 which includes a tax file number protected by the Privacy Act 1988.

21. As to documents obtained from confidential sources, counsel submitted that the confidence of the source may be either express or implied. It was acknowledged that where information is supplied under an express or implied pledge of confidentiality it is the pledge of the respondent Board that is relevant. It is the respondent's case that there was an implicit pledge of confidentiality in the material before the Tribunal.

22. I turn now to the documents identified in Exhibit 3 and which have been found to be relevant to the application by the respondent.

23. Document 1 is a complaint in writing to the respondent by a government agency which also forwarded to the respondent a letter of complaint by a company and referred to two complaints by others. The covering letter includes the following:

"We would ask that you treat this matter confidentially in the best

interests of these clients and investigate the background to their

complaints."

It will now be apparent that a request, so made, was outside the practice of the Board as set out in paragraph 8 of Mr Reinecker's affidavit (Exhibit 1). There is no material to suggest that any approach was made to the government agency or the complainant's nominated by the agency seeking consent in terms of the Board's practice.

24. Documents 3, 4 and 5 are misdescribed in Exhibits 1 and 3 as letters from the respondent Board, but are in fact copies of letters dated 26 November 1986 in the name of "J. E. Scanlon, Deputy Commissioner of Taxation" addressed to three of the complainants. Document 3 is addressed to the government agency and includes the following sentence:

"This office has communicated directly with the clients involved in order

that an investigation may be carried out and disciplinary action, if

necessary, be imposed on the firm."

The letter does not disclose the nature of the "disciplinary action" which the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation had in mind.

25. Document 6 is a letter dated 28 November 1986 from the government agency addressed to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation withdrawing one of the complaints. Document 7 is a letter addressed to the Australian Taxation Office dated 1 December 1986 from the original complainant also withdrawing the complaint.

26. Document 8 is a further letter dated 3 March 1987 from the government agency to the Board stating that the agency had not had a reply to date on the matter. There is nothing in these letters to suggest that the complainants had withdrawn the express request for confidentiality.

27. I am therefore satisfied that each of these complaints was made in confidence with disclosure to be limited to the purpose for which the information was given, i.e. for the Board to investigate the complaints but not so as to disclose the identity of the complainants to the applicants. That is the reasonable explanation of the request for confidentiality in Document 1 and which applies by reference to Documents 2, 6 and 8.

28. I am further satisfied that because of the policy set out in paragraph 8 of Mr Reinecker's affidavit the information was provided under an express or implied pledge of confidentiality. It seems to me to be not relevant that the Board then breached the confidentiality by referring the complaints to the Australian Taxation Office. It follows, in my view, that the breach of the duty of confidence by the Board does not alter the operation of the Act. The exemption provided by subsection 45(1) will apply. The information was not only said to be confidential but was given in circumstances where the complainants understood that the Board had an obligation of confidence unless the complainant agreed to allow the complaint to be disclosed. That there was an unauthorised use of the information by the disclosure to the Australian Taxation Office and whether that unauthorised disclosure would found an action for breach of confidence seems to me to be of no moment in this case, but, in any event, unlikely to succeed (cf. Allied Mills Industries Pty Ltd v Trade Practices Commission [1981] FCA 11; (1981) 34 ALR 105).

29. However, more fundamental is the duty of confidence imposed by the circumstances in which the information was supplied, importing an obligation of confidence against the rest of the world. Document 1 makes it clear that the information was supplied on this basis and Mr Reinecker's affidavit makes it clear in paragraph 8 that it was accepted on this basis.

30. I am therefore satisfied that subsection 45(1) operates to exempt documents 1 to 8 because to disclose those documents under the Act would found an action by the complainants for breach of confidence. In coming to that conclusion I have relied upon the judgment of Gummow J in Corrs Pavey Whiting and Byrne v Collector of Customs and Anor [1987] FCA 266; (1987) 74 ALR 428 at 433. In so doing I recognise that his Honour was there dealing with subsection 45(1) as it was enacted before the amendment was made in 1991.

31. I turn now to documents 13 to 16. Document 13 is an undated letter of complaint, probably written in 1989, relating to the taxation affairs of Harwell Investments Pty Ltd and another taxpayer. The letter is addressed to "The Secretary, Tax Agents' Registration Board" and has been written in the context of on-going enquiries by the respondent Board. There is nothing in the letter to suggest that the contents of the letter were given in confidence nor do the contents of the letter suggest an obligation of confidence by the addressee. The writer is not one of the persons approached by Mr Couchman immediately prior to the hearing of this matter.

32. Document 14 is a letter dated 22 July 1992 addressed to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Brisbane, (once again the schedule misdescribes the recipient as the Board), by a firm of solicitors acting for the author of document 13. The letter includes two assertions of fact which are relevant. The first is to the effect that after the letter, which is document 13, was sent to the Board in 1989, the Secretary of the Board gave the author a verbal undertaking that "any information passed by him (I presume the Secretary) to the Taxation Department would under no circumstances be revealed in any ensuing investigation".

33. The second relevant assertion of fact in the letter is that the author of document 13 "has recently been contacted by an officer of your Department, ... to discuss the contents of his letter and also to conduct an interview pursuant to Section 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act". The letter goes on to discuss the possibility of the author being subjected to cross-examination in relation to the letter which is document 13. It is not clear from the letter as to how it was envisaged that the author could be subject to cross-examination

34. Two things are readily apparent. The letter which is document 13 is not asserted to have been given in a situation importing an obligation of confidence - such is not asserted by the solicitors - and the Board did not regard itself as being under an obligation of confidence because it passed the letter on to the Taxation Office. The Board was not required and did not assume the obligation of confidence except as it is required to do pursuant to section 16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

35. The solicitor's letter really only relies upon section 16 and makes no claim as to breach of confidence nor does it assert that their letter was given in confidence.

36. I am satisfied, therefore, that subsection 45(1) cannot apply to either of documents 13 and 14.

37. As to whether disclosure would have a substantial adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct of the operations of the Board; if the assertions in document 14 are correct in fact, then it must be the case that the Australian Taxation Office intended to use document 13 in the course of formal proceedings which could include cross-examination of the author. There is nothing in Mr Reinecker's affidavit to contradict this assertion by the solicitors. There is nothing in the contents of document 14 which disclosure could or would in any way reasonably be expected to have a substantial adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct of the Board, given the Board's policy. I am therefore satisfied that paragraph 40(1)(d) cannot apply to documents 13 and 14.

38. Because I am not satisfied that documents 13 and 14 were given in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence, nor is there anything in document 13 to the effect that the author did not want the identity revealed and at least the Australian Taxation Office apparently did not regard itself under any obligation of confidence because of the assertions in document 14, I am not satisfied that paragraph 37(1)(b) can apply to document 13 and cannot apply to document 14 for the same reasons. There is nothing before me to establish that the assurance of confidentiality sought by the solicitors was ever given. No other exemption provision appears to be relevant.

39. Document 13 is, however, a document containing personal information about the author which is, in my view, relevant to the complaints as to conduct by the applicants. There is, however, nothing before me to establish that notice has been given in accordance with section 27A of the Act, notwithstanding that the decision under review and the claims made in this Tribunal do not include a decision or claim that document 13 is an exempt document under section 41 of the Act.

40. As it appears the notice required by section 27A has not been given and it will therefore be necessary for the respondent to give that notice prior to the Tribunal proceeding further in handing down its decision in these applications unless I find that section 41 applies to exempt the documents. Because no claim for exemption under section 41 has been made by the respondent, the parties did not address the section in their submissions to the Tribunal.

41. I therefore propose to have this matter listed for further hearing on a date to be advised and after notice of these proceedings has been given to the author of document 13. That hearing will exclude the applicant. A hearing will not be necessary if the author of the document and the respondent elect to make written submissions.

42. Documents 15 and 16 are computer printouts prepared by the Australian Taxation Office. The claim for exemption is confined to section 38 which in turn relies on subsection 16(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. There is, however, nothing in these documents relevant to complaints as to the conduct of the applicants. In my view the documents are not relevant to the request for access and should not therefore be released. If I am wrong as to the relevance of the documents, I would have held that they are documents coming within the terms of subsection 16(2) and therefore exempt under section 38 because they refer to the income tax affairs of two taxpayers, disclosure of which is prohibited by subsection 16(2) except by an officer, as defined, in the performance of any duty as an officer.

43. I turn now to documents 17, 18 and 19. Documents 17 and 18 are both dated 3 April 1990. They are identical copies of a letter sent by mail and by facsimile transmission to the respondent Board by a firm of accountants relating to the refusal on the part of the applicants to release client information in a situation where the clients had transferred their work to the firm. The letters deal with the taxation affairs of the clients - the only reason for the letter being written. Document 19 is a further letter from the firm addressed to the respondent in effect staying the earlier request. In my view document 19 is bland in its contents. Standing alone, it says nothing regarding complaints as to the conduct of the applicants. The complaint is enunciated in documents 17 and 18.

44. I am satisfied that document 19 is irrelevant to the request for access because it is not a complaint as to the conduct of the applicants - if anything, it is a complaint as to the lack of activity of the respondent or relates to the contents of the Board's letter referred to in document 19, but not before this Tribunal. In either event document 19 is irrelevant to the claim made.

45. As to documents 17 and 18, I am satisfied, and so find, that those documents contain information obtained by the respondent about the taxation affairs of two taxpayers and therefore fall within the exemption contained in section 38 of the Act because section 16(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 applies to prohibit the disclosure of the information in those documents which are therefore exempt documents (FCT v Swiss Aluminium Ltd No 2 (1986) 66 ALR 159).

46. I turn now to documents 20 to 31. These documents relate to a complaint to the respondent Board by a client of the applicant's. The letter of complaint had attached to it a copy of an earlier letter of complaint to the applicants, a copy of a notice of assessment issued to the complainant by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation at the address for service of a different firm, I presume, of tax agents, a copy of a letter by Steve Hart and Associates Pty Ltd and copy of a further letter of complaint addressed to the applicants. Typed transcripts of handwritten letters are included in the documents.

47. Document 28 is a copy of a letter addressed to the complainant by the respondent marked "strictly private and confidential" referring to the complaint and indicating that the Board may wish to forward a copy of the letter of complaint and the materials which accompanied the letter to the applicants. The letter sought consent for that proposed action by the Board. Document 28 also referred to a part of the letter of complaint which, the letter says, "may be considered defamatory. Consequently you may wish to change the part before a copy is sent to the tax agent. If you wish to do so, kindly forward an amended letter to me." That caused the applicant to write a further letter (Document 31) which enclosed a revised page 4 and requested that it be substituted for the existing page 4 which should be destroyed. Clearly the original page 4 was not destroyed because it still forms part of document 28. Just as clearly the complainant was giving implied (but not explicit) consent to document 28 being sent to the complainant. Certainly no objection was taken to the proposed course of action and it is unlikely, in my view, that the complainant would have revised page 4 if he did not want the respondent Board to take its proposed action. However, the Board apparently took no further action on the complaint because the consent sought was not given by the complainant, i.e. the Board was, it seems, seeking an express consent before it referred document 20 to the applicants.

48. There is no material before the Tribunal which suggests that document 20 with its attachments is on its face a confidential document nor that it was given to the Board in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence. No such suggestion appears in the affidavit of Mr Reinecker nor is the complainant one of those contacted by Mr Couchman.

49. I am not satisfied therefore that document 20, its attachments (documents 22 to 27) have the quality of confidentiality, nor were they given in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence. The Board's practice of disclosing such complaints to the tax agent required the Board to make sure it had the complainant's permission to disclose the complaint. Although it seems to have proceeded on the basis that no such permission was given, the Board misunderstood the import of the complainants letter which is document 29. That document carries with it a necessary implication that the complainant was consenting to disclosure. It follows, in my view, that of the documents 20-31 it is only the Board's letter of 8 May 1990 (document 28) which can be said to be a confidential document but it cannot be said to be a document the disclosure of which would found an action for breach of confidence by a person other than the Commonwealth.

50. As to the claim for exemption under section 41 in relation to documents 20 - 24 and 26 - 28, I am satisfied that the documents each contain information which is personal information as defined in subsection 4(1) of the Act. For this reason I am satisfied those documents are exempt documents within the terms of subsection 41(1) because disclosure under the Act would involve an unreasonable disclosure of personal information about the complainant. I have come to this conclusion because I am satisfied that, given the Board has apparently taken no action on the complaint made in 1990, it would now be unreasonable to disclose the personal information including the complainant's identity to the applicants 4 1/2 years after the complaint was made. I would have come to the same conclusion if the time involved were 3 1/2 years i.e. the time between the making of the complaint and the request for access made in August 1993. Document 25 is not relevant to the claim. Documents 29 and 31 are incorporated by reference in document 20 and document 30 is a typed transcript of document 28. The claim for exemption must also apply to these documents.

51. Documents 32 to 50 relate to complainants who firstly made an oral complaint which is recorded on an Australian Taxation Office minute paper. That minute (document 32) contains significant details about the income tax affairs of the complainants and other related matters.

52. Document 39 is a letter addressed to the respondent Board by the complainants dated 27 April 1990 which reads in part as follows:

"Re your letter 26 March 1990

We agree to have our complaint against Steve Hart and Assoc. dealt

with in the manner suggested."

Document 40 is referred to in the letter but I am satisfied that the respondent's letter of 26 March 1990 is not before me. I cannot proceed any further with the claims for exemption in relation to these complainants until that document is filed in the Tribunal. It is, presumably, a document which has been overlooked or is not the subject of any claim for exemption. I will direct that a copy of the document be filed in the Tribunal within fourteen days. If appropriate, the document can be filed on a confidential basis. I will make further directions after the documents is filed.

53. I turn now to documents 51 and 52. Document 51 is a copy of a written complaint dated 27 April 1990 made to the Tax Agents' Liaison Centre, which I understand to be part of the Australian Taxation Office. Attached to that letter is a copy of a letter to the complainant from a company which the material before me suggests is associated with the applicants. Also, attached is a copy of a document which seems to be in the nature of an acknowledgment of certain matters by the complainant. There is nothing in these documents to suggest that they were given or received in confidence. The complainant sought an investigation of the allegations and a response but there is nothing before me to suggest that either occurred. The documents do not appear to deal with the taxation affairs of the complainant or anyone else nor could they be said to be in any way relevant to or have any adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct of the respondent's operations.

54. There does not seem to be any issue of personal privacy to be considered but it will be necessary to cause notice to be given to the complainant in accordance with section 27A before the claim for exemption under section 41 can be determined.

55. Documents 53-59 relate to a complaint about fees charged by the applicants for preparation of income tax returns over several years. There is no indication as to whom the letter of complaint was addressed but Exhibit 3 asserts it was addressed to the respondent whereas Exhibit 1 asserts it was received by the respondent on 30 March 1990. Document 54 is a further letter of complaint dated 17 May 1990 given in response to a letter from the respondent. The respondent's letter is not before the Tribunal and I can go no further with a consideration of the claim for exemption in relation to these documents until I see a copy of the respondent's letter. It may well be that personal privacy issues will also arise but I cannot decide that issue at present because I do not know the basis upon which the complainant proceeded with her complaint. As a matter of precaution notice under section 27A is to be given to the complainant.

56. Document 60 is a letter of complaint addressed to the respondent by a firm of accountants alleging that its clients were being solicited by the applicants. There is only general allegations in the letter with no details of the affairs of individuals. The respondent claims exemption under paragraphs 37(1)(b) and 40(1)(d) and subsection 45(1). There is, however, nothing in the document to suggest that the letter was given to the respondent in confidence. This is one of the complaints, the subject of Mr Couchman's affidavit. I have already explained my difficulty with that affidavit. The document does not assert in any way that it was given in confidence, rather it asserts a desire for the respondent to take action in relation to the allegations. If this were not the case there would be no apparent purpose for directing the letter to the respondent. Release of the document cannot be expected to have any adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct of the respondent s operations. The reverse is in fact the case because the public interest requires the Board to investigate the allegations and this will include disclosure to the applicants.

57. There are no other documents before me relevant to document 60. I am satisfied there is no basis for a claim for exemption in respect to document 60.

58. Documents 61 and 62 are documents addressed to the Australian Taxation Office regarding a particular and specific complaint about the taxation affairs of an individual. Because the documents deal specifically with the taxation affairs of a taxpayer and would be rendered meaningless if those details were deleted from the documents I am satisfied that section 38 applies to exempt these documents. I am satisfied that subsection 16(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 applies to these documents so as to prohibit divulging information in these documents.

59. The Tribunal is not in a position to make a decision in relation to documents 13, 14, 51, 52, 53-59 until notice has been given to the complainants in accordance with section 27A of the Act. I will direct the respondent to do so. Notice is also to be given of these pending proceedings and the terms of section 59A of the Act and these reasons.

60. In relation to documents 32-50 I will direct that the respondent file a copy of its letter of 26 March 1990.

61. In relation to document 54 I will direct that the respondent file a copy of its letter referred to in that document.

62. I will also direct that these actions are to be taken within fourteen days of the date of these reasons.
