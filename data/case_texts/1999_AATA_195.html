REASONS FOR DECISION



25 March 1999 J Block, Senior Member



1. The objection decision in this matter is the disallowance by the Respondent of an objection dated 11 October 1993 against an assessment numbered 345020/14 issued on 9 August 1993 in respect of the year ended 30 June 1991 ("Year of Income").

2. Mr John Balazs of Baker & McKenzie, solicitors, instructed by Simmons Johnson, accountants, appeared for the Applicant. The Respondent was represented by Mr Roger Gormly, an officer of the Respondent. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act together with a number of exhibits as follows:

  • Exhibits A1 to A10 (inclusive) are the tax returns and financial statements of the Applicant for the Year of Income and the four preceding years;
  • Exhibit A11 is the Guarantee referred to in clause 21 of the Facts;
  • Exhibit A12 is a summons by Ford Credit against six defendants and including the Applicant as sixth defendant.

3. Mr Balazs, with the consent of Mr Gormly, handed up a statement of three pages, entitled "Facts". That statement reproduces (with two additional sentences) the facts which form part of the Applicant's Statement of Facts and Contentions. Mr Gormly confirmed that the Facts handed up (and which I refer to henceforth as the "Facts") were agreed between the parties. There was no oral evidence and the matter was argued on the Facts.

4. (a) The Facts are as follows:

"FACTS

The relevant facts are:

  1. Bell & Moir Corporation Pty Limited (the "Company") has at all material times carried on business as a dealer in new and used motor vehicles and motor vehicle spare parts, as a retailer of motor vehicle tyres and as an insurance agent for FAI Insurance.
  2. The Company also acts as a holding company for two operating subsidiaries, B&M Leasing Pty Limited ("Leasing") and Gorey Holdings Pty Limited ("Gorey") involved, respectively, in motor vehicle leasing and car rentals as an Avis franchisee.
  3. The Company's principal place of business is in Mt Isa in Queensland.
  4. Since just after the Second World War the Company has held a franchise from Goodyear Tyre and Rubber Company ("Goodyear") and since 1979 the Company has also held a franchise from Toyota Australia Limited ("Toyota").
  5. In early 1987 the directors of the Company were approached by Barry Harrod and Michael Kelly, the principals of Barry Michael Motors Pty Limited ("BMM"), who were seeking some much needed capital to help BMM with financial problems that it was experiencing.
  6. At that time and at all relevant times, BMM carried on business as a motor dealer and held franchises from Ford Motor Company of Australia Limited ("Ford") as well as Toyota.
  7. BMM's principal place of business was in Bowen in Queensland.
  8. At the time they were approached to assist BMM with its financial problems, the directors of the Company were aware of strong rumours that Comalco were considering further investment in the Bowen region and this was confirmed by some local sources.
  9. At the time there was a Goodyear dealership in Bowen which was independent of BMM and the Company.
  10. In relation to the financing of motor vehicles, the Company had access to floor plan finance from Toyota Finance Australia Limited ("Toyota Finance") whereas BMM had access to such financing from both Toyota Finance and Ford Credit Australia Limited ("Ford Credit").
  11. In deciding whether to take a minority stake - 33.33% - in BMM, the directors of the Company considered the investment to be primarily one through which the Company could improve its own profitability by way of a relationship with BMM that would enable it to increase revenues through:
    • The sale of Toyota spare parts to BMM. The Company benefited from volume discounts which BMM did not. By entering into the relationship, the Company could provide BMM with Toyota spare parts at prices that were lower than those at which BMM was then paying for the spare parts.
    • The sale of tyres. BMM would effectively become an outlet for tyres that would be supplied by the Company rather than the local Goodyear franchisee.
  • The sale of used motor vehicles to the public. The companies were able to swap vehicles (a common practice between new car dealers), effectively increasing the size of their stock without having to acquire such stock directly.
  • The sale of utility bodies to BMM.
  • Other sources of finance. The possibility of accessing floor plan finance from Ford Credit was seen as offering some possible benefits.
  • Access to Ford brand motor vehicles which were needed to help expand the leasing business of Leasing. By offering Ford as well as Toyota motor vehicles, the opportunity to secure leasing deals increased markedly with corporate customers whose requirements were not brand driven.
  • Increased commission income from FAI Insurance.
    1. After considering the likely benefits of developing a relationship with BMM and undertaking a review of the accounts of BMM, the directors of the Company agreed to make an investment in BMM.
    2. The investment was made by way of the issue and allotment of 110,000 fully paid $1.00 shares at par. These shares were issued in February 1997.
    3. Following on from the acquisition, the Company became involved in the management of BMM's day to day activities and seconded to BMM one of its directors, John Kostowski, partially at its own cost, to enable BMM to improve its performance and to enable the Company to maximise opportunities for it and Leasing.
    4. The accounts of the Company reveal that over the period from 1 July 1987 through to 30 June 1991 the revenues of the Company and Leasing grew. Some part of this growth in the revenues of the Company and of Leasing was attributable to the various intercompany transactions referred to at point 11 above.
    5. Late in 1988, one of BMM's directors and shareholders, Barry Harrod, retired and to accommodate this change it became necessary for BMM to renegotiate its arrangement with Ford Credit. As part of the renegotiation of the arrangement with Ford Credit, the Company became a joint and several guarantor of the floor plan.
    6. The Company agreed to act as a co-guarantor because of its wish to continue with its relationship with BMM. It should be remembered that the Company's investment was only $110,000 and the entering into of the guarantee was not to protect the investment but to ensure continuity of the dealings between BMM and the Company that enabled the Company to earn income.
    7. The guarantee with Ford Credit was entered into on 20 December 1988.
    8. In 1989 BMM changed its banking arrangements and transferred its accounts to the Company's bank and branch, the Westpac Banking Corporation Limited ("Westpac") at Mt Isa. This was done to allow the Company to have some control over the cash flow of BMM because the Company was concerned about the trading position of BMM.
    9. As part of the new banking arrangements, Westpac sought a guarantee from the directors of BMM.
    10. Jim Young was at the time the Company's representative on the board of BMM and it was agreed that he would enter into the guarantee as the Company's representative. Jim Young signed the guarantee in his own name on 25 September 1989, but did so on the basis that he was the representative of the Company, as noted in the minutes of the Board meeting of the Company held on the morning of 25 September 1989.
    11. In August 1990, Westpac, at the request of the directors of the Company, decided not to honour any further cheques drawn by BMM and called on the Company to pay to it the outstanding balance of BMM's account with Westpac, pursuant to which request the Company paid to Westpac on 8 January 1991 the sum of $180,231.56.
    12. On 15 August 1990 Ford Credit also withdrew its financial support from BMM and in accordance with its rights under the floor plan sold the motor vehicles owned by it and sought from the guarantors payment of the loss that it incurred.
    13. On 3 December 1990 the Company paid Ford Credit the sum of $282,881.20.
    14. On 30 April 1991 the Company paid Ford Credit's solicitors a further $7,200.00.
    15. The Company then took action to recover money from Michael Kelly and this led to the bankruptcy of Michael Kelly and the Company received no money from Michael Kelly.
    16. BMM went into liquidation around October 1990 and at the time it was clear that the Company would recover nothing from BMM in relation to the payments made to Ford Credit and Westpac.
    17. The total amount paid pursuant to the guarantees by the Company was $470,313 and together with $2,455 in legal fees paid to its lawyers, the total amount claimed by the Company as allowable deductions in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1991 (the "Year of Income") was $472,768."

(b) The abbreviations contained in the Facts will, when used in these Reasons, have the same meaning; the terms "Company" and "Applicant" are thus interchangeable.



(c) Mr Gormly advised the Tribunal at the outset of the hearing that the Respondent accepts that:



(1) the payments referred to in clauses 22, 24, 25 and 28 of the Facts aggregating in all $472,768 can properly be categorised as "outgoings" rather than "outlays"; and



(2) the payments referred to in subclause (1) constitute expenses which satisfy the nexus tests in section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("Tax Act"); and



(3) accordingly the only issue before the Tribunal is as to whether the Applicant is to be denied a deduction under section 51(1) of the Tax Act because the payments are of a capital nature.



I note in this context, that on reflection, I am not at all sure that nexus for the purpose of section 51(1) is as clear as the Respondent apparently believed. However, that concession having been made the matter was argued on that basis, and the Tribunal accepts that in the circumstances it must be concerned only with the negative limb of section 51(1).



5. In amplification of the Facts, and in accordance with statements made by Mr Balazs (and which were not disputed by Mr Gormly):

(a) The Applicant is based in Mt Isa in East Queensland whereas BMM is based in Bowen, a considerable distance to the west; the time involved in driving between the two would be approximately 12 hours.



(b) From the perspective of the Applicant, the relationship between the Applicant and BMM, (as described in the Facts), resulted for the Applicant in an increase in revenue and profits.



(c) Exhibits A1 to A10 indicate that the profits and revenues of the Applicant did increase during the period covered by them; however, there was no evidence before the Tribunal as to the extent to which any such increases arose from business referable to the relationship between the Applicant and BMM.



(d) When the Company acquired 33.33% of the issued shares in BMM (through a subscription) the Company acquired the right to appoint one member of BMM's board; that appointee was originally Mr J.Singh (the Applicant's managing director) and he was later succeeded, on his retirement, both as managing director of the Company, and as an appointed member of BMM's board of directors by Mr J.Young. Mr Kostowski (referred to in clause 14 of the Facts) moved to Bowen to be involved in BMM's business, and his remuneration was paid partly by the Company and partly by BMM; he did not become a member of BMM's board of directors.



(e) So far as Mr Balazs was aware, no shareholders agreement was entered into in consequence of the acquisition by the Applicant of the shares referred to in clause 11 and 13 of the Facts.



(f) The guarantee in favour of Ford Credit (referred to in clause 16 of the Facts) was executed some 20 months after the share acquisition.



(g) The Guarantee in favour of Westpac (referred to in clauses 19 and 20 of the Facts) was executed in 1989, some 9 months later, as part of a change in banking arrangements in respect of BMM. Under those new arrangements BMM's banking account was moved to Westpac in Mt Isa, where the Applicant was based; this was done in order to confer a degree of control over the account on the Company. The precise degree of control so conferred was not clear, having regard to the fact that deposits continued, naturally enough, to be made in Bowen, and where cheques were also necessarily issued. But the Applicant would have been aware of daily balances and, as appears from clause 22 of the Facts, the Westpac facility was terminated at the instance of the Applicant. Ford Credit withdrew its support (as appears from clause 23 of the Facts) in the same month.



(h) There was no evidence before the Tribunal of any documentation referable to the Ford Credit floor plan or the Westpac bank facility (each a "facility" and collectively the "facilities") or as to the relevant limit in respect of either of them, or as to security given in respect of either of them, although it was believed that Ford Credit obtained a charge. The guarantee which is Exhibit A11 is an all-moneys guarantee and not limited as to amount. The Ford Credit guarantee was not before the Tribunal; the Tribunal infers that it may have been executed on similar terms.



(i) After BMM went into liquidation, the Applicant attempted to recover contributions from its co-guarantors but without success. Mr Kelly, a co-guarantor was made bankrupt; Mr Harrod, another co-guarantor, contested the claim against him, and the Company did not think it worthwhile to pursue it. The Tribunal accepts that any rights of subrogation or contribution were, in fact, worthless.



6. Mr Balazs argued, in particular, that the subrogation rights which were acquired in accordance with the guarantees in favour of Ford Credit and Westpac (collectively "the Guarantees" and each a "Guarantee") were an incident of them and obtained in consequence of them, but not for the purpose of acquiring them. He contended moreover that it was in point that the Applicant first acquired the shares in BMM (and its shareholding was a minority interest) and thereafter entered into the guarantees not, so he contended, to support or prop up a subsidiary, but so as to access markets and to increase its sales and profits.

7. Mr Gormly contended that Mr Balazs was not drawing the proper distinction between "nexus" and "nature", or put in other words, between the "tree" and the "fruit". Mr Gormly contended that, in effect, each of the facilities was structural in nature, in that in each case BMM needed the relevant facility in order to operate its business, in the same way that it needed premises on or within which to place its cars. He drew an analogy between each facility and a loan of money; when a borrower obtains a loan of money, the interest may be deductible, but repayment of the loan is on capital account. The Westpac facility would, on this basis, be a loan or financing facility, notwithstanding the fact that the principal amount owing under it could fluctuate; much the same analogy applies to the Ford Credit floor plan.



8. The parties referred, to a large extent, to the same cases, even though at times they drew different inferences as to their import. The Tribunal thus proposes to deal with the relevant case law, in its own preferred order. The Tribunal does not consider it necessary to deal with all of the cases cited or referred to by the parties.

9. The starting point for a consideration of the issues is the High Court decision in Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337. In that case, and in very broad terms, the appellants paid a substantial sum to a rival publisher to prevent the publication of a rival newspaper. The following quotations from that decision are helpful:

(a) Latham J at p355/6:

"It is true that the payments did not result in obtaining a new capital asset of a material nature, but they did obtain a very real benefit or advantage for the companies, namely, the exclusion of what might have been serious competition. When the words "permanent" or "enduring" are used in this connection it is not meant that the advantage which will be obtained will last forever. The distinction which is drawn is that between more or less recurrent expenses involved in running a business and an expenditure for the benefit of the business as a whole: See per Rowlatt J. in Anglo-Persian Oil Co Ltd v Dale (6), where consideration is given to the significance of the word "enduring" in this connection. The effect of the payment was to enlarge the goodwill of the enterprise, which was one of its most valuable assets. There is no doubt that the goodwill of the Sun newspaper became worth very much more as the result of the agreement which prevented the publication of a competitive newspaper within the same area, possibly at a lower price, by persons who had the control of a press and the necessary plant, together with a newspaper organisation in being."



(b) Dixon J (as he then was) at p362/3:

"But the idea of recurrence and the idea of endurance or continuance over a duration of time both depend on degree and comparison. As to the first it has been said it is not a question of recurring every year or every accounting period; but "the real test is between expenditure which is made to meet a continuous demand, as opposed to an expenditure which is made once for all" (per Rowlatt J., Ounsworth v Vickers Ltd (2)). By this I understand that the expenditure is to be considered of a revenue nature if its purpose brings it within the very wide class of things which in the aggregate form the constant demand which must be answered out of the returns of a trade or its circulating capital and that actual recurrence of the specific thing need not take place or be expected as likely. Thus in Anglo-Persian Oil Co. Ltd. v Dale (3) the establishment and reorganisation of agencies formed part of the class of things making the continuous or constant demand for expenditure, but the given transaction was of a magnitude and precise description unlikely again to be encountered. Recurrence is not a test, it is no more than a consideration the weight of which depends upon the nature of the expenditure.

Again, the lasting character of the advantage is not necessarily a determining factor."



(c) Dixon J at page 363 (referred to by Hill J in the SmithKline case as the "classic statement"):

"There are, I think three matters to be considered, (a) the character of the advantage sought, and in this its lasting qualities may play a part, (b) the manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under the former head recurrence may play its part, and (c) the means adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing a periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment or by making a final provision or payment so as to secure future use or enjoyment."



Sun Newspapers indicates in clear terms that the fact that the expenditure did not result in the addition of a new capital asset of a material nature did not have the consequence that it was on income account; it establishes also that recurrence, while relevant is not decisive.



10. The decision in Hallstroms Pty Limited v FC of T [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 is now best known for the dissenting judgment of Sir Owen Dixon, which has been cited with approval in later cases. See in this context Dixon J at p646 as follows:

"My own opinions upon the question I have attempted to explain in Sun Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commission of Taxation (1) and I shall not re-state them. I shall treat the passage to which I refer as incorporated in this judgment. Once more, however, I shall endeavour to apply what I conceive to be the principles that determine whether an outgoing is on account of capital or of revenue. As a prefatory remark it may be useful to recall the general consideration that the contrast between the two forms of expenditure corresponds to the distinction between the acquisition of the means of production and the use of them; between establishing or extending a business organisation and carrying on the business; between the implements employed in work and the regular performance of the work in which they are employed; between an enterprise itself and the sustained effort of those engaged in it."



The prefatory remark in the passage quoted is of particular relevance; it is necessary to distinguish expenditure (as between capital and income) in accordance with the distinctions there enumerated; in particular the distinction to be drawn is that between establishing or extending a business and the running of it.



11. In Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v FC of T (1951 - [1952] HCA 75; 1952) 85 CLR 423 Dixon C.J., McTiernan, Fullagar and Kitto JJ made it clear that the fact that no asset was brought into existence could not be relevant; moreover nothing turns on the matter in which the expenses are dealt with in a company's accounts.

12. (a) The Tribunal refers next to the decision of Hill J in SmithKline Beecham Laboratories (Aust) Pty Ltd v FC of T 93 ATC 4629 because it sums up a number of relevant aspects of the law involved; see in this regard his Honour's decision at pages 4633 and 4634:

"It may be recalled that in Hallstroms the taxpayer had expended money on legal fees to oppose the extension of a patent of a competitor. The majority of the Court found the expenditure to be deductible but it is the judgment of Sir Owen Dixon in dissent which has in later cases been referred to with approval. In Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v FC of T [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 9 ATD 423; (1951-1952) 85 CLR 423 the High Court had to consider the deductibility of expenditure on legal fees to oppose the grant of a licence under the Theatres and Public Halls Act 1908-1946 (NSW) which would have had the consequence of permitting a competitor to show motion pictures at Broken Hill in opposition to the taxpayer. Not only was Hallstroms case distinguished, but the view of Dixon J disapproving the decision of Lawrence J in Southern (HM Inspector of Taxes) v Borax Consolidated Limited [1941] 1 KB 111 was specifically affirmed. That affirmation carried with it presumably the disapproval of the judgments of Latham CJ and Williams J, two of the majority in Hallstroms, both of whom relied, at least to some extent upon Southern v Borax Consolidated Limited.

...

The resolution of the question whether an outgoing is of capital or revenue is often greatly assisted by reference to the judgment of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers Limited & Associated Newspapers Ltd v FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 5 ATD 87; (1938) 61 CLR 337. In that case his Honour referred (at ATD 94; CLR 360) to the distinction between income and capital as dependent upon the distinction between the profit yielding subject on the one hand and the process of operating it on the other. Relevant to the distinction, as His Honour points out, is a difference between an outlay which is recurrent, repeated or continual on the one hand, and outlay which is final or made "once and for all" on the other. Expenditure made to bring into existence or procure an asset or advantage of a lasting nature to enure for the benefit of the profit-earning subject would ordinarily be on capital account as distinct from expenditure which falls:

"within the very wide class of things which in the aggregate form the constant demand which must be answered out of the returns of a trade or its circulating capital ..." (at ATD 95; CLR 362)."



(His Honour then referred to the classic statement, which has been quoted previously in these Reasons.)



13. The Tribunal refers also to the judgment in John Fairfax and Sons Pty Limited v FC of T (1958 - [1959] HCA 4; 1959) 101 CLR 30 at 36 where Dixon CJ said:

"It is not in my opinion right to say that because you obtain nothing positive, nothing of an enduring nature, for an expenditure it cannot be an outgoing on account of capital. What Viscount Cave L.C. said in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v. Atherton (2) was this, "But when an expenditure is made, not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, I think that there is very good reason (in the absence of special circumstances leading to an opposite conclusion) for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital" [(1926) A.C., at pp.213, 214]. That is an affirmative proposition. But it is hardly necessary to say that it is a logical fallacy to turn it round and say that an expenditure cannot be attributable to capital unless it is made once and for all and is made with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage of the enduring benefit of a trade. Nor did Viscount Cave L.C. mean to say that no expenditure falling outside his proposition would be of a capital nature."



14. (a) In National Australia Bank Limited v FC of T 97 ATC 5153, a decision of the Full Federal Court, the bank paid $42,000,000 to the Commonwealth for the exclusive right to make subsidised home loans to ADF Personnel for a period of 5 years. It was held (per the headnotes) that:

"1. The $42 million payment was of a revenue nature and was therefore deductible. The advantage sought by the Bank in making the payment was the expansion of its home loan customer base and the earning of income from the loans. The right obtained was not "in the nature of a monopoly" nor was it "comparable to the acquisition of goodwill.

2. The payment was part of the consideration for the "franchise" granted by the Commonwealth and was calculated by reference to the number of home loans the Bank expected to make to ADF personnel. The fact that it was a once-and-for-all payment was suggestive of a capital nature but not conclusive. Several factors in the present case diluted the significance of non-recurrence.

3. The true contrast between a capital and a revenue expense is between altering the framework within which income-producing activities are for the future to be carried on and the taking of a step as part of those activities within the framework. The $42 million payment did not enlarge the framework within which the Bank carried on its activities but rather was incurred as part of the process by which the Bank operated to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay - the difference between the outlay and the returns representing profit or loss. The payment was in the nature of a marketing expense which pointed to it having a revenue rather than a capital aspect. The present case was not unlike the BP Australia case."



(b) Mr Gormly agreed that the National Australia Bank case was correctly decided. The bank was in the business of making loans; its payment of $42,000,000 did not enlarge the relevant framework, and accordingly the payment, albeit large and non-recurrent, was held to be deductible on income account.



15. McLennan v FC of T 90 ATC 4047 was also a decision of the Full Federal Court. Hill J said at page 4054:

"The same idea was expressed by Dixon J in Hallstroms Pty Ltd v FC of T [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at p.647 where his Honour referred to:

"the general consideration that the contrast between the two forms of expenditure corresponds to the distinction between the acquisition of the means of production and the use of them; between establishing or extending a business organisation and carrying on the business; between the implements employed in work and the regular performance of the work in which they are employed; between an enterprise itself and the sustained effort of those engaged in it."

The present is not a case where the appellant expended money to acquire some new income-producing asset. The shares in the co-operative were not acquired for the purpose of thereafter deriving any dividend or for that matter liquidation distribution that might be received from them. Nor can it be said that the applicant acquired a weir as a result of the expenditure; that weir was always intended to be and did remain the property of the Queensland Government. The advantage from a practical and business point of view which the levy was calculated to effect was the acceleration of the building of the second weir, providing no doubt a greater degree of water storage upon which the appellant's business and the businesses of all other suppliers to the Invicta Mill depended. While the fact that the payment of the levy might add value to the lands of owners served by the storage area is relevant, that in itself, while pointing in the direction of capital, will not be determinative. For example, councils are permitted by the Local Government Act 1919 (NSW) sec. 120 to levy special rates upon particular landowners. Those rates may be used for specified purposes such as particular road projects and the like. The fact that a landowner whose land is used for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income is levied a special rate for some road works which could add value to his property, will not require the conclusion that such a special rate is an outgoing of a capital nature."



It is important to note in respect of McLennan, that what the taxpayer obtained, in effect, was a supply of water. The taxpayer did not acquire the shares in the co-operative for any income derivation purpose; nor did the taxpayer acquire a weir in consequence of the expenditure.



16. FC of T v E.A.Marr & Sons (Sales) Ltd 84 ATC 4580 was also a decision of the Full Federal Court; the part of the headnote to that decision which contains the findings will suffice; those findings read as follows:

"1. The activities of the taxpayer in leasing plant from finance companies and making available to subsidiaries were part of the business of the taxpayer. This part of the business was carried on for the purpose of producing assessable income of the taxpayer. Although the subsidiaries did not pay rental to the taxpayer, they did relet the leased plant to generate profits or used it in their own businesses. The taxpayer could direct profits from its subsidiaries to itself, for example by having the subsidiaries declare dividends.

2. The payments were made in discharge of the liabilities incurred by the taxpayer in the course of carrying on its business. Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 distinguished. The taxpayer was probably not carrying on business when the payments were made, but it did not necessarily follow from this that the payments were not on revenue account. Nor was it conclusive against the taxpayer that the payments were probably made by the receivers in their capacity as liquidators. The payments were made when the taxpayer was solvent, the insecured creditors were fully paid out, and some assets and staff were retained."



The payments in question in E.A.Marr were on income account because the liabilities were incurred as part of the ongoing business.



17. I refer next to the decision in Hooker Rex Pty Limited v FC of T 88 ATC 4392. The taxpayer owned subsidiaries which in turn owned land. The taxpayer furnished guarantees in order to procure the liquidation of those subsidiaries, and so the land was awarded to it (the taxpayer as distributions in specie) and became part of its trading stock. The costs referable to those guarantees were deductible because of their connection with the acquisition of trading stock. The taxpayer also had other subsidiaries which were, in relation to their own subsidiaries, doing much the same thing. In respect of the latter subsidiaries, the payments under the guarantees were held to have been on capital account; there was no link, in relation to the taxpayer itself, to trading stock. The Court (Sweeney and Gummow JJ) said:

(a) at page 4402 in respect of the allowable deductions:

"We deal first with the deductibility of what the Commissioner in his written submissions described as the "Bowden & Schadel amounts". The Commissioner contended that the losses or outgoings of the taxpayer, flowing as they did from the guarantee or indemnity in respect of the tax liabilities of the Bowden & Schadel subsidiaries, were too remote from the income-earning activity of the taxpayer and, in any event, were of a capital nature. The submission was that the undertaking was given by the taxpayer, not for the purchase of the land held by the four subsidiary companies, but for their liquidation and the subsequent transfer of their land in specie to their parent, Bowden & Schadel.

In truth, there was an extra step. The effect of the resolution of the directors of the taxpayer on 23 July 1971 was to provide for the liquidation of Bowden & Schadel itself and the distribution in specie to the taxpayer of the shares in the four subsidiaries; the subsidiaries would be placed in liquidation and the land of the subsidiaries would then be transferred in specie to the taxpayer for subdivision, development and sale by the taxpayer. As we have earlier explained in these reasons, approval of these steps was given to the liquidator by the Deputy Commissioner; on or about 30 August 1972 the land held by each of the Bowden & Schadel subsidiaries was transferred to Bowden & Schadel, and thereafter from that company to the taxpayer. The provision of the undertaking by the taxpayer to the Deputy Commissioner on 28 August 1972 was sequential to the liquidator having informed the Deputy Commissioner on 31 July of the proposal to make an in specie distribution of the land of the four subsidiaries to the holding company, Bowden & Schadel, and then a further in specie distribution to the taxpayer so that the taxpayer might sell the land for home building as soon as possible. We have previously described the significant impact of sec. 215 of the Act upon these proposals. From the outset, it was plain that the implementation of the resolution of 23 July 1971 would require approval by the Commissioner under that provision.

In those circumstances, it is, in our opinion, plain that the undertaking was given to the Deputy Commissioner so that he would give his consent under sec. 215 of the Act to the series of in specie distributions which would provide the taxpayer with the title to the land in question to be used by it in its trading activities. The advantage for which the losses or outgoings were incurred was the acquisition of trading stock by the taxpayer: cf. Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542 at p.4548; [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183 at p.191. The legal title was acquired by the means provided by company law and the law of real property for the voluntary liquidation of companies and the transmission of legal title to land. However, sec. 215 of the Act rendered illegal the in specie distributions without the leave of the Commissioner. The price paid for that leave in respect of the series of in specie distributions was the provision by the taxpayer of the guarantee or indemnity."



(b) at pages 4403 and 4404 and in respect of the deductions which were not allowable:

"The evidence, given for the taxpayer, was that Marketing & Sales was "selected" as the purchaser of the shares in Shangri-la because Marketing & Sales had past year losses available "for income tax purposes". The nature of the advantage or benefit sought to be obtained from the giving of the guarantee or indemnity in respect of the liquidation of Shangri-la and the distribution in specie to Marketing & Sales was the acquisition by Marketing and Sales of trading stock, by the removal of the impediment otherwise presented by sec. 215 of the Act to the distribution by the liquidator of Shangri-la to Marketing & Sales. The giving of the guarantee thus was a step in a process which led to an increase in the asset backing of, and, other things being equal, the value of, the shares held by the taxpayer in Shangri-la. Accordingly, the outgoing was on capital account. Similarly, the loan by the taxpayer to the subsidiary in FC of T v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd (supra), was presumably of a capital nature: see Parsons, Income Taxation in Australia [§6-245].

We also accept the submission by the Commissioner that the taxpayer's loss or outgoing in respect of the undertaking given to permit the in specie distribution from Shangri-la to Marketing & Sales was not incurred in gaining or producing the taxpayer's assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on a business within the second limb of subsec. 51(1) of the Act. We accept that the loss or outgoing was at best incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income of Marketing & Sales. There was lacking, in our view, what Brennan J. has called the required connection between the loss or outgoing and the gaining or producing of the assessable income of the taxpayer: Ure v FC of T 81 ATC 4100 at pp. 4103-4104; [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 50 FLR 219 at p.223, Magna Allows Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542 at pp. 4545-4551; [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183 at pp. 187-195. Nor could the outgoing be brought within the second limb of subsec. 51(1)."



The Tribunal considers that the decision in Hooker Rex, insofar as deductions were allowed, is distinguishable from the fact situation in this matter, primarily because of the close link between the losses or outgoings which were allowed and the acquisition of trading stock. Hooker Rex in this regard is, in the opinion of the Tribunal, best classified as a case which was concerned with nexus rather than nature or structure.

18. A recent decision of O'Loughlin J in Email Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1999] FCA 128 (24 February 1999) came to my attention shortly before the hearing and I drew the attention of the parties to it. Clauses 52, 53, 54, 58 and 59 of the judgment in the Email case are relevant:

"52. The sale by OCAL was the sale by it of a capital asset. Had the investment in Dowell been owned directly by Email, the sale would also have been of a capital asset. The sale therefore represented the disposal of OCAL and, indirectly by Email, of portion of its profit earning structure. Be that as it may, I am of the opinion that, in the circumstances of this case, the giving of the indemnity by Email had the potential to allow its subsidiary company (OCAL) to achieve a greater capital profit (which would in due course be available for the declaration of a dividend in favour of Email). Thus, payments made as a consequence of that indemnity were payments on account of revenue and therefore deductible under both limbs of subs 51(1) of the Act. Factually, this conclusion can be based on this evidence of Mr Brink who, in par 37 of his affidavit of 16 April 1998 said:

"Throughout my commercial experience, it has been normal commercial practice for the purchaser of an existing business or corporate entity to seek security such as a warranty or guarantee from a responsible commercial entity by way of indemnity against unascertained liabilities of the entity being purchased. In the case of entities being acquired from a substantial corporate group, it was normal practice for purchasers to require security from the holding company of the group. The Email Group, when acquiring new corporate assets, followed this practice itself."

53. In support of his evidence - that the giving of an indemnity of the kind that is presently under consideration was part of the normal business activities of Email - Mr Brink exhibited to his affidavit four Deeds of Sale. Three of them related to the sales by a subsidiary company of businesses and the fourth dealt with the sale of a subsidiary's Plumbing Supply Division. In each case Email provided an indemnity to the purchaser. The earliest of these sales occurred in November 1985, two occurred in late 1986 and the fourth was in October 1989. These Deeds were tendered by counsel for Email to make good the proposition that the giving of an indemnity by Email with respect to the sales of businesses, divisions or (as in this case) shares in controlled entities was part of the ordinary business activities of Email.

54. I accept Mr Brink's evidence as an accurate statement of Email's business practice. I find, in terms of the first and second limb of subs 51(1) that the payments made by Email under the indemnity were incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. It should be noted for completeness that the Commissioner did not seek to argue that a dividend received by Email and sourced from the sale of the shares in Dowell would not have been assessable income.

58. The essential question is what was the advantage that Email was seeking? As to this, the evidence points, in my opinion, in only one direction: Email sought to advantage itself by increasing the likely flow of dividends that it would ultimately receive. The decisions in Total Holdings and Marr are cases in which the holding company sought to advantage itself by improving the profitability of a subsidiary and the size of its ultimate dividends. The indemnity in the present case was given to promote and preserve the size of future dividends from its subsidiary.

59. The evidence has satisfied me that the conduct of Email in giving the indemnity was not an isolated transaction; on the contrary, it accorded with an established practice that Email had adopted as part of its corporate policy. As counsel for the applicant submitted, the element of repetition strengthens the revenue character of the outgoings: Fanmac Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4703. That case is an example of expenditure which, but for the repetitive conduct of the taxpayer, might otherwise have been classified as expenditure of a capital nature. The taxpayer company carried on a business of establishing, marketing and managing mortgage trusts. Since the commencement of its business it had established twenty-two such trusts. The taxpayer claimed a deduction for expenses incurred in the course of establishing a particular trust; those costs included legal fees, printing costs, an underwriter's fee and a rating establishment fee. In holding that the expenses were deductions under both limbs of subs 51(1) Beaumont J said that if the only relevant activity of the taxpayer had been the instant trust:

'... there would be much to be said for the view that this item if expenditure was an affair of capital.' (p.4709)."



In my view the judgment in Email is distinguishable from the facts in this matter precisely because of the recurrent nature of the indemnities which Email gave in respect of its subsidiaries. As his Honour pointed out in his reference to the Fanmac case, it is an example of expenditure which but for the repetitive conduct of the taxpayer, might have been classified as capital. Moreover, in my view an indemnity given upon the sale of a subsidiary has legal characteristics which differ from those of a guarantee given on behalf of either a subsidiary or a company in which the guarantor has an interest. And his Honour was plainly influenced by the decision in FC of T v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd (1979) 24 ALR 401 and the fact that Total had the power to advantage itself through the profitability of a subsidiary "and the size of ultimate dividends". The Applicant in this matter had one third of the shares in BMM and no power to control future dividends.



19. A number of Board and Tribunal cases were cited to me by each of the parties' representatives, but I do not think that it is necessary for me to refer to them.

20. I refer to the decision of the High Court in Cliffs International Inc. v FC of T [1979] HCA 8; (1979) 142 CLR 140, because it is a decision of the High Court, and because it was referred to by both parties. It is not, in some respects, easy to reconcile. No less an authority than Professor R. Parsons in Income Taxation in Australia appears also to have found it difficult; see in particular paragraphs 7.45 and 7.46 of Income Taxation in Australia.

The Tribunal considers, in any event, that the decision in Cliffs International is clearly distinguishable on its facts.



21. Mr Gormly cited aspects of the argument of Mr Anthony Mason KC (as he then was) in Hallstroms. I agree that his argument supports the Respondent; it is, as Mr Gormly argued, and following the decision in SmithKline, clear that the minority decision of Dixon CJ in Hallstroms was correct. I need not refer to that argument further.

22. The leading cases establish that while the element of recurrence may be relevant, it is by no means decisive. They establish also that a payment will not necessarily be on revenue account merely because it does not result in the acquisition of an asset. The Tribunal agrees with Mr Gormly's contention that the fundamental question in this matter relates to structure; if a payment results in a structural acquisition or an enhancement of structure, that payment will be capital in nature. The Tribunal considers that, in general terms, all expenditures made in a business context, and whether on capital or revenue account are intended, either directly or indirectly, to produce or increase income, or to reduce or eliminate costs, and which has the same effect. The acquisition of a building in which a business is to trade does not mean that its cost is on revenue account, because profits will increase through the elimination of rent which, on this hypothesis, might be less than (if relevant) any interest cost referable to a loan procured to purchase the premises. In the same way, a loan obtained from a bank which is expended on trading stock or to pay rent or interest does not have the effect that the borrowed moneys are on revenue account. All of this is, of course, so obvious as to be trite.

23. Where a guarantee is given by a taxpayer as part of its ordinary business (and a good example would be that of a bank which gives guarantees on behalf of its customers to suppliers) payments made thereunder will, of course, be deductible on revenue account. And the decisions in E.A.Marr and Email indicate that a similar result may flow from analogous (and recurrent) conduct. But this said, the example given is far removed from the facts in this matter. The Company gave only two Guarantees on behalf of BMM; in neither case did it receive any fee or consideration for doing so. In each case the relevant facility, per the Facts, was necessary to BMM; while BMM was not a subsidiary of the Company, the Company had a significant stake in BMM; it appointed a member of BMM's board, seconded one of its own directors to work for BMM and apparently had the power to terminate the Westpac facility. Clauses 19 and 20 of the Facts make it clear that the giving of the Guarantee was necessary in order to obtain the Westpac facility. As regards Ford Credit (and see clauses 16 and 17 of the Facts) the position is less clear, but it is reasonable to infer that the giving of the Guarantee by the Applicant was material to or at the very least highly relevant in relation to the reorganisation, and thus the continuation of that facility. The Applicant states that it gave the Guarantee to Ford Credit in order to continue its relationship with BMM, and to ensure continuity of its dealings with BMM. The Ford Credit facility was plainly important (to say the least) to BMM; the very fact that the Applicant executed the Guarantee leads me to infer that the rearrangement and continuation of that facility might not have been possible in the absence of its grant. Mr Balazs argued that I should draw a distinction between the two facilities and so that the payments under one Guarantee might be classified in a manner different from that applicable in respect of the other. Mr Balazs did not, however, give me any reason for drawing a distinction in this manner or make any submission as to which facility should be classified in which category.

See in this context Income Taxation in Australia at paragraph 6.245 where Professor Parsons states:

"Where the giving of the guarantee relates to an investment by another in an entity in which the taxpayer-guarantor is interested, whether the investment is a loan to the entity or the allowing of credit in some transaction with the entity, the character of the guarantee and of a payment under the guarantee will reflect the character that an investment by the taxpayer himself would have had. ... Generally the giving of the guarantee and payment under the guarantee will not be acts in carrying on a business. They are unlikely to be such where the taxpayer's interest in the entity is, for example, the interest of a shareholder in a private company. The making of the payment under the guarantee and the failure of the subrogation rights to realise the amount of the payment may involve a relevant loss, but it is not a working loss."



The passage from Income Taxation in Australia is, of course, directly in point. The Guarantees were closely linked to the facilities; in the case of Westpac the facility was conditional on the relevant Guarantee, and in the case of Ford Credit it was either conditional or at the very least highly relevant. This being so, the character of the Guarantees may well be coloured by the character of the facilities. Assuming that BMM had repaid the principal amounts of the facilities, those payments would clearly have been on capital account. The Applicant was not in the business of giving guarantees nor did it do so recurrently; when it executed the Guarantees it did so in its capacity as a shareholder in BMM, and where the Guarantees were closely related to the facilities. The payments of principal were made by the Applicant because BMM could not do so; those payments would then, in accordance with this line of reasoning, also be on capital account.



24. (a) I agree with Mr Gormly's contention that the Applicant has, in effect, confused nexus and nature. There can be no doubt that when the Applicant entered into the Guarantees, one of its purposes was to procure an increase in business and profits for itself. That was also its purpose when it acquired its 331/3% shareholding in BMM. This is why the nexus requirements of section 51(1) of the Tax Act may have been satisfied. But so said, it ignores the fact that the Guarantees were given on behalf of BMM in order to enable BMM to obtain the facilities without which BMM could not have conducted its business. Each of the facilities were, as I have said previously, just as much a part of BMM's structure as the premises in which it conducted its business. This assumes of course that it is correct to examine the structural aspect from BMM's point of view.



(b) Accepting as I have that the nexus aspect, having been conceded, is satisfied, my decision must relate only to the question of whether the payments were of a capital nature. The facilities were, as I have said, structural in relation to BMM; it could not have continued its business without them. And, of course, and as set out previously the Guarantees were closely linked to the facilities.



(c) The matter can be approached along a number of possible lines:



(1) It may be that because repayment of the facilities by BMM would have been capital in nature, payment by the Applicant under the Guarantees must be characterised in the same way. But Email would tend to suggest that the solution is not quite so simple.



(2) It may be that in a guarantee situation, and particularly where the guarantor is a shareholder in a private company (as to which see the passage from Income Taxation in Australia referred to earlier), the question of structure is to be examined in relation to the principal debtor, and if structural in relation to the principal debtor the same characterisation flows to the guarantor. As I understood Mr Gormly's argument, the facilities were structural for BMM, and thus also for the Applicant as guarantor. But this too may be an over-simplification.



(3) The share subscription by the Applicant was clearly of a capital nature notwithstanding that it was made in order to establish a relationship designed to bring about the business relationship referred to in the Facts. There was no evidence before me as to the size of the Applicant's investment in BMM in relation to its whole structure; it would seem that it was not insignificant. Looked at from the point of view of the Applicant it was part of its own structure designed, in the manner set out in the Facts, to increase its profits. Had the Applicant borrowed the cost of the shares it might have been entitled to a deduction of the interest cost incurred, although Total Holdings might suggest that a deduction of this nature is available only where the flow of dividends can be controlled. But a loss of the amount subscribed for the shares would be on capital account. Thereafter, and when each Guarantee was executed, the correct characterisation would on this view be that it (and any loss sustained in consequence of it) is similarly capital.



Clause 17 of the Facts states that "the Company's investment was only $110,000 and the entering into of the guarantee was not to protect the investment but to ensure continuity ..." It is in this regard that, to use Mr Gormly's words, the Applicant confuses nexus with nature. Having invested $110,000 for the purpose of increasing its profits, the Applicant found that it was necessary for precisely the same purpose to increase its stake in BMM. Instead of subscribing further capital or furnishing loan capital to BMM, the Applicant guaranteed the Ford Credit facility and the same analysis applies, of course, in much the same way to the Westpac facility. Viewed in this manner the Guarantees were just as much structural to the Applicant as was the shareholding in BMM, and indeed the three transactions (the share subscription and the Guarantees) can properly be treated as linked.



(4) It is my view that the correct approach is that set out in subclause (3), namely that the shareholding in BMM and the Guarantees constituted a stake in BMM which, looked at from the viewpoint of the Applicant, was structural in nature; that stake was part of its overall structure, and would, it was hoped, produce income as contemplated by the Facts. (It is relevant, albeit not decisive, that it was not in any way recurrent; there was no evidence of any other similar venture.) This being so the losses under the Guarantees were losses of a capital nature. It is also relevant in the opinion of the Tribunal that the Applicant did not seek a deduction for its loss of the amount of $110,000 subscribed by way of share capital. This, despite each of the share subscription and the giving of each Guarantee being entered into for the same reason, that being the hope that it would result in an increase in the Applicant's turnover and income through its association with and support of BMM. There was, put in other words, no difference in objective in respect of any of the three constituent parts or steps.



25. As Professor Parsons states in paragraph 7.39 of Income Taxation in Australia: "A conclusion that an asset is structural and the cost of it not deductible must always depend on a judgment of the significance of the asset to the process of income derivation". And in paragraph 7.31: "The word 'assets' is not intended to have a meaning that will import the notices of proprietary rights which belong to the law of property. A more appropriate term - indeed the term used by Dixon J in the classical formulation of principle in Sun Newspapers is 'advantage'". BMM in respect of the facilities obtained advantages which were significant. Similarly, the Applicant acquired the shares, and thereafter executed the Guarantees, in order to obtain an advantage, that being the business relationship and anticipated profits contemplated by the Facts. That the investment was unsuccessful in the result is not to the point.

26. In the circumstances, the objection decision under review is affirmed.
