Re Wilfred Gelsthorpe and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 322 (4 November 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: WILFRED GELSTHORPE

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S87/163

AAT No. 4724

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.A. Layton(1) (Deputy President), J.A. Kiosoglous(1) (Senior Member)

and B.C. Lock(2) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - issue of Benefit Classification Certificate - degenerative disc disease of lower lumbar spine - meaning of "real risk" - whether "real risk" of premature retirement - factors: degree of risk, characteristics of applicant.

Words & Phrases: "real risk", "not likely"

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.3(1), 16(4), 16(8), 154(4)

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986, (No. 80 of 1986) ss.4(1), 5, 6(1)

HEARING

ADELAIDE

4:11:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor a decision is made that the Benefit Classification Certificate issued in respect of the applicant be revoked.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision made by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 29 July 1986 refusing to revoke a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") issued in respect of the applicant pursuant to para. 16(8)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), which decision was confirmed by a delegate on 29 June 1987 pursuant to sub-s.154(4) of the Act.

2. The applicant became an "eligible employee" as defined in sub-s.3(1) of the Act on 5 September 1985 when he was appointed as a permanent employee. He was therefore required to undergo a medical examination in accordance with sub-s.16(2) of the Act, to determine his fitness for employment and in particular whether or not a BCC should be issued. A BCC was issued pursuant to sub-s.16(4) of the Act specifying the applicant's physical condition to be "Injury to Lumbo-Sacral Intervertebral Disc" (T7/32).

3. On 7 January 1986, the applicant appealed against the issue of the BCC, which appeal was treated by the Commissioner as a request to revoke the BCC pursuant to sub-s.16(6) of the Act (T8/33). In response to this request the applicant was required to undergo further medical examinations with Mr D.S. Dhillon, a general surgeon, Mr G.T. Davies, a radiologist, on 3 April 1986 (T9/38-40) and Dr E.J. Lloyd, Acting Assistant Director, Department of Health, on 22 April 1986 (T10/41). On consideration of these medical reports, a delegate refused to revoke the BCC, although the condition specified on the Certificate was changed to read, "Degenerative Disc Disease of the Lower Lumbar Spine". This decision refusing revocation was made pursuant to para. 16(8)(c) of the Act on 29 July 1986, and is the decision reviewable by this Tribunal.

4. On 6 August 1986, the applicant requested this decision be reconsidered by the Commissioner. This request was taken to be made pursuant to sub-s.154(2) of the Act, and pursuant to sub-s.154(4), a delegate of the Commissioner upon reconsideration, confirmed the decision of 29 July 1986.

5. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by counsel, Mr D. Johns and the respondent was represented by an agent, Mr R. Eadie. Apart from the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents"), the applicant gave oral evidence and called Mr Harold Schaeffer, a neuro-surgeon and Professor Smith, a specialist in occupational medicine. The respondent called Mr Peter Byrne, a general surgeon, Mr Dhillon, a general surgeon, and Mr Wallace, a domestic service supervisor.

6. It was agreed by the parties and the Tribunal also accepts, that the legislation applicable to this review is the Superannuation Act 1976 as amended firstly by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (No. 80 of 1986, assented to on 24 June 1986) insofar as sub-s.4(1), s.5 and sub-s.6(1) of the amending Act amended ss.3, 9 and 16 of the Superannuation Act (supra), which amendments are made retrospective to 1 July 1976. Secondly by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No. 2) 1986 (No. 151 of 1986, assented to on 18 December 1986) insofar as the amendments affect the procedural operation of s.16 of the Superannuation Act (supra) (applying the authorities of Re Costello and Secretary, Department of Transport (1979) 2 ALD 934 and Re Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60).

7. The provisions of relevance to this review are sub - s.16(4) as amended which provides:

"The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the

opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or

mental condition or conditions referred to in the

report or reports, will not continue to be an eligible

employee until the person attains his maximum retiring

age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit clas

sification certificate to that effect, being a

certificate in which the relevant condition or

conditions is or are specified."

and sub-s.16(8) which as amended provides:

"The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports

(if any), and such other matters as he considers

relevant, and -

(a) where there is not more than 1 physical or mental

condition specified in the benefit classification

certificate and the Commissioner is of the

opinion that there is not a real risk that the

person, by reason of or for a reason connected

with that condition, will not continue to be an

eligible employee until he attains his maximum

retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the

certificate;

(b) where there is more than 1 physical or mental

condition specified in the benefit classification

certificate -

(i) if the Commissioner is of the opinion

that there is not a real risk that

the person, by reason of or for a

reason connected with those

conditions, will not continue to be

an eligible employee until the person

attains his maximum retiring age -

the Commissioner shall revoke the

certificate; or

(ii) if the Commissioner is of the opinion

that there is a real risk that the

person, by reason of or for a reason

connected with 1 or some only of the

conditions specified in the certificate,

will not continue to be an

eligible employee until he attains

his maximum retiring age - the

Commissioner shall revoke the

certificate and issue in substitution

for the certificate a new benefit

classification certificate specifying

only the condition or conditions in

respect of which the Commissioner is

of the opinion referred to in this

sub-paragraph;

(c) in any other case - the Commissioner shall refuse

the request to revoke the benefit classification

certificate or to revoke the benefit classification

certificate and to issue in substitution for

the certificate a new benefit classification

certificate."

8. The background facts for the purposes of reviewing the delegate's decision not to revoke the BCC are as follows.

9. The applicant was born on 9 June 1935 in Sheffield, England. After leaving school at the age of 15 years, he was employed for two to three years as an apprentice fitter. Before he qualified in that trade, he entered the Royal Air Force as a batman/waiter for two years. Following that service, he began working in heavy industry as a blacksmith forgeman, in hot conditions involving heavy lifting of molten metals and steel. He remained working in England until 1960 when he emigrated to Australia. He gained employment with Australian National Industries in New South Wales again as a blacksmith forgeman, and worked for this company for a period of five years before again returning to England where he resumed work as a forgeman blacksmith for a further five years. He returned to Australia in 1970.

10. After working a further eighteen months as a forgeman for Repco in Victoria, he moved to Adelaide in 1973. Initially he was employed by John Shearer at Kilkenny as a night manager overseeing other forgers and pressworkers, and on occasions he was also required to perform the physical duties involved in presswork, blacksmithing and forging.

11. In 1976, the applicant left Repco due to a downturn in the making of farm machinery and again became a blacksmith forgeman at Perry Engineering. This work was heavy and the applicant stated that at times he was required to carry his own bodyweight of 75 kilograms. Nevertheless he performed overtime three days a week of four to five hours on each day. He was a healthy, strong man and prided himself on his fitness.

12. In 1978, during the course of his duties at Perry Engineering, the applicant injured his back when he was forced to take the weight of a large steel girder which slipped off the edge of a template. Notwithstanding the pain in his back, the applicant completed his shift and continued working for the four following days until the pain became unbearable and he was forced to consult his doctor.

13. The applicant was referred to a specialist and was subsequently hospitalised and placed in traction for a period of three weeks. He was then off work for a total of four months. During this period, the applicant walked and swam "for miles" in order to get "right again".

14. On his return to work at Perry Engineering, the applicant was offered the position of courier/driver for the same remuneration which he was previously receiving as a blacksmith forgeman; he was not offered the opportunity of returning to forge work. This work suited the applicant because the conditions and nature of employment were comparatively easy. He remained in that position for two to three years until he was retrenched in 1982 because of a downturn in the steel industry.

15. The applicant gave evidence that at the time of his return to work at Perry Engineering, he experienced no further problems with his back, and indeed from that time up to the present he has been symptom-free. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence which indeed is supported by his activities outlined hereafter.

16. Following retrenchment, the applicant industriously sought employment not merely in the blacksmith forgeman trade, but over a broad range of employment. He was unsuccessful in his applications largely due to the refusal by employers to engage a person with a history of previous back injury. This was notwithstanding the fact that the applicant no longer suffered any symptoms as a result of that injury.

17. The Commonwealth Employment Service sent the applicant to St Margaret's Rehabilitation Centre for assessment to ascertain his capacity to perform employment. The results of the assessment tests completed on 6 March 1984 are contained in T8/36-37 and may be summarised as follows:

"Throughout his tests GA Wilfred displayed a positive

and enthusiastic approach to all of the activities he

was asked to carry out. He listened to and followed

instructions well and at all times tried to put into

practice the correct techniques of lifting and

carrying.

It should also be noted that Wilfred completed all of

the tests in 1 hour. Under normal circumstances they

would be carried out in 3-5 sessions of 3/4hr duration

each. Wilfred was attentive to the various techniques

involved with all of the activities and this was

evident in his physical performance. A very good

effort on his part."

18. The applicant was also assessed by Professor Smith, a specialist in rehabilitation medicine, and a summary of his assessment is contained in T14/48-49 in the following terms:

"21/2/84

Back Injury in 1978 - Strain only, recovered

completely.

Now no problems at all but cannot get back into the

work force after being retrenched.

No physical problems - fully and completely recovered.

Assessment and then work placement in manufacturing

industry or driving.

"PROF D SMITH"

3/8/84

No complaints.

O/E:

1) L/S - Full range. Normal contour.

2) No tenderness.

3) SLR 70o both

4) Neurology (tick)

Power "

Sensation "

He is fit for manual work.

"PROF. D SMITH"

28-09-84

No problem. A very fit, well motivated man whose back

has fully recovered. He would be an asset to any

employer. I recommend that he be discharged as a

completely successful rehabilitated client. There are

no restrictions on his employment.

(SIGNED) DENNIS S SMITH"

19. Following these assessments, a Special Placements Officer at the Commonwealth Employment Service ("CES") sent the applicant to the Daw Park Repatriation Hospital for employment. The applicant was initially employed on a temporary basis under a seventeen week scheme, subsidised by the CES. When this period of subsidised work ended, the applicant, through sheer persistence and after obtaining numerous extended periods of temporary work, commenced full-time employment at the Daw Park Repatriation Hospital on 18 July 1984. He was examined for the purposes of becoming a permanent employee on 6 August 1985 and the Examining Medical Officer, Dr Stewart, took the view that the applicant was "not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until attaining the relevant maximum retiring age". The reason given by the Medical Officer was "Injury to Lumbo-Sacral Intervertebral Disc" (T6/31).

20. On 5 September 1985, the applicant became permanently employed as an orderly at the Daw Park Repatriation Hospital. On 19 December 1985, the delegate issued a BCC pursuant to sub-section 16(4) of the Act certifying "Injury to lumbo-sacral inter vertebral disc". As previously mentioned, the applicant sought reconsideration of that BCC. On 3 April 1986, x-rays were taken of the applicant's lumbo-sacral spine and the following report was given by Dr G.T. Davies:

"LUMBO-SACRAL SPINE

The normal curve is straightened but there is no

scoliosis. No fracture seen and the pedicles are

normal. There is moderate narrowing of the L3-4, 4-5

and quite marked narrowing of the L5-S1 disc spaces.

There is anterior osteophyte formation at several of

the lumbar disc spaces and there is also some

sclerosis in the facet joints posteriorly at L5-S1.

Bone density generally is normal. The SI joints are

normal.

The appearances are consistent with degenerative disc

disease in the lower lumbar spine."

(T9/40)

These x-rays contrasted with x-rays apparently obtained on 13 December 1978 and the only reference to the contents of those x-rays is a reference providing little detail in a report of Mr Dhillon of 10 April 1986 at T9/39 where he states:

"X-rays taken on 13.12.1978 - show slight narrowing of

L5-S1 disc space."

21. On 10 April 1986, following an examination of the applicant on 3 April 1986, Mr Dhillon concluded that:

"Opinion

In 1978 Mr. Gelsthorpe injured his lower back after a

heavy lifting incident at work, and was treated with

traction. He remained off work for 3-4 months. On

the basis of the history related to me and on the

clinical findings at the examination, there is no

doubt that he has completely recovered from that

injury.

However, he has degenerative disc disease in the lower

lumbar spine as evidenced by the recent x-rays taken

on 3.4.1986. There is marked narrowing of L5-S1 disc

space.

I feel that he would be at grave risk of aggravating

this pre-existing degenerative condition if exposed to

heavy lifting duties. The duties of a medical orderly

involve considerable heavy lifting, like lifting

patients, linen bags, pushing wheel chairs and

trolleys, and are considered unsuitable for him.

I am unable to state with confidence that at his

present job he will not agravate (sic) his pre-

existing low back condition and precipitate the onset

of symptoms subsequently leading to invalidity

retirement.

It would be advisable for him to take up a position

where no heavy lifting is required. With this

restriction I would expect him to continue gainful

employment until retiring age."

(T9/39)

22. On 22 April 1986, Dr Lloyd, upon viewing the documentation but without examining the applicant, reached the following conclusion:

"The specialist reports that Mr Gelsthorpe has

completely recovered from his previous back injury.

However, x-rays taken on 3 April 1986 reveal de

generative disc disease of the lower lumbar spine.

I recommend that a Benefit Classification Certificate

be retained as the applicant is not likely by reason

of his lumbar degenerative disc disease, to continue

to be an eligible employee until attaining the maximum

retiring age.

Mr Gelsthorpe's work involves bending and lifting,

which could aggravate the back condition resulting in

inability to continue his duties."

(T10/41)

23. On 29 July 1986, a delegate of the Commissioner refused to revoke the BCC, having regard to Mr Dhillon's report of the medical examination undergone by the applicant on 3 April 1986 (T3/19).

24. By letter of 6 August 1986, the applicant indicated he wished to appeal against the decision of the delegate and as previously mentioned, this letter was treated as a request for reconsideration pursuant to sub-s.154(2) of the Act.

25. The applicant then obtained the services of solicitors who arranged for him to be seen by Mr Harold Schaeffer, a neuro-surgeon, on 29 November 1986. Following examination, Mr Schaeffer issued a medical report dated 1 December 1986 in which he concluded:

"He suffered a ligamentous back strain as a con

sequence of a lifting incident in 1978.

However he has long since recovered from the con

sequences of that lifting strain. He has had no

symptoms referable to his back for a period of

approximately seven years.

At this current examination there is no evidence

whatsoever of any clinical back problem and, as I have

just indicated, his x-rays show no more than mild to

moderate degenerative change which would be consistent

with his age.

He is clearly doing quite heavy work on a regular

basis.

I believe that this man has recovered very well indeed

from his previous injury and I do not really believe

that his back is significantly more vulnerable than

that of the average 51 year old man. Specifically, I

believe that he will be able to continue duties as an

orderly and that, on the balance of probabilities, he

will remain fit for work of this nature until normal

retiring age.

In reply to your further question, it is my opinion

that, on the basis of this examination, there is not a

reasonably identifiable risk of his being unfit for

normal duties as an orderly up until the age of 65

years.

Mr Gelsthorpe presented to me as a particularly well-

motivated type of man."

(T15/51-52)

Following this report, upon request Mr Schaeffer sent a further letter in which he expressed additional opinions in relation to the x-ray report of 3 April 1986 and comments made by Mr Dhillon. Mr Schaeffer replied in these terms:

"I believe that the true situation here is that

moderate narrowing of the L3/4 and L4/5 discs and

quite well-marked narrowing of the L5/S1 disc space

would be commonplace findings in a man in his early

fifties and such radiological findings certainly do

not necessarily imply any significant tendency to

recurring back symptoms.

I should point out that it would, in fact, be unusual

to see x-rays of a man in his early fifties which do

not show some degree of degenerative change of this

nature.

I have noted that you have asked me to specifically

comment upon the opinion of Mr Dhillon and in this

regard I would have to say that I believe that the

weakness of Mr Dhillon's report is that he appears to

me to be basing his opinion exclusively upon the x-ray

results and he ignores his own negative clinical

findings.

To quote Mr Dhillon:- "There were no abnormal physical

findings. He had a full range of low back movements

and there was no neurological abnormality in both

lower limbs. To demonstrate to me that he was in no

way physically restricted he stood on his hands.".

One cannot arrive at an opinion about a back condition

without considering all aspects of the case - clinical

and radiological."

(T16/53)

26. In the meantime the respondent arranged for the applicant to be seen by a general surgeon, Mr Byrne, on 14 January 1987. Following his examination, Mr Byrne issued a report and concluded:

"In summary it is my considered opinion that this man

is fit to continue employment as a Ward Orderly at RGH

Daw Park. I do not feel that at the moemnt (sic)

there is a "real risk" that this man will be unable to

continue his tasks before he reaches retiring age.

In answer to your specific quetions (sic):-

(A) This man has radiological evidence of mild to

moderate lumbar spondylosis but he is completely

asymptomatic as has already been stated by Prof.

Smith.

(B) Spondylosis is somewhat unpredictable. It may

possibly deteriorate but most certainly at the moment

is long standing and has not shown any symptomatic

evidence of doing so. It is important of course that

Mr. Gelsthorpe continues to maintain correct postures

when lifting but has already demonstrated his ability

to do this during his rehabilitation testing in 1984.

(C) It is unlikely that he will have his working life

cut short before the age of 65 by deterioration,

however, nothing is ever absolute in this condition.

(D) It would be correct to redefine Mr. Gelsthorpe

condition as a degnerative (sic) disc disease of the

lower lumbar spine but not of an advanced degree."

(T17/55)

27. Again, Dr Lloyd was asked to express an oversight of the opinions of the specialists, and on 23 January 1987, after summarising the various specialist views, he concluded:

"Due to the differing specialist referee's opinions, I

find it difficult to oversight this case. However, I

consider it reasonable to give Mr Gelsthorpe the

benefit of the doubt and recommend that the BCC for

"Injury to Lumbo-Sacral Intervertebral Disc" be

revoked."

(T19/58)

28. Subsequent to Dr Lloyd's expression of opinion, further letters were written on behalf of the respondent to Mr Byrne, Mr Schaeffer and Mr Dhillon requesting them to "express in percentage terms the risk .... that Mr Gelsthorpe, by reason of or for a reason connected with his back condition, will not continue working to his maximum retiring age". The doctors in reply to those letters expressed, in the case of Mr Byrne, a 5% risk (T23/62), in the case of Mr Schaeffer, "no more than 10%" (T24/63) and Mr Dhillon stated that the risk was 50% (T25/64).

29. On 29 June 1987, a delegate of the respondent, on reconsideration, refused to revoke the BCC but having regard to the specialists' reports indicating that the applicant had completely recovered from his previous back injury, varied the condition specified in the BCC to "Degenerative disc disease of the lower lumbar spine" (T2/3-18).

30. In addition to this chronological summary of the background history to the decision under review, additional evidence supplementing this information was given at the hearing. The applicant, since 18 July 1984 up until the present time, has been employed as an orderly at the Daw Park Repatriation Hospital. From time to time, he has also been employed as a security patrolman. His duties as an orderly are in part contained in a Duty Statement attached to T27, although that Statement is out-of-date and no longer accurately contains the duties performed by the applicant as an Orderly Grade I.

31. The applicant's duties as an orderly are as follows. The greatest majority of the applicant's duties are cleaning activities which take up approximately five hours in any shift. These cleaning activities may vary as the orderlies work in teams of three and different cleaning tasks are allocated to each. Cleaning duties include washing around the Sister's station, washing handbasins, vacuuming carpets and linoleum, sterilising bottles, mopping floors, wiping over locker tops and, to a lesser extent, polishing floors and wiping down walls and windows if visual inspection indicates they require cleaning. The cleaning operations will sometimes involve moving beds and waist high patient lockers which are on wheels. Occasionally heavier items may be required to be moved for the purpose of cleaning, but any awkward or heavy equipment is only moved with the assistance of another orderly. It is agreed that although the Duty Statement in T27 refers to "perform heavy cleaning tasks" this is an overstatement of the cleaning requirements which are not regarded by the applicant nor any other witness as "heavy".

32. In addition to cleaning duties, the applicant is also required to porter or trolley infirm patients and cadavers on either wheelchairs or beds. There are occasions when patients need to be trolleyed or ported up or down a slight incline. If beds are used as a means of transport, they are wheeled by two orderlies, one orderly at each end of the bed and in this manner even the inclines, which are not great, can be traversed without difficulty. The only incline of any significance at the hospital is one that the applicant would not usually be required to use.

33. The prime responsibility for lifting of patients onto a bed, trolley or wheelchair is that of the nursing staff. Previously it was a duty of the orderlies, but of more recent times it is recognised that the nursing staff are in a better position to assess a patient's particular condition for lifting purposes and to perform such lifting. Orderlies are only required to assist in lifting patients if a request is made by nursing staff, which is a rare occurrence. If an orderly is asked to assist with lifting, it will usually be to hold the legs or feet of a patient, while two or three nursing assistants lift the rest of the body. The only other occasion when orderlies may be involved in lifting is in an emergency, for example, if a patient falls out of a bed. But again, it is recognised that the prime responsibility for lifting that patient resides with the nursing staff, and a red button is used to call for a nurse. On occasions where a minor fall is involved and the patient is able to move him or herself, then perhaps an orderly may assist a patient to his or her feet or to a chair. These are emergency situations and in Mr Wallace's experience of some twenty two years, orderlies are not generally used in such emergencies and he can think of only one occasion in the last nine months where an orderly was involved in assisting a patient who had fallen out of bed. In summary, therefore, only minimal lifting of patients is involved in the duties of an orderly.

34. An orderly is also required to carbolise and make the beds of patients who are either discharged or have died. They are not required to make patients' beds on a daily basis during their occupancy. The dirty linen, including sheets, towels, pyjamas and hospital robes, is placed into a linen bag which is suspended from a circular trolley. It is the duty of an orderly to take the full linen bags into a "disposal room" where the bag is unhooked from the trolley, tied and dragged approximately twenty feet across the floor and lifted onto a bench at waist height and placed into the main bin. The weight of the linen bag varies between ten and seventeen kilograms but an average weight would be around thirteen kilograms. This is the heaviest item that an orderly is required to lift alone.

35. An orderly is also required to empty and clean refuse containers which consist of a small plastic bag in each patient's room which are collected and placed into a green garbage bag. The small containers can be lifted with one hand and the green garbage bags which will contain that refuse together with paper towels used by nursing staff, is then taken to a central area. The applicant estimates that the green garbage bags would only weigh approximately one kilogram.

36. On rare occasions the orderlies are also requested to handle cylinders of medical gases. These are approximately eighteen inches to two feet in height and weigh only three kilograms. Being light, they can be lifted with one hand.

37. Orderlies are also asked to assist with control of pests which again is a rare occurrence and would probably be only with regard to flies or ants. Rodents are handled by a separate contractor.

38. Orderlies, along with nursing staff, are required to perform fire control duties, which consist of having one or two fire drills a year in which they are given theoretical instructions on how to evacuate patients. The drills do not require actual evacuation. In the event of a real fire, the orderlies would be required to act on instructions from the nursing staff and to assist in evacuation of patients either in wheelchairs, beds, or by dragging them on appropriate bedding or carpet. In the twenty two years that Mr Wallace has been working at the hospital, there has never been an actual fire which has required these evacuation procedures to be put into operation.

39. In addition, orderlies may be called on to assist in moving furniture. With the exception of recent years, the moving of furniture is an unusual occurrence which has been performed on only a few occasions. The moving of furniture entails shifting desks and chairs from one room to another within the hospital. Of recent times, with renovations being undertaken at the Daw Park Repatriation Hospital, the orderlies have been involved in shifting beds and lockers from one end of the hospital to the other. This has been performed by a team of orderlies with the use of trolleys with furniture being moved by two or more orderlies. The orderlies are instructed on lifting techniques by both a physiotherapist and an occupational therapist and are expected to utilise those techniques when shifting furniture.

40. The duties of security patrolman have been performed by the applicant only on a temporary basis when the hospital's patrolman is on holidays or ill or alternatively, when the applicant has been asked to perform overtime which sometimes includes security work. The work of a patrolman is that of reconnaissance, checking cars, equipment and doors, on foot or alternatively on a bicycle. There is no heavy work involved, merely mobility.

41. Over the period when the applicant has been employed as an orderly, he has done considerable overtime. In an eight month period from August 1987 until April 1988, the applicant worked 170.5 hours actual overtime. This is representative of the average annual amount of overtime which the applicant performs at the hospital, the majority of overtime work being available during the months from August to April.

42. The applicant gave evidence that he has no difficulty whatsoever with his employment duties which he regards as light, particularly when compared with the work he previously performed as a blacksmith forgeman. The applicant considers he is a very fit 53 year old man. He walks on every available opportunity and swims in all seasons three days per week, up to distances of four kilometres each day.

43. The applicant presented himself at the hearing as a fit, alert man and each of the doctors commented favourably on his degree and level of fitness which they all regarded as above average for a 53 year old man. In particular, Mr Schaeffer and Mr Byrne commented on his suppleness and agility which the applicant has developed to the extent that during the course of examination, he voluntarily stood on his hands to indicate his level of fitness and demonstrated the degree of mobility of his spine while doing so.

44. At the hearing, of the four doctors who gave evidence, three of them, namely Mr Schaeffer, Mr Byrne and Professor Smith, expressed the strong point of view that the applicant had completely recovered from his back injury in 1978, and that the x-ray findings of the applicant's back were what one could reasonably expect for a man of his age (although two of the doctors expressed the view that whilst there was marginally more degeneration than would be seen for a man his age, the x-rays were consistent with a man of his age who had worked in hard occupational work for most of his life). These three doctors were also strongly of the view that the applicant was above average fitness for his age group. All three doctors expressed the view that the applicant was able to perform all of the duties required of him in his job as an orderly, including those duties which are required or requested of him only on an occasional or rare basis. Each of the three doctors expressed the view that the applicant's degenerative condition of his spine was such that not only was he no more at risk of injuring his back or becoming incapacitated from his back than would an average 53 year old, he was in a better position than most people of his age because of his level of fitness and because of his knowledge and use of lifting techniques taught to him both at the Rehabilitation Centre and reinforced in his present occupation. All three doctors were in agreement that there was a less than 10% risk of the applicant not being able to continue with his employment as an orderly up until 65 years due to lumbo-sacral degeneration, one doctor assessing the risk as only 5%.

45. The three doctors specifically expressed distrust in viewing x-ray material alone in assessing a person's back condition and expressed the view that x-ray findings should only be looked at in conjunction with a patient's presentation on clinical examination, complaints of symptoms or the absence of signs or symptoms. Each of the doctors were impressed by the high pain threshold, motivation and level of fitness of the applicant.

46. On the other hand, the fourth doctor, Mr Dhillon, expressed a different point of view. Mr Dhillon was impressed by the radiological findings which, in his view, indicated a level of disc degeneration which he considered to be in excess of the average 53 year old person. He expressed the opinion that the injury which the applicant sustained in 1978 partially con tributed to the x-ray findings. Mr Dhillon was not impressed by the absence of symptomatology of the applicant, but held the view that given the level of degeneration which the applicant presently has and the projected degeneration which was likely to continue in the future, in combination with what the doctor foresaw as "heavy" orderly duties requiring lifting of patients and pushing of beds up inclines, the applicant had a 50% risk of not being able to reach his retirement age of 65 having regard to his back condition.

47. In considering the conflicting medical evidence before us, we prefer the evidence given by Mr Schaeffer, Mr Byrne and Professor Smith where it conflicts with that given by Mr Dhillon. It is not merely the fact that those three doctors together form the majority opinion, but rather we think that Mr Dhillon presumed that the duties of an orderly were more onerous than in fact was indicated on the evidence. The Tribunal also considers that Mr Dhillon placed too much emphasis on the radiological evidence and omitted to take adequate account of the positive characteristics of the applicant including his fitness, motivation and high pain threshold. Further, there is some doubt as to whether Mr Dhillon was applying the correct test insofar as there is a suggestion in his evidence he was considering the assessment of "real risk" at some time in the future rather than assessing the "real risk" of premature retirement as it presently exists.

48. Having reached this conclusion on the evidence, we will now turn to consider the legislative requirements.

49. The decision of the delegate of 29 July 1986 refusing to revoke the BCC was made pursuant to para.16(8)(c) of the Act as it stood prior to amendments made by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No. 2) (supra). Paragraph 16(8)(c) as it stood provided:

"The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports

(if any), and such other matters as he considers

relevant, and -

....

(c) in any other case - the Commissioner shall refuse

the request to revoke or vary the benefit

classification certificate."

50. In the applicant's case, the delegate refused to revoke the certificate pursuant to para.16(8)(c). The applicant requested a reconsideration of this decision under sub-s.154(2) and another delegate of the Commissioner reconsidered that decision under sub-s.154(4) on 29 June 1987. By that time, amendments had been made to para.16(8)(c) which required the decision-maker "in any other case" to either refuse the request to revoke the BCC or to revoke the BCC and to issue a new BCC in substitution. The delegate who was required under sub-s.154(4) to either "confirm, revoke or vary" the previous delegate's decision, chose to confirm the decision to refuse to revoke, but amended the description of the condition on the BCC by removing the reference to the injury and in his view more accurately describing the applicant's condition as "Degenerative Disc Disease of the Lower Lumbar Disc".

51. In so confirming the previous delegate's decision, and varying the description on the BCC, the delegate should have had regard to para.16(8)(c) as amended by Act No. 151 of 1986, which from 18 December 1986 reads:

"in any other case - the Commissioner shall refuse the

request to revoke the benefit classification certi

ficate or to revoke the benefit classification

certificate and to issue in substitution for the

certificate a new benefit classification certificate."

52. The delegate should have instead varied the decision under sub-s.154(4) and then with regard to para.16(8)(c), revoked the original BCC and issued a new BCC in substitution containing the varied description. These remarks are by way of aside, as the decision which is to be reviewed by this Tribunal is that made by the previous delegate under the former para.16(8)(c) with this Tribunal taking into account the subsequent amendments made to that paragraph.

53. Paragraph 16(8)(c), as it stood before the amendments and as it stands after the amendments, is a provision which applies if on consideration, the Commissioner is of the opinion that paras.16(8)(a) or (b) are not applicable. This inter pretation is gleaned from the commencing words "in any other case" which must refer to any case other than that described in paras.16(8)(a) or (b). Paragraph 16(8)(c) was and is therefore designed as a "catch-all" paragraph.

54. Unlike paras.16(8)(a) and (b), para.16(8)(c) does not itself contain any criteria by which the Commissioner shall refuse the request to revoke the BCC or to revoke the BCC and issue a new BCC in substitution. As to the first of these options, namely the refusal of the request to revoke the BCC, the absence of criteria is not a problem as such a consequence would automatically flow if the Commissioner was of the opinion that neither of the requirements in para.16(8)(a) nor (b) were fulfilled.

55. This is not such a simple solution in relation to the second option to revoke the BCC and to then issue a new one in substitution. The mischief to which this latter option is presumed to provide a solution, is one in which on consideration, the Commissioner is of the opinion that there was not a real risk that by "reason connected with the condition" (para.16(8)(a)) or all of the conditions (para.16(8)(b)) specified in the BCC, the applicant would prematurely retire, but that such a consequence would result instead from another condition not mentioned in the BCC. However, the criteria for revoking a BCC may not be the same as the criteria for issuing a new BCC in substitution, and that difference is reflected in the wording of paras. 16(8)(a) and (b) themselves. The Commissioner in revoking a BCC under either para. 16(8)(a) or sub-para.16(8)(b)(i), is required to be satisfied as to a negative, namely that "there is not a real risk" before revoking a BCC, whereas the Commissioner in revoking and issuing a new BCC under sub-para.16(8)(b)(ii) is required to be satisfied as to a positive, namely that "there is a real risk". (Tribunal emphasis) The difference and significance of the negative and positive expressions of "not a real risk" and "real risk" are canvassed in Re Rohde and Commissioner for Superannuation S.87/66 (delivered 4 November 1988).

56. In considering what criteria should be used by a decision-maker for the issue of a new BCC under para.16(8)(c), we first of all note the obvious, namely that para.16(8)(c) forms part of s.16, which section is generally concerned with the issuing and revocation of BCCs in conjunction with the complementary requirements to undergo medical examination. It is therefore reasonable in the absence of specific provisions (as are contained in para.16(8)(a) and sub-paras.16(8)(b)(i) and (ii)), when considering the revocation and substitution of a new BCC under para.16(8)(c), to look at the criteria upon which an initial BCC was issued under sub-s.16(4). Sub-section 16(4) like sub-para.16(8)(b)(ii), expresses the risk to be in the positive, namely that the decision-maker must be of the opinion that there "is a real risk" of premature retirement.

57. In these circumstances, it is consistent with sub-para. 16(8)(b)(ii) and sub-s.16(4), that in deciding under para.16(8)(c) to revoke a BCC and issue a new one, the Commissioner should be of the opinion that the positive exists, namely that there is a "real risk" that, by reason of the new condition, the employee will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains maximum retiring age.

58. For the sake of completeness, we mention that before reaching the above interpretation of para.16(8)(c), we also gave consideration to another possible interpretation. A possible alternative interpretation is that pursuant to para.16(8)(c), the Commissioner may either refuse the request to revoke the BCC, or the Commissioner may refuse the request to revoke the BCC and issue a new BCC in substitution, the latter request encompassing both the revocation of a BCC and the issuing of a new BCC. Such an interpretation gains prima facie support from the wording of sub-s.16(6) which uses the same wording and as amended reads:

"an eligible employee ... may ... request the

Commissioner to revoke the certificate or to revoke

the certificate and issue in substitution for the

certificate a new benefit classification certificate"

(Tribunal underlining)

However, if this interpretation were adopted, the Act would be deficient in that there would be no provision which empowered the Commissioner to accede to an employee's request (pursuant to sub-s. 16(6)) to revoke an existing BCC and at the same time issue a new BCC which specified a condition or conditions not previously specified. This situation would arise because the alternative power of a Commissioner under para.16(8)(c) is to refuse the request to revoke the BCC and issue a substitute BCC, and the only other powers are either to revoke the BCC (paras.16(8)(a) and (b), or refuse the request to revoke the BCC (para.16(8)(c)). There would be no power to revoke a BCC and issue a certificate in substitution unless it related to a condition already specified in the certificate (sub-para.16(8)(b)(ii)).

59. This was obviously not the intention of the legislature when it drafted the amendments to sub-s.16(6) and para.16(8)(c) as contained in Act No. 151 of 1986. Prior to these amendments, the Commissioner was empowered to "revoke or vary the certificate"; the insertion of the words "to revoke the certificate or to revoke the certificate and issue in substitution for the certificate a new benefit classification certificate" was in effect a replacement of the alternative to "vary" with the alternative to revoke and issue a new certificate.

60. To deny an employee's right to have a legitimate request for revocation and issue of a new BCC acceded to by the Commissioner bearing in mind the intention of the legislature, would be contrary to the common law principles of common sense and justice.

61. We turn now to consider what is meant by the words "real risk". In considering the meaning of those words, it is necessary to put them in an historical perspective.

62. Before the amendment made to sub-s.16(4) by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (supra) assented to on 24 June 1986, the test expressed in that sub-section was that the Commissioner was required to be of the opinion that "the person is not likely .... to .... attain(s) his maximum retiring age" (Tribunal emphasis). As a result of the amendments made by Act No. 80 of 1986, the wording was changed to require the Commissioner to be of the opinion that "there is a real risk that the person .... will not .... attain(s) his maximum retiring age" (Tribunal emphasis). The main amendment to sub-s.16(4) was therefore to delete the words "not likely" and to substitute for those words the phrase "there is a real risk". It is to be noted that prior to the amendments the Commissioner was required to be satisfied as to a negative condition, namely "not likely" whereas the amendment now required the Commissioner to be satisfied as to a positive condition, namely "a real risk".

63. The term "real risk" first appears in relation to s.16 as it stood prior to the amendments in the decision in Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN N93. That matter was concerned with a review of a delegate's decision refusing to revoke a BCC pursuant to sub-s.16(8) of the Act as it stood prior to Act No. 80 of 1986. In the course of discussion of the meaning of the word "likely", the Tribunal stated:

"When, in sub-ss (4) and (8) of s 16, parliament has

referred to a person who is not 'likely', by reason of

or for a reason connected with some physical or mental

condition, to continue to be an eligible employee

until attaining maximum retiring age, it seems to us

that, in the context, parliament must be referring to

conditions which give rise to a 'real risk' and not

merely to conditions which create a 'probability' or

'better than even chance' that the person will not

continue until attaining maximum retiring age.

There is no question in our minds that where the

medical evidence establishes a probability or 'better

than even chance' that a particular condition will

prove disabling before a person attains maximum

retiring age, the Commissioner is entitled, if not

obliged, to issue a certificate. But we are unable to

accept that the section limits the Commissioner to

issuing a certificate only where such a degree of

certainty is established. The general medical

standards developed for the guidance of Commonwealth

medical officers, which were designed at establishing

acceptable levels of risk, are as we understand them,

consistent with that broader view. Failure to meet

the general medical standards may not render it

'probable' that a person will not continue in

employment until maximum retiring age. But such

failure does suggest that, if the condition persists

early death or retirement on grounds of invalidity is

an event which might well happen."

Contained within that passage is the suggestion that the word "likely" in either sub-ss.16(4) or 16(8) of the Act as it then stood, referred to conditions giving rise to a "real risk" or "an event which might well happen", in contrast to a "probability".

64. Subsequently a Tribunal differently constituted by a Presidential member in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation 1985) 8 ALD 293 gave detailed attention to the meaning of the words "not likely" in the context of sub-s.16(4) of the Act as it stood prior to the amendments made by Act No. 80 of 1986. Following careful reasoning, the Tribunal expressed the view that in the context of sub-s.16(4) the word "likely" should not be interpreted alone but should necessarily be linked with the previous word so that the term "not likely" should be considered as an entity:

"We have to say also that we do not consider it

correct to adopt an interpretative approach which

discards the word "not" and construes the word

"likely" standing on its own, in so doing introducing

connotations of that word that are quite inapposite

when the total phrase, in particular the expression

"not likely", is in question. The presence of the

word "not" makes it very difficult to read into the

word "likely" the connotation "such as may well

happen". It follows that in our opinion the requisite

opinion cannot be formed unless it is found that it is

more probable than not that the person in question

will not continue to his or her maximum retiring age."

(pp 311-312)

65. Following the Tribunal decision in Re Bewley (supra), the Tribunal in a later decision in Re Weeks and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N18 was asked to review the decision in Re Bewley (supra). The Tribunal did so and affirmed the conclusions reached in Re Bewley (supra). Subsequently, a Tribunal composed of a judicial Presidential member in Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N129, when reviewing a delegate's decision made pursuant to sub-s.16(4) of the Act as it then stood, specifically disagreed with the interpretation of sub-s.16(4) adopted by the Tribunal in Re Bewley (supra) and instead made the following comments at N132 and N133:

"Taking these matters into consideration, it seems to

us that the language of sub-s 16(4) is apt to include

a case where the opinion is held that, by reason of or

for a reason connected with the disclosed condition,

death or retirement before attaining the maximum

retiring age is an event which may well happen. A

person in respect of whom such an opinion is held may,

we think, properly be described as one who is "not

likely .... to continue to be an eligible employee"

for the requisite period. Although the sub-section is

expressed in terms of the continuation of a state of

affairs in which the person remains an eligible

employee, its obvious concern is with the degree of

likelihood that, by reason of or for a reason

connected with the disclosed condition, that state of

affairs will not continue. It is concerned with the

degree of likelihood of death or retirement prior to

the attainment of the maximum retiring age, either of

which events would precipitate a claim for the payment

of benefits under the Act."

.....

"We acknowledge that the change of language to which

we have referred gives rise to some difficulty but we

think the difficulty remains whichever of the two

suggested meanings of sub-s 16(4) is adopted. The

existence of the difficulty, therefore, provides no

guidance as to the meaning which should be ascribed to

sub-s 16(4). It seems to us that, giving to sub-s

16(4) the meaning which we think it has, sub-s (8) is

to be construed as meaning that a benefit classifica

tion certificate is to be revoked only where the

Commissioner is of opinion that there is no real risk

that early death or retirement will occur. Such a

construction of the provision is, we think, reasonably

open."

66. With a view to statutorily resolving these conflicting decisions, amendments were introduced by Act No. 80 of 1986 which followed the expressions used by the Tribunal in Re Dakin (supra). The interpretation of the legislation adopted by the Tribunal in Re Bewley (supra) was specifically rejected by the government. Senator Walsh, Minister for Finance, during the Second Reading of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Bill on 10 June 1986, in Hansard, 10 June 1986, p 3614, made this quite clear.

67. More recently since the amendments, the Tribunal in Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217 has grappled with the meaning of "real risk" in the context of a review of a decision made pursuant to the amendment of sub- s.16(4) of the Act and the Tribunal at para. 8 expressed the following point of view:

"Both Mr Lorkin and Mr Whithear drew a distinction

between a bare possibility and a real risk. We

consider that that is a proper distinction to make.

We were invited to examine the meanings of the

individual words "real" and "risk" given in various

dictionaries, and have done so; however, we are

satisfied that the mere conjunction of any of the

meanings of "real" with any of the meanings of "risk"

does not capture the essence of the phrase "real

risk", which is a phrase having connotations of its

own distinct from the dictionary meanings of the two

words comprising it. In the phrase "risk" refers to a

chance that something detrimental will occur. There

is a "real risk" where a factual situation exists

which has inherent in it a particular potential to

result in that detrimental occurrence. Usually the

phrase is used in relation to the process of making an

assessment of whether precautions are warranted to

prevent either the occurrence of the detrimental thing

or the consequences of it occurring. We are satisfied

that in the context of the Act section 16 is intended

to provide that a benefit classification certificate

should be issued where an eligible employee is

suffering from an identified condition which has a

particular potential to result in his or her dying, or

becoming so incapacitated as to be unable to continue

in employment, before reaching maximum retiring age.

In our view, if an eligible employee has a permanent

medical condition which may directly or through its

complications cause premature death or incapacity,

rarely, if ever, can an opinion properly be formed

that there is not a real risk that by reason of it or

for a reason connected with it the employee will die

or retire before reaching the maximum retiring age."

68. At the hearing before us, it was contended on behalf of the applicant that the words "real risk" meant something more than a bare possibility but something less than a probability. A similar view was expressed by the respondent but, in addition, Mr Eadie also submitted that the words "real risk" incorporated "a good deal less than a probability". The respondent also incorporated the test enunciated in Re Nicholson (supra), namely whether or not a factual situation exists, that is, a person has an identified condition which has inherent in it a particular potential to result in that detrimental occurrence, namely incapacity or death, such that the person is unable to reach maximum retirement age.

69. This Tribunal finds some difficulty in applying only the test which is set out in Re Nicholson (supra). The Tribunal does not consider that the words "particular potential" for a condition to result in a detrimental occurrence, are a substitution for the words "real risk". The words "particular potential" in turn would require some definition. Does the word "particular" mean "specific" or does it mean "something special"? It could be said that any mental or physical condition has inherent within it a particular potential to result in a detrimental occurrence, and the word "particular" says nothing about the gravamen or the extent of that potential. This Tribunal considers it preferable to view the words "real risk" not as a term of art but rather to regard them as a matter of common parlance. The word "risk" refers to a chance or hazard (see Macquarie Dictionary) and the word "real" in that context would mean something other than nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful. Therefore a "real risk" would be a chance which is more than nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful but less than a probability; how much less than a probability it is not possible to specify.

70. A further difficulty this Tribunal has with a substitution of the words "real risk" with the test described in Re Nicholson (supra) is that the test as expressed concentrates only on the condition of the person and whether or not the condition has a particular potential to result in incapacity or death prior to maximum retirement age. In our view, the words "real risk" are directed not only to the condition, but also to the subject matter, "the person", and whether or not the person by reason of a condition will not reach maximum retirement age. By the statutory inclusion of the words "the person" there is a requirement for a decision-maker to take into account not merely a physical or mental condition in the abstract, but a condition suffered by the individual, which necessarily includes the characteristics of the person suffering from the said condition. The words "real risk" are of course also directed and probably primarily directed to the condition by which the person may prematurely retire, and regard must be had to the condition itself, its nature, characteristics, symptomatology, whether it is progressive, static or likely to improve and all other factors of the condition relevant to the person's employment.

71. A further issue raised by this review is whether the nature of the employment and indeed the particular duties required to be performed in that employment, are relevant criteria in assessing the risk. Sub-section 16(4) asks that a decision-maker consider the real risk that a person will not continue to be "an eligible employee until ... maximum retirement age". The words "eligible employee" are defined in s.3 of the Act and, in general terms, include employees and others as described. There is no indication that either the nature of employment nor the duties performed by that person are necessary considerations to be taken into account when assessing "real risk". On the contrary, the inference is that it is only being an employee or being in employment in a general sense, namely paid employment by the Commonwealth up to the maximum retirement age, that is the relevant consideration.

72. In this case, much evidence was adduced as to the type of employment and the specific tasks performed by the applicant in his employment. This evidence could be adduced in this case with some confidence because we were looking in retrospect, and because the likely future employment of this applicant was known. This will not be the case in many instances when examination of potential applicants for the purposes of joining the Super annuation Scheme takes place. Many persons enter the Scheme when they are young and their future career is unknown. It would be an impossible task involving guesswork to assess their employment future if the nature and duties of employment until retirement were requisite considerations in the issuing of BCCs. There may be some instances where the nature of the employment and its duties may be relevant in assessing "real risk" of premature retirement, and those instances may include a case such as this where the time-span before retirement is short and the probable nature of employment and its duties are known factors. It would appear foolish in such circumstances to ignore the reality of the employment.

73. In addition to the above comments, we do not view the term "real risk" as meaning something quantitative such that it can be expressed in percentage terms. Whilst it may be of assistance in ascertaining the degree of risk to have some regard to percentages in assessing the degree of risk, as a decision-maker is required to have regard not only to the physical or mental condition, but also the characteristics of the applicant, it is not appropriate in putting all of these component parts together to express the result in percentage terms. It may be a convenient method for at least some witnesses, such as doctors endeavouring to come to grips particularly with the physical or mental condition to illustrate the seriousness or impact of the condition on employment by expressing the risk in percentage terms. However, because of the overall components required to be considered under "real risk", percentages are not appropriate.

74. The Tribunal considers it preferable not to substitute other tests to determine "real risk" bearing in mind the variety of persons, conditions and employment which may be the subject of consideration when issuing BCCs. The variety is infinite but all variables must be taken into account in assessing the "real risk" of premature retirement.

75. To summarise our views:

(1) The term "real risk" is not a term of art and

should be interpreted in accordance with its

dictionary meaning;

(2) "Real risk" refers to a chance of an occurrence

which is real and is more than nominal, imaginary,

fictitious or fanciful but less than a probability;

(3) "Real risk" requires consideration and assessment

of the characteristics of the person, the nature,

gravamen and characteristics of the condition in

the context of employment to maximum retirement

age;

(4) Although percentages may sometimes assist in

assessing the seriousness or otherwise of certain

conditions in relation to employment, "real risk"

is not a quantitative assessment such that it can

be reduced to percentage terms in order to

determine its existence.

76. Applying these concepts to the findings of fact.

(1) We are of the opinion that there is not a real risk

that the applicant, by reason of the physical

condition named in the BCC, that is, "Injury to

Lumbo-Sacral Intervertebral Disc", will not

continue to be an eligible employee until he

retains maximum retiring age;

(2) We are not of the opinion that there is a real risk

that the applicant, by reason of the physical

condition of "Lumbo-sacral disc degeneration" as

named in the amended BCC, will not continue to be

an eligible employee until he attains the maximum

retiring age (sub-s.16(4) and para.16(8)(c)).

77. In reaching these conclusions, we have taken into account the applicant's personal characteristics including his stamina, fitness, motivation and age. We have also taken into consideration the condition of the applicant's back adopting our preferences on the medical evidence, and had regard to the percentages of risk expressed by the preferred doctors. We have also considered these factors in the context of the applicant's employment in a general sense, that is, as an employee of the Commonwealth Government.

78. Insofar as the particular employment or duties as distinct from employment in a general sense may be a relevant consideration as to whether there is a "real risk" of premature retirement, in this case we have the benefit of the fact that it will only be twelve years until the applicant's retirement. During this relatively short time-frame, his employment is most likely to be that of an orderly. On the evidence, we do not consider this work to be heavy or demanding, he is fit and he has symptomless disc degeneration, and we are not satisfied there is a real risk of his premature retirement due to lumbo-sacral disc degeneration, which degeneration is consistent with his age. We are satisfied that he has fully recovered from the injury he sustained to his back in 1978. Further, insofar as the particular duties of the applicant's employment may be relevant, we are of the view that the remote chance of the applicant injuring his back for reasons connected with lumbo-sacral disc degeneration in an emergency situation such as a fire, is not a factor indicative of a "real risk" of the applicant prematurely retiring due to that condition. The chance of a fire occurring, in combination with a situation in which the applicant is involved in an awkward lift of a patient thereby causing him to injure his back and retire permanently due to lumbo-sacral disc degeneration or a condition connected with such degeneration, is too remote, even in combination with other factors, to give rise to a "real risk" of premature retirement.

79. For these reasons, the Tribunal finds the decision under review should be set aside and in substitution therefor, a decision be made that the BCC issued with respect to the applicant should be revoked.

I agree with the majority that the Benefit Classification Certificate should be revoked.

2. I agree with the interpretation given to the words "real risk".

3. I have a different view on the interpretation of sub-section 16(8) for reasons which I have submitted to the presiding member. I am bound, however, by the terms of sub-s.42(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 to accept the ruling of the presiding member on questions of law.

4. I would agree the Act is deficient.
