Re AK and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 340 (4 November 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: A.K.

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A86/39

AAT No. 2971

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

Dr D.J. Howell (Member)

N.J. Attwood (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - applicant deemed retired on grounds of physical or mental incapacity in 1977 - condition of anxiety depression and paranoid personality disorder - s. 74 examination requested by the applicant as a means to qualify for re-instatement to the Public Service under s. 75 in 1984 - decision that the applicant has not been restored to health - decision on appeal - evidence other than s. 74 medical reports accepted in evidence - regard to issues of health and suitable duties in the construction of s. 75 - paranoid personality disorder as a discrete condition.

Words and Phrases - "restore"

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ss.37, 40(1A)

HEARING

CANBERRA

4:11:1986

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is a review of a decision made under section 75 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("The Act") following an examination of the applicant made pursuant to section 74 (1) of the Act. The relevant parts of the legislation are in the following terms:

"74. (1) The Commissioner may, by notice in writing

given to a person to whom invalidity pension is payable

but who has not attained the age of 65 years, require

the person

(a) to submit himself for medical examination by a

medical practitioner at a time and place specified

in the notice; or

(b) to furnish in writing to the Commissioner, within

such a period as is specified in the notice, such

information as is required by the notice with

respect to any employment (whether as an employee

or on his own account) in which he has been engaged

during such period as is specified in the notice.

.....................................................

75. (1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after

receiving the report or reports of a medical

practitioner or medical practitioners with respect to

the health of a person to whom invalidity pension is

payable that the health of the person has become so

restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind

that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable

to be performed by him (having regard to the duties

performed by him immediately before his retirement on

the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as

the Commissioner considers relevant), the Commissioner

shall so inform such person or authority as the

Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that

person or authority finding suitable employment for the

pensioner.

(2)

If

(a) the pensioner is offered by the Commonwealth or by

an approved authority employment (not being

employment on a part-time basis) that involves the

performance of duties that, in the opinion of the

Commissioner, are suitable to be performed by him

(having regard to the duties performed by him

immediately before his retirement on the ground of

invalidity and to such other matters as the

Commissioner considers relevant); and

(b) the person unreasonably refuses or fails to accept

the offer within 14 days after the receipt by him

of the offer, or within such further period as the

Commissioner allows,

the Commissioner may cancel the person's entitlement to

invalidity pension."

2. The applicant is entitled to an invalidity pension under the Act. He had retired from the Public Service on 30 June 1977, then being a Senior Finance Officer Grade 2, in the Investment Division, Policy and Development Branch, Department of Treasury.

3. By a series of events he came to be regarded as having been deemed to have retired on the grounds of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, due to the mental conditions of anxiety depression and paranoid personality disorder. He now claims that he has been restored to health so as to enable him to perform the kind of duties that he was performing at the time of his retirement. The decision under review is that he has not been so restored to health.

4. Contrary to the usual operation of sections 74 and 75, the applicant himself requested the initial examination. Dr Travers, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, reported on 30 August 1984: "Still suffering personality disorder and anxiety depression" and added that the condition had retrogressed since last examination. Then followed a number of further psychiatric examinations to which reference will be made later.

5. Certain facts relating to the applicant's behaviour were put to some of these psychiatrists in addition to reports of other psychiatrists. This led to the first problem that had to be resolved at a directions hearing.

Ruling on Evidence

6. The applicant contended that the Commissioner (and therefore this Tribunal) was entitled to look only at medical reports before coming to a decision. It was argued that factual evidence relating to the applicant's behaviour was irrelevant to the issues before us. The argument was based on a construction of the opening words of section 75 of the Act. It was put that the Commissioner could satisfy himself as to a restoration of health only on the basis of the reports referred to in the opening. These reports, in effect, were the exclusive basis of jurisdiction for the making of any decision under the section. This argument was rejected and we agreed to state our reasons for this ruling is this, our final decision.

7. We agreed to receive evidence of facts relating to the employment of the applicant between the date of his retirement in 1977 and the date of the hearing in 1986. For medical purposes, particularly for diagnostic and treating purposes, evidence of behaviour prior to 1977 is very important. A psychiatrist could not ignore events in the life of his patient, prior to the age of 32. However, we considered that the terms of the section limited the issue initially to one of restoration and that accordingly we should not receive evidence relating to the applicant's employment prior to his retirement. We also agreed to receive factual evidence relating to his behaviour outside the field of employment if it appeared to us to have some relevance in explaining his medical condition. We agreed to accept this evidence for two main reasons.

8. The evidence of all the psychiatrists who were called was unanimous in affirming that in the case of a personality disorder it would be necessary to have regard to the behaviour of the patient, both for the purpose of diagnosis and for treatment. The more a psychiatrist knows of the behaviour and motivation of his patient, the better able he is to advise him. It will be seen later that this evidence was, by and large, put to various psychiatrists to give them a broader history than might otherwise have been available from the applicant himself. For this reason alone we consider that the evidence was properly received.

9. Secondly, however, we consider the evidence falls within the expression "such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant" appearing between the brackets in section 75(1). In order to decide what duties are suitable, the Commissioner is entitled, and probably bound, to have regard not only to the duties performed by the applicant immediately before his retirement, but also to any other matters that might have a bearing upon his ability to resume performance of those duties.

10. For reasons which will later appear, this factual evidence we consider to be relevant for that purpose.

Construction of Section 75

11. The opening words of section 75 refer to receiving "the report or reports of a medical practitioner or medical practitioners". This is a reference to the result of the examination provided for in section 74. In the present case there was only one initial examination pursuant to that section. The report of Dr Travers is referred to above. In our view it would be too narrow a construction of section 75 to hold that the Commissioner, and this Tribunal, are restricted to considering only that report. We consider that the phrase opens up and makes admissible any relevant medical reports obtained either by the applicant or by the respondent having a bearing on the issues to be decided under section 75. All the medical reports and the oral medical evidence before us fall into that category, notwithstanding that they were made or given after the decision under review. To hold otherwise would, in our view, unduly hamper all parties in resolving the essential issues. The function of this Tribunal is to embark upon a fresh review of these issues. Anything of a relevant nature that goes before the conclusion of that review may be taken into account, notwithstanding the fact that it may occur after the initial decision.

12. The section itself is, in a way, circuitous. We are to have regard to the question whether the health of the person has become restored so as to enable him to perform certain duties. To some extent the duties will be dictated by the state of health of the applicant. Unless both issues are therefore taken together, it would be impossible to give content and meaning to the question before us. It is not possible in our view to look at both parts of the section independently. It is not as if the restoration decision founds jurisdiction to consider separately what are suitable duties. The health of the applicant must be considered as a factor in determining those duties.

13. The meaning of "restore" in section 75 has been considered by this Tribunal on a number of occasions. In Re Stoddart and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 915, 21 December 1982) the Tribunal said at paragraph 12:

"12. The word "restored" in the context of s.75(1) of

the Act conveys the sense of "brought back to a healthy

or vigorous state" (see definition of "restore" -

meaning 4(c) in The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary).

It is certainly capable, in its ordinary meaning, of

embracing circumstances in which there has been an

improvement in a person's state of health from a former

condition of invalidity. It is also capable, in our

view, of embracing cases where the underlying disability

conditions may not have changed significantly, but, on

fuller medical investigation, the nature and extent of

those disabling conditions is seen to be less than was

earlier thought to be the case. For s.75(1) to apply,

it is not necessary that the pensioner's health must be

found to have been brought back to a perfect state.

What is required is that it must be seen to be

sufficiently restored to enable the pensioner to perform

the duties said to be suitable to be performed by him."

This definition was approved and followed in Re Armstrong and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 1943, 8 January 1985).

In Re Grover and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 1154, 22 July 1983) the Tribunal adverted to the possibility of a partial restoration in these terms:

"'Restored' in that provision does not mean completely

restored to the same state of health as before. It is a

qualified state of restoration. The person's health is

to be "so restored as to enable him to perform duties of

a kind .... suitable to be performed by him" And what

is so suitable is to be determined having regard amongst

other things to "the duties performed by him immediately

before his retirement". The question here is whether

the applicant's state of health has been restored to

that extent.

We do not agree, as counsel for the applicant suggested,

that for the section to apply the applicant must be so

restored to health as to be able to resume work so

closely identifiable with her former work as to be "of a

similar nature to her pre-accident work."

14. In order to consider whether there has been a restoration within these terms it is, in our view, necessary to consider both the starting point and the finishing point. Restoration implies a comparison between the state of health of the applicant at the time at which he became entitled to invalidity pension, and the time at which the s.74 or other medical examination is carried out. In that sense, restoration is a synonym for improvement. It will be necessary, therefore, to examine what medical evidence is available to show the applicant's health in 1977 and on the various occasions where medical evidence has become relevant between then and 1986. If there has been a restoration, it should be possible to trace this path of improvement.

15. In deciding what are duties of a kind that are suitable to be performed by the applicant, we consider that we should have regard, not only to this medical evidence, but to all other circumstances having a bearing on the capacity to carry out duties of any kind. Non-medical factors have been taken into account by this Tribunal in other cases for that purpose. In Re Grover (supra) the Tribunal took into account whether suitable duties had to be located in Perth or whether suitable duties should be settled upon in general terms without regard to geographical considerations. Similarly in Re Stoddart (supra) the Tribunal came to the view that suitable duties should be limited to those that could be carried out in the United Kingdom.

16. In Re Rees and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 1216, 19 August 1983), the Tribunal held that it was relevant to consider whether or not an applicant would suffer a recurrence if he were re-employed. It also held that the fact that the applicant had only limited work experience for the 10 years since his retirement was a proper factor for consideration. Both these considerations, as will appear, are relevant in the present case.

17. By way of contrast it was held in Re Hendy and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 1264, 13 October 1983) that the therapeutic value to the applicant of a return to work was not an appropriate consideration in deciding whether there were suitable duties for him. This was a decision of a Tribunal presided over by the President, Davies J. With respect we adopt the view that the section is not philanthropic in its purpose and should not be so construed. The evidence of Dr Lawrence, to which reference will be made later, is especially relevant here. Whatever may be appropriate, suitable or desirable treatment for a person who finds himself in the applicant's position is a matter more for that person's medical adviser than the Commissioner. The scheme of the Act, one of a number of Acts that regulate the conditions of employment of public servants, is to ensure a fair and just administration of a superannuation system as between all contributors. It is not intended to be used for the purpose of medically supporting any pensioner.

Manner of Proof

18. Evidence, both of medical opinion and of facts covering the period from 1977 to 1986 was received in a variety of ways.

19. To the extent that the factual evidence was necessary to support a medical diagnosis or prognosis, we considered that such facts should be proved. However, as was pointed out in Paric v. John Holland Constructions, [1985] HCA 58; 59 ALJR 844 at 846, it is not necessary to prove such facts with complete precision. All of the facts to which reference will be made were proved to our reasonable satisfaction through oral and documentary evidence.

20. In 1977, and for some time thereafter, Dr McDonald had been the applicant's treating psychiatrist. He has since died. We considered it important to receive his evidence and for this purpose were referred to previous transcripts in which he had given sworn evidence and previous transcripts in which had his opinions relating to the applicant at that particular time. This evidence was received both on the principle of being sworn evidence given by a deceased witness (Curley v. Duff (1985) 2 NSWLR 716) and on the basis of it consisting to some extent of statements made by the applicant to his then psychiatrist (Gordon v R. 41 ALR 64).

21. In addition to this, a large number of witnesses gave oral evidence of a factual nature. We made every endeavour to ensure that this evidence was direct and excluded hearsay evidence wherever possible. There were, in addition, reports both factual and medical and admissions. The original documents provided by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were considerably expanded at the specific request of the applicant and by later additions by the respondent. There being no objections, we had regard to all these documents as evidence.

22. The applicant himself chose not to give evidence. He was content merely to call Dr Lawrence in chief, and Dr Gupta in reply. We were invited to issue a summons under section 40(1A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act requiring the applicant to submit himself to cross-examination by the respondent. Having regard to the discretion inherent in that section, we considered this an unwise course. Apart from the practical difficulties that could obviously arise, we considered that it was not a good practice to require an applicant to prove his case in any particular way, and in particular to make himself available for cross-examination if he has not given any evidence in chief. The applicant, of course, must demonstrate his entitlement to the relief he seeks. He must show that the decision he puts before us for review is not the correct or preferable one in the circumstances. If he chooses not to give evidence on his own behalf then that may be a matter for comment and may be a matter that goes to the weight of all the other evidence that has been received. That is the applicant's risk. If he chooses to take this risk, then we do not consider the Tribunal should compel him to behave otherwise.

23. A large body of evidence was thus received. In order to give it some structure and make it understandable we have decided to divide it into various categories of behaviour before going to the psychiatric evidence. We are fully conscious that people do not live in categories. What we do in one context is influenced by what we do in others. Our thoughts and feelings are present in all our actions. In the case of personality disorders in particular, there is a constant interplay between emotions and actions in all situations. We have divided the factual evidence into categories only as a convenient way of managing a large number of relevant facts.

History of Employment

24. Since 29 June 1977 when the applicant resigned from the Treasury he has occupied himself in a variety of ways. There have been intermittent episodes of employment throughout the 9 year period, all of which have been marked by a constantly recurring inability to relate to other people, leading to other types of behaviour which will subsequently be examined.

25. In 1978 he made a number of applications for employment and in fact was offered two jobs. He refused both of them thinking that at that stage he would not be able to cope with them.

26. In June 1979 he became a part-time Research Assistant at the Australian National University Centre for Resource and Environment Studies. This lasted only six weeks. He was dismissed after complaints by a member of the staff following a violent incident against a female. The applicant complained of harrassment. This too is an often recurring theme. It will be noted as a characteristic of the applicant's behaviour during these episodes that he continually felt aggrieved and that he believed others continually acted towards him in an unreasonable manner. In 1979 he distributed advertising material for three weeks.

27. In 1979 and 1980 he was employed in the Queensland Public Service for 4 months. Personal clashes again occurred during this period. As he said in giving evidence about this job on a previous occasion: "The problem was that this person was difficult and aggressive and argumentative but the way everyone else in the office saw it, it was my fault and that was the reason why I would have been dismissed if I had not resigned".

28. In 1981 he held four temporary positions with real estate agents in Canberra. Once again interpersonal relationship problems led to termination of his employment. Each position was held for an average of two weeks.

29. From 3 June 1983 to 2 September 1983 he held the position of a temporary Clerical Assistant Grade 1 in the Department of Defence. Although the job ran its course, there were several incidents in the familiar pattern along the way. Finding his Government issued ball point pen on another officer's desk he assumed that it had been stolen and made a number of complaints about this incident, evidence of which was given before this Tribunal. The importance of the incident was blown out of proportion. A trivial event led to major staff repercussions. It is characteristic of the applicant's behaviour, however, that this occurred.

30. From 8 September 1983 to 23 September 1983 he was a temporary Clerk Class 1 with the Department of Transport. He was again involved in an act of physical aggression with other employees. This led to an investigation of misconduct. He resigned, however, prior to the conclusion of the investigation as he came to the view that he would not receive a fair hearing. Impulsive acts born of a sense of grievance recur constantly in his behaviour.

31. He then took up a position of volunteer tutor in English at the Migrant Centre of the Department of Immigration and Ethnic Affairs. This position was terminated due to a conflict he had with staff. The conflict, typically, involved violent language and abuse and apparently irrational behaviour.

32. From 21 May 1984 to 27 June 1984 he held an appointment as a temporary Clerk Class 1 in the Department of Primary Industry. Perhaps because of the temporary nature of the employment and its short duration, the job ran its course.

33. From 1 November 1984 he was employed as a temporary Clerical Assistant Grade 1 in the Corporate Affairs Commission. After further inter-personal difficulties he resigned after eight days.

34. From 15 April 1985 to 27 June 1985 he was a temporary Clerk Class 4 in the Department of Housing and Construction. Quarrels and difficulties with other staff led to his being moved directly to be supervised by the Senior Executive Officer.

35. In July 1985 he was refused promotion, notwithstanding an appeal to the Grievance & Appeal Bureau and a further appeal to the Merit Protection and Review Agency.

36. In April 1985 he lodged an application for the position of Senior Applied Economist with the Industries Assistance Commission. The decision not to interview him led to a series of complaints from him concerning the interview selection process. These culminated in approaches to the Ombudsman.

37. Again in October 1985 various job applications for Clerical Administrative Class 8 and Class 9 were unsuccessful. There followed a large number of complaints concerning the interviewing process for these positions. He was not selected for interview in September 1985 for a number of Classes 5, 6 and 7 positions with the Industries Assistance Commission for which he had applied. These unsuccessful applications preceded the present attempt to rejoin the Public Service through the mechanism of sections 74 and 75 of the Act.

38. We have been unable to discern any pattern of improvement in behaviour in the work place which might lead one to the inference that his personality disorder had been ameliorated. On the contrary the repetition of familiar behaviour traits consistently to be observed leads one to the conclusion that these are facts that would be helpful to a psychiatrist asked to give an opinion on their significance. Associated with all of these jobs there has been a continuing thread of violence in deed and language, and of aggression which we will now consider.

53. Once again we are unable to detect any reduction in the level of physical and verbal violence that have accompanied the actions of the applicant over the years. If anything, there may have been an increase in their intensity in the last few years. This sad leitmotiv continues undiminished.

54. When we come to examine the criteria that help to distinguish the mental condition of paranoid personality disorder, excessive litigation will be shown to be one of the hallmarks. The applicant has a long history of conduct of this nature which we will now examine.

55. The applicant has brought six previous applications in this Tribunal against the Commissioner for Superannuation. At the time of the hearing of this, the seventh, the result of the sixth was not then known.

He has made innumerable applications under the Freedom of Information Act. It may well be understood that all of these actions were taken as a result of grievances which he held and in which he considered he was being unfairly treated. By no means were all these incidents related to his employment or to his efforts to regain entry to the public service. The history of his academic career will illustrate a consistent behavioural pattern outside that field.

By this time his conduct within and outside the University had led to a decision by the appropriate officers of that body that he would be a disruptive influence and ought not to be allowed into the formal structure of classes. This refusal, of course, was followed by the usual farrago of applications, complaints and appeals.

74. All these facts have been reviewed as it is necessary to understand the propositions that were put to the psychiatrists who gave evidence before us. It must be these facts and any other relevant facts discerned from clinical examinations that must base any psychiatric opinion upon which reliance can be placed.

Psychiatric Evidence

75. There was some question raised whether a condition of paranoid personality disorder ought to be viewed as an illness. In Dr Gupta's view (to which reference will be made later), the applicant simply had a difficult personality. This did not mean that he was ill. We cannot accept this view of the condition in view of the proponderence of evidence. "Paranoid personality disorder" is not merely a nosological convenience. It has been judicially and medically recognised as a discrete condition with characteristic symptoms that set it apart not only from other personality disorders but also from other recognised mental illnesses.

76. In McMullen v. Commissioner for Superannuation 61 ALR 189 at 207, the Federal Court held that a paranoid personality is a "mental condition" without it necessarily being a medical condition, that is a condition requiring medical treatment.

77. The clinical existence of such a condition is recognised in two authoritative publications.

78. The first is a document entitled "Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders" third edition, published by the American Psychiatric Association. Notwithstanding Dr Gupta's brave assertion that this "lacks in established credibility among all psychiatrists", we consider that coming as it does from an authoritative source and having been endorsed by other witnesses who gave evidence before us, some help is to be derived from the description it gives of features that are associated with paranoid personality disorders. These are as follows:

"Common associated features include resentment and

anger, which may lead to violence. Grandiosity and

ideas or delusions of reference are common. Often there

is social isolation, seclusiveness, or eccentricities of

behaviour. Suspiciousness, either generalised or

focussed on certain individuals, is common. Letter

writing, complaining about various injustices and

instigation of legal action are frequent".

79. If the credibility of that document is questioned, there can be no such doubt concerning the "Manual of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Injuries and Causes of Death" based on the recommendations of the 9th Revision Conference 1975 and adopted by the 29th World Health Assembly. This document is published by the World Health Organisation in Geneva and clearly has international recognition.

80. That document defines a paranoid personality disorder at paragraph 301.0 in the following terms:

"Personality disorder in which there is excessive

sensitiveness to setbacks or to what are taken to be

humiliations and rebuffs, a tendency to distort

experience by misconstruing the neutral or friendly

actions of others as hostile or contemptuous, and a

combative and tenacious sense of personal rights. There

may be a proneness to jealousy or excessive self

importance. Such persons may feel helplessly humiliated

and put upon; others, likewise excessively sensitive,

are aggressive and insistent. In all cases there is

excessive self reference".

81. One of the bases upon which the applicant became entitled to invalidity pension was that he was suffering from paranoid personality disorder. In view of the evidence to which we will now refer, there can be no doubt that this was certainly the case in 1977. The only question is whether his mental health has been restored to the appropriate degree since then.

82. His own doctor thought that he had not improved by 31 August 1981. In a report of that date Dr McDonald said:

"This man suffers from a severe paranoid personality

disorder which under stress, becomes frankly psychotic.

Because the paranoid constantly feels persecuted by

others his own ideas are determined by others and he is

always uncertain of what are and are not his own

thoughts, especially when he is not in a psychotic

phase."

83. In earlier proceedings Dr McDonald gave his view on the difference between a personality disease and a neurosis in these words:

"The difference between a personality disease and a

neurosis is a very clear distinction. The neurosis is

felt by the person as a foreign body in their

personality. It is something that does not belong to

them, and they are well motivated to get rid of the

neurosis. A personality disorder is completely ego

symptomatic and felt to be a part of oneself, and a

person with a personality disease never complains about

symptoms. They complain about other peoples' reactions

to them, whether they are paranoids, whether they are

... It is so much a part of them that it is not

experienced as a disease. What they complain of are

other peoples' reactions. One student that I had

thought he had a perfect right to come and put his feet

up on my desk and was quite shocked when I told him to

get his feet of my desk. That is the sort of thing.

They believe they have a right to be what they are, and

they do not suffer from what they are; they suffer from

other people's reactions to them."

84. In 1980 Dr McDonald believed that the future looked bleak for the applicant. He gave evidence in previous proceedings in these words:

"I belive the prognosis for this man is extremely grim

and I can see the time in the near future when he will

end up in an institution. I may be wrong and I hope I

am but knowing the history of this disorder, I think

that is going to be the eventual outcome for him, so I

would say definitely he is unemployable."

85. He based his view in part upon the nature of personality disorders as such. He said:

"What one must realise about a personality disorder is

that its foundations are laid early in life. That is,

its reaction patterns are established. It may lay

dormant and paranoid conditions usually do not emerge

until the 30's. The highest peak for paranoia is around

about the late 30's, early 40's. The thing about it - I

think I mentioned this last time - is that the paranoid

person is usually a highly intelligent one, well above

average as a rule, not the paranoid schizophrenic but

the paranoid, and it is usually their intelligence which

protects them over the earlier decades of their lives.

Then an imbalance between the emotional forces and the

intellectual capacity arises and the paranoia becomes

frankly obvious."

86. Dr McDonald as we noted earlier has since died. Happily his fear that the applicant may finish up in an institution has not so far been realised. However, at the age of 41, the applicant has certainly come to a difficult stage in his life.

87. To prove his case, he relied heavily on the evidence of Dr Lawrence who has been seeing him for psychotherapy treatment over the past 12 months. Dr Lawrence was not sure if he could be described as the applicant's treating psychiatrist. He could not, of course, give any first hand opinions concerning the applicant's condition between 1977 and 1985. He did, however, believe that he had detected an improvement.

88. His evidence tended to wander off into irrelevant areas at frequent intervals but the thrust of it generally was that in his view it would be beneficial for the applicant to be re-employed in the public service, provided that both the applicant and his supervisors changed their attitude and provided that circumstances could be so arranged that the applicant was not put under stress. Two extracts from his report dated 11 September 1986 will illustrate his views:

"In my view were Mr given the opportunity to commence

work at the level of his competence, this in itself

would be a morale booster of great moment and could go a

long way towards Mr establishing a far more

satisfactory process in dealing with others within the

workforce". ........

"Were Mr to be given an established position

commensurate with his skills he would no longer be in

the disadvantaged position, and it may well be that with

a new positive attitude on both sides, this long running

conflict could be resolved in a positive direction, and

Mr be given the opportunity to contribute to the

workforce in a positive fashion".

89. In cross-examination he would go no further than saying that re-employment could "go some way towards changing this behaviour pattern". When asked what degree of certainty he was postulating he said: "I don't know really. It is hard to say."

90. All the facts reviewed above were put to him. Obviously they had not formed part of any full history he had obtained from the applicant. He was inclined to question the rights and wrongs of each of the incidents referred to and was not impressed at all by their multiplicity. Nevertheless, he agreed that relationships would be fragile in the event of re-employment:

"Well, I think the whole exercise would be fraught with

a great deal of apprehension on both sides and a I think

the little scenario that Dr Saboisky talked about in his

report would have to apply initially at least."

91. Dr Saboisky had been asked to examine the applicant by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Bromhead. The examination took place after an unpromising beginning when the applicant quarrelled with Dr Saboisky's receptionist demanding a fresh appointment in what appeared to be a most unreasonable manner.

92. In his view there had been some improvement since 1977 in that he found no current evidence that the applicant was suffering from anxiety depression. He considered, however, that the applicant's paranoid personality traits remained in evidence. He said in his report dated 11 September 1985:

"By definition personality disorders are enduring ways

of thinking, perceiving and behaving in the world and as

such do not change completely during developments.

There is evidence, of course, that some people with

personality disorders of the magnitude which Mr has,

do work successfully but cause problems within the

workplace. I do not believe, given his recent history,

that he is free of projective hostility and therefore

would, from time to time, cause significant and

unnecessary alterations within the workplace."

93. All psychiatrists agreed that the applicant is a highly intelligent man. He has a third class Honours Degree in Economics from Sussex University. His intelligence has never been in issue. Dr Saboisky reported:

"In respect of suitable duties I believe that he has

intelligence to be employed at the level where he worked

previously and there is ample evidence during my

interview with him that he has maintained a very keen

interest in current economic matters, especially in

respect of Government policies. I am obviously not

fully acquainted with the procedures of employment in

the public service but there is no evidence that there

has been any intellectual decline since his retirement.

If he were to be employed within the public service, I

think the question of who should employ him is something

which should be negotiated between Mr and his

employers. I imagine a job which did not involve any

contact with the general public where his work role is

clearly defined and where the supporting hierarchy was

clearly demarcated."

94. This is the "scenario" referred to by Dr Lawrence and was to recur in further evidence as being a possible solution to the applicant's dilemma. In the absence of any such special arrangements, Dr Saboisky had no doubt what would happen if the applicant were re-engaged. He gave this evidence:

"Given your diagnosis doctor, what would your

professional opinion be of Mr 's circumstances should he

be returned to the public service? --- Well, I think the

best predictor of the future is the past. In most

psychiatric conditions, especially personality

disorders, because they are generally life long and some

people tend to soften up a bit in their 40s and 50s,

they conceal their difficulties a bit better, but

generally speaking, I suspect he will have ongoing

problems.

These ongoing problems, would you - what form would they

take? ---- Precisely the form they have taken in the

past. I think once people are about 25 they have got a

fairly stable repertoire of behavioural attitudes and

beliefs and they do not change very much after that."

............................................................................

Yes. If Mr were to go back to work what sort of

conditions or constraints would you see as being

necessary in order for him to be able to function

adequately at a Class 9 level? --- I suppose a similar

sort of set-up that they have at the Ombudsman's office.

There would be one individual who would deal with him in

conflicts. I would anticipate that there would be

periodic conflict over biros, over photostating, over

who should do what, etcetera, etcetera. And there would

need to be some sort of mechanism to resolve it rapidly

rather than getting involved in letters to other senior

members of departments. Otherwise I think there would

be quite a lot of havoc in any department he is in.

Senior officers would be spending an inordinate amount

of time dealing with what amounts to fairly trivial

problems in the first instance. I think that the

records suggest that he could have problems dealing with

members of the public and he could have problems dealing

with certain types of superiors."

95. Dr Gupta examined the applicant on one occasion in December 1985. As a result of that clinical examination and as a result of reading three medical and one non-medical reports, he was prepared to give evidence that in his opinion the applicant is not, and never was ill. He considered that the American description of paranoid personality disorders lacked all credibility. Indeed he gave the impression that he attached no credibility to any medical manual emanating from that part of the world. He saw no reason why the applicant should not immediately return to the public service. In his view the Government was simply using psychiatry in order to "chuck him out". He was not interested to know of any behavioural facts that related to the applicant's non-work situations. The Government was simply indulging in "Russian tactics".

96. He received a limited history from the applicant at the December 1985 examination. Counsel for the respondent attempted to put to him all the facts reviewed above to see whether or not he wished to modify his opinion in the light of those facts. He was given a document to read which set out the facts in question. However, he gave evidence that he read four pages then discarded the document as he considered that these facts had no bearing on the evidence he was called upon to give.

97. His evidence was so extreme and argumentative that we found it of little, if any, assistance.

Suitable Duties

98. The applicant himself considered that the only duties which were suitable for him and to which he wished to return were those of a Class 9 officer. He held that rank at the time of his resignation. He considered that his intelligence and training went beyond anything of a lower rank.

99. In order to examine what these duties were and whether it would be possible to carry them out with the constraints and within the framework suggested by Dr Saboisky, Mr Capp was called to give evidence.

100. He is the Director, Re-deployment and Retirement, Staffing Branch of the Public Service Board. He gave the following evidence:

"Focusing on the Class 9 level, I wish to put a few

facts to you which I am asking you to accept and then I

will ask an opinion of you. If a person were found fit

to return to work but subject to the restriction that

they were prone to interpersonal relation difficulties,

such that they were not to be a supervisor, that they

were to have very well defined role with their

supervisor and were to have minimal or no contact with

support staff or with the public, would such a Class 9

position exist in the Public Service? --- There would

be, in my view, very few in that the vast majority of

Class 9's are senior middle management level positions,

third in the 10 grade structure. They have generally

large numbers of subordinate staff and by their very

nature involve difficult work which involves

interpersonal skills in achieving the required output,

involves communications not only at level and with

supervisors but also with subordinate and ancillary

staff.

Could you conceive a Class 9 position where there was no

subordinates and where a person is not to have contact

with the public and was not to be put in a position of

having to liaise with other staff in co-operative

ventures and was to have minimal contact with typing and

other registry and other support staff, could you

conceive that that situation would exist? --- I would

have great difficulty believing that such a position

could exist.

And if I superimposed the additional assumption on that,

that he was not to have a female supervisor, what then?

--- Well, by the very transitory nature of staffing in

the public service that is a condition that could not be

maintained with any certainty over a period of time

because people come and go and the supervisor might very

well change quite quickly and there would be nothing to

prevent, indeed quite the opposite, a female from

obtaining such a position if it was vacant.

Now I put to you the same facts as you have got but a

few additional facts now. If the conditions of

employment were that the people that he did have contact

with, support staff, fellow employees and supervisors

were to be asked to exercise special sensitivity in

dealing with the man, with the person, is that situation

one that you can envisage? --- Superimposed over the

other conditions, no, I cannot. Looked at separately

from the other conditions I think I would go back to

saying that the transitory nature of staffing is such

that it would be a very difficult condition to fulfil

over any period of time.

The skills that a Class 9 needs or is presumed to have

in clerical work, can you just in general form outline

what they would be? --- Well, in the broadest sense

very well developed managerial skills at the middle

management level. Again both in experience and through

middle management training on the way, good

communication skills, oral and written. In most

situations highly developed interpersonal skills,

ability to get on with people at all levels in the

organisation and, of course, it involves clients from

outside the organisation that becomes of overriding

importance. I think that is the major point."

101. From this evidence it is quite clear that the imposition of special conditions would be quite impractical. It is true that section 75 does not give the applicant a vested right to employment in the event of a favourable finding. All the Commissioner is bound to do is to report to the appropriate authority. He is in effect saying: "See what you can do for this man". We would point out, in passing, that in our opinion the Commissioner is limited in his power to the public service. Notwithstanding the wording of section 75 which obliges the Commissioner to "inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate" it can hardly be said that this would entitle him to inform, say, a Canberra estate agent "with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner". This is because the Act is part of the legislative regulation of rights and obligations of public servants. Suitable duties must be found only within the structure of the public service. Counsel for the applicant suggested that it would be appropriate for this Tribunal to add conditions of isolation and particular reporting mechanisms to any favourable recommendation. It would then be up to the Public Service Board to see whether any such employment was available. He suggested, by way of example, that the position of lighthouse keeper in the remote southern coast of Australia could be examined. But we see three difficulties in following the course of action advocated by the applicant's counsel.

102. Firstly there is the evidence of Mr Capp that virtually no such position exists. Secondly, we have the evidence of predictions of experts as to the likely conduct of the applicant in virtual isolation. Thirdly, we have the applicant's own evidence of what in fact occurred previously in what might have been thought to be ideal surroundings. In earlier proceedings before this Tribunal the applicant gave this evidence:

"I think you obtained a position at the Australian

National University in about June or July 1979? - Yes,

that is correct. It was a part-time Research Assistant

position there which was in the Centre for Resources and

Environmental Studies. It was an ideal job for me. I

was able to work virtually my own hours. I was working

on my own and was not required to interact with anyone,

and it was reasonably well paid.

The Tribunal; when was this? - This was in June 1979.

Mr Rowles; For how long did you hold that position? - I

held the position I think for about 6 weeks.

What was the reason you ceased working with the

University? - I was dismissed.

What was the reason for that? - I was dismissed for

pushing a female employee in an argument."

103. The past, as is often the case, is the best guide to the future. Even in flawless conditions the applicant will still need to interact with somebody. He has demonstrated over and over again that he is simply unable to deal with the ordinary tracasseries of life. This inability repeatedly has led, and in our opinion will continue to lead, to suspicion then abrasion then verbal abuse then quarrels then violence. There is no reason to consider that personality traits amounting to a disorder now firmly entrenched at the age of 41 will change in the foreseeable future.

104. We have concluded, therefore, that although the applicant's health has been partially restored by the remission of anxiety depression, his paranoid personality disorder has not improved since 1977 and the effects and manifestations of it are such that there are no duties of a kind in the Australian Public Service suitable to be performed by him. In fact it seems to us that because of the disciplined structure of the service, the need to manage and relate to people in the service, the requirement to harmonise with people outside the service - in short the people intensity of the service - the Australian Public Service as a whole is egregiously unsuitable for the applicant. In saying this we have not overlooked his intellectual qualifications or the experience he received in the service prior to 1977. Viewing the review of his health and the examination of suitable duties together (as we must to make sense of the section), we are satisfied that the decision under review should be affirmed.
