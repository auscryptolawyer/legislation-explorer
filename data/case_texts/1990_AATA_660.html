QT89/35-43 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 660; (1990) 21 ATR 3645; 90 ATC 555 (11 September 1990)

Last Updated: 2 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) NO. QT89/35-43

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: QT89/35-43

APPLICANTS



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT



AAT Decision No 6179



Before: Dr P. Gerber, Deputy President



Date: 11 September 1990



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. I do not need to reserve my decision in this case. I find the facts clear and beyond doubt and the law simple in its application to those facts.



2. Mr and Mrs Macbeth are the applicants before me. They, in partnership with two other couples (both called Banquo), acquired three different allotments of undeveloped land in Troilus and Cressida between 1972 and 1973. For purposes of simplicity, I do not propose to go into the various permutations and combinations by which these various interests in the land changed hands. It is sufficient for my purposes if I find that a common purpose prevailed throughout, and nothing turns on the fact that - post 1973 - the ownership of the land was gradually transferred from the Banquos to the Macbeths or one or other of them.



3. For present purposes, I am satisfied that when Mr Macbeth - and I refer only to him since, by agreement, the same consequences will prevail in relation to Mrs Macbeth - obtained title in what has been described as Lot 1 in Troilus on 25 August 1972 and lots 4 and 5 Troilus in April 1973 and, subsequently, in the further allotment in Cressida on 26 July 1973, he (and his partners) acquired the said land with the avowed intention of selling all the allotments quickly and at a substantial profit. The reasons that the land - save for Lot 1, which was sold only recently - is still held by them is not relevant to these proceedings. Suffice it that every attempt was made for a quick disposal, alas, due to zoning problems, to no avail.



4. It became evident during the hearing that the Macbeths owned substantial land holdings ("I am a good buyer but a bad seller"). However, he was adament that these transactions were quite separate from his other land transactions, whether for business or for investment purposes, and I have not taken them into account in reaching my decision.



5. Lot 1 was originally owned by the Banquos and the Macbeths acquired a third share between them, the remaining lots were acquired by the parties with funds, which included substantial borrowings from Mr Macbeth's bank, the interest being capitalised. I find it significant that from the time these loans were taken out in 1973 until 1982, no deductions whatever were sought for interest in the returns of Mr and Mrs Macbeth.



6. No very persuasive explanation was forthcoming for this somewhat odd feature if, as was strongly urged upon me by Mr Peden (who ably assisted Mr Macbeth in the presentation of the case), the acquisition of these allotments constituted the carrying on of a business. Whilst it is true that Mr Macbeth had some health problems at or around this time, both his returns and those of his wife, which were professionally prepared (not by Mr Peden I hasten to add) do not reflect the carrying on of a business undertaking. On the other hand, the failure to claim these outgoing as a deduction is consistent with a belief that the acquisition of the subject land was an affair of capital and the profit on sale a non-taxable capital gain. On that view, the interest payments would not be an annual deduction. For the sake of completeness, I should add that the small rentals from these allotments, achieved in these early years, were likewise not returned as income. This was "explained" on the basis that one of the Banquos took the rents as recompense for his allegedly herculean efforts in trying to sell the land.



7. Whether or not the issue of "capital" may have coloured Mr Macbeth's attitude to his returns in the first ten years or so of having acquired this land I am unable to determine with certainty. In the end, I am not persuaded that any such definitive view is required. I find, on the whole, Mr Macbeth did his best to recall the events, some of which now go back almost 20 years.



8. By 1981/82 the issue became crystallised in the returns to the extent that the land acquisitions and the interest payments were now claimed for the first time as deductions. This, in turn, set in train an investigation which resulted in the interest payments being disallowed as deductions.



9. The issue which I have to decide comes to this: was the money borrowed as part and parcel of a business undertaking; that is for the purposes of producing assessable income? Mr Peden submitted throughout the hearing that the acquisition of the land was part of a business undertaking, that the gain constitutes assessable income and the interest payments deductible. He placed heavy reliance on the decision in Travelodge Papua New Guinea Ltd v Chief Collector of Taxes 85 ATC 4432.



10. I am not persuaded, however, that that case is relevant to the facts in this case. In Travelodge, a company engaged in the hotel business borrowed large sums of money to finance the construction of an hotel. The company sought to deduct the capitalised interest, rates etc incurred prior to the hotel earning income as a loss and outgoing under a provision similar to our sec 51(1). After a careful review of the Australian authorities on the point, the judge held (on a provision similar to our section 51(1)) that the outgoings should be characterised as a recurring expenditure incidental and relevant to the production of the company's assessable income and was not so integrally connected with the creation of a capital asset as to amount to a payment of a capital nature. This is a far cry from the instant facts where the profit - if any - would only be generated after the sale of the land, giving rise to very different tax consequences.



11. Once I am satisfied - as indeed I am - that the three allotments were bought with the intention of a quick and profitable sale rather than as a business undertaking, it follows that when such sales take place, the profit or loss, as the case may be, will then be calculated after taking into account all holding charges, which include, of course, any interest payments. In other words, where sec 25A(1) (the old sec 26(a)) applies - as here - the fiscal consequence will be that the interest payments are a cost of computing the taxable gain or loss, as the case may be.



12. What was sought to be done here is to put the cart before the horse in that, year by year from 1982 onwards, these two taxpayers sought to deduct the interest component from their other assessable incomes. On the findings that I have made that is impermissible. In these circumstances, I am not persuaded that anything turns on whether or not the income that is finally generated on the sale is income under ordinary concepts pursuant to sec 25(1) or sec 25A(1).



13. I noted in Case V65 88 ATC 498 - and I repeat it today - that in the current uncertain state of the law in which no one has attempted to define the relationship, if any, between section 25(1) and 25A(1), I do not see it as part of the function of an administrative tribunal to undertake a task which distinguished judges have refused to attempt in circumstances where such distinction is not relevant, since I am satisfied that, whatever the parameters of the two sections, the profit generated in this case falls into both.



14. The remaining issue deals with the disallowance of a licence fee for a pistol.



15. Mr Macbeth gave evidence that when in practice as a solicitor in Dunsinane, his office was frequently holding large sums of money, and that there had been a number of break-ins in the neighborhood. In consequence, he had been advised by the local police to obtain a pistol. This evidence was not challenged, nor that Mr Macbeth owned a pistol (albeit without bullets). In the circumstances, I find the connection between the outgoing and the derivation of his income as a solicitor sufficiently proximate to make the deduction allowable under section 51(1).



16. That leaves the issues of the sec 226 penalties. The returns from 1972 onwards until such time as the matter became crystalized in 1982 and even thereafter, were such as to ignore and, later, to give a quite misleading picture of the land acquisition activities. Indeed, Mr Macbeth persisted almost until the day of the hearing in the assertion that he and his wife were engaged in subdivisions and/or business activities. The objections, year by year, repeat what was previously said in a letter to the Commissioner, to the effect that the interest was necessarily incurred in carrying on the business of property development. In those circumstances, I do not find the imposition of the penalty imposed in this case sufficiently excessive to call for intervention by me.



17. Save for the claimed deduction for the pistol licence, which I allow, the decisions on the objections must otherwise be upheld.



This is to certify that this and the preceding 4 pages are a true and correct copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein.
