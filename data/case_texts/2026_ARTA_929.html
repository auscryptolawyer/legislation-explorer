Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. This proceeding involves two applications made to the Tribunal by Mr Mahmoud Eltamimy (‘the Applicant’) for review of objection decisions relating to income tax assessments (or amended assessments) issued to him by the Commissioner of Taxation (‘the Respondent’) for the 2020 to 2022 years of income (‘the Relevant Years’).
  2. The dispute arose in the context of the Applicant’s claims for deductions (or greater amounts of deductions) for expenses he said that he incurred in relation to a rental property and a motor vehicle. The Respondent had also questioned the way in which the Applicant had treated certain insurance payouts.
  3. The objection decisions also cover administrative penalties imposed by the Respondent on the Applicant. The Applicant says that no penalties should be applied to him.

Background and key events

  1. The Tribunal understands that the following matters are not in dispute[1]:
    (a) The Applicant was born in Egypt and emigrated to Australia as an adult.

    (b) The Applicant was employed as a Vice President by a biotechnology company located in suburban Melbourne during the Relevant Years.

    (c) The Applicant and his former wife bought a property, as joint tenants, in the suburb of Meadow Heights (‘the Meadow Heights Property’) in May 2004. They subsequently divorced, with the title to the Meadow Heights Property being transferred to the Applicant as sole proprietor on 2 November 2020.[2]

    (d) From 2005 to 2021 the Applicant declared ‘rental income’ from the Meadow Heights Property in his income tax returns, as well as claiming deductions for expenses associated with that property.

    (e) The Applicant owned or leased one or more motor vehicles in the Relevant Years.

Respondent’s assessment and review processes

  1. An understanding of the assessment and review processes undertaken by the Respondent provides some clarity about the issues that were ultimately in dispute before the Tribunal:
    (a) The Applicant lodged his income tax return for the 2020 year of income in August 2020, with the Respondent issuing an assessment for that year in September 2020.[3]

    (b) The Applicant lodged his income tax return for the 2021 year of income in November 2021, with the Respondent issuing a notice of assessment for that year in that month.[4]

    (c) In February 2022 the Respondent commenced its first audit of the Applicant’s tax affairs, which was in relation to the 2020 year, and which resulted in an amended assessment for that year being issued on 9 June 2022.[5]

    (d) In August 2022 the Applicant lodged an objection (‘the First Objection’) against the amended assessment for the 2020 year.[6]

    (e) On 27 October 2022 the Respondent disallowed the First Objection (‘the First Objection Decision’).[7]

    (f) The Applicant lodged his income tax return for the 2022 year of income in April 2023.[8]

    (g) In May 2023 the Respondent commenced its second audit of the Applicant, which was in relation to the 2021 and 2022 years. This second audit resulted in both an amended assessment for the 2021 year being issued on 22 September 2023 and an assessment for the 2022 year being issued on 25 September 2023.[9]

    (h) On 24 November 2023 the Applicant lodged an objection (‘the Second Objection’) against the amended Assessment for the 2021 year and the assessment for the 2022 year.[10]

    (i) On 1 March 2024 the Respondent disallowed the Second Objection (‘the Second Objection Decision’).[11]

  2. The Applicant was assisted by a registered tax agent in relation to the lodgement of his income tax returns for the 2021 and 2022 years (but not for the 2020 year) and this tax agent was also engaged in relation to the First Objection.
  3. In relation to penalties:
    (a) The Respondent did not impose any penalties in relation to the 2020 year.

    (b) In relation to the 2021 year, the Respondent imposed an administrative penalty on the basis that the Applicant made a ‘false or misleading statement’ to the Respondent. The penalty was calculated at 50% of the tax shortfall amount on the basis that the Applicant was ‘reckless’ when he prepared his income tax return, however, the Respondent also decided that it was appropriate to remit half of that base penalty.[12]

    (c) In relation to the 2022 year, the Respondent imposed an administrative penalty on the basis that the Applicant made a ‘false or misleading statement’ to the Respondent. The penalty was calculated at 50% of the tax shortfall amount on the basis that the Applicant was ‘reckless’ when he prepared his income tax return. The penalty for the 2022 year was increased by a further 20% because the Applicant had been liable for an administrative penalty in a previous year. However, the Respondent also decided that it was appropriate to remit that 20% ‘uplift’ amount and half of the base penalty.[13]

  4. The Respondent also remitted part of the shortfall interest charge (‘SIC’) charged in relation to the 2021 year on the basis that there had been an unreasonable delay on the part of the Respondent of a month during the second audit.
  5. In this decision the Tribunal refers to:
    (a) The amended assessment for the 2020 year, the amended assessment for the 2021 year and the assessment for the 2022 year as the ‘Disputed Assessments’.

    (b) The assessments of administrative penalties for the 2021 and 2022 years as the ‘Penalty Assessments’.

Tribunal processes

  1. Two applications were filed with the Tribunal. The first application was made on 28 October 2022, relating to the First Objection Decision.[14] The second application was made on 4 March 2024, relating to the Second Objection Decision.[15]
  2. In March 2025 the parties sought an extension of time for filing of materials on the basis that the Applicant needed additional time for the purposes of seeking advice and drafting submissions. The Applicant said that the Respondent had raised several new questions which had not previously been discussed during audit, objection or ADR processes, which meant that he would need to gather further evidence and amend the submissions he had previously filed. The Tribunal understands that these new questions generally related to the treatment of the insurance policy payouts.[16] The Tribunal’s Registry refused this extension of time request on the basis that extensions had been given to the parties previously.
  3. The Respondent then wrote to the Tribunal in support of the Applicant’s request for an extension of time, confirming that the Respondent had obtained new information about insurance matters which it only provided to the Applicant in January 2025 and had raised additional ‘factual concerns’ with the Applicant at that time. The Respondent said that it would be appropriate for summonses to be issued to the insurer so that all relevant information was able to be obtained and considered. The Respondent noted that the Applicant was self-represented, the issues in dispute were factually dense and the Applicant had the burden of proof in this matter. The Respondent asked the Tribunal to list a case management directions hearing so that the parties could explain these developments to the Tribunal.[17] The Respondent also provided summons requests and draft forms of summons to the Tribunal on 7 April 2025.
  4. A case management directions hearing was held on 26 May 2025 before this Member, who was subsequently constituted to the substantive hearing of the applications. By that time the Applicant had already obtained some documents from the insurer through directly contacting them[18] and which he provided to the Tribunal. The parties were directed to confer as to the extent to which these documents were responsive to the summons requests previously made by the Respondent.
  5. On 11 June 2025 the parties were directed to file any further or amended submissions and evidence in support of such submissions.
  6. The Respondent was directed to prepare a Hearing Book containing all materials the parties intended to rely upon at the hearing, and this was filed with the Tribunal (with a copy to the Applicant) as directed, a week prior to the hearing.[19]
  7. The matter was heard over three days commencing on 9 February 2026. The Applicant represented himself, and the Respondent was represented by counsel.

OVERVIEW OF ISSUES FOR CONSIDERATION BY TRIBUNAL

  1. There were issues raised and arguments made before the Tribunal which were not set out in the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision, and which apparently developed over the course of the proceeding. The Tribunal understands that the Respondent relied on section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (‘TAA 1953’), in that it was for the Applicant to prove that the Disputed Assessments and Penalty Assessments were excessive (and what amount they should have been) and the Respondent was not necessarily bound by the specifics of how the amounts in those assessments had been determined.

Issues as described during objection processes

  1. In the First Objection Decision the Respondent said that:
    (a) In relation to deductions for work-related car expenses the Applicant had not kept a valid log book[20] and should have used the ‘cents per kilometre’ method for working out his expenses rather than the ‘log book’ method he had used for the 2020 year. However, the Respondent had decided ‘on this occasion’ not to reduce the amount of deductions claimed by the Applicant.

    (b) In relation to deductions for rental expenses, the Applicant had not let the property ‘consistent with normal commercial practices’, having leased the property to a ‘friend’ and the rent charged by the Applicant seemed to be below market value. Further, the rental income returned and deductions claimed were not consistent with the Applicant’s ‘share of ownership.’ Therefore, the Respondent had limited the amount of deductions allowable for the 2020 year to the amount of rent received, relying on the ‘working rule’ adopted by the Respondent in its public taxation ruling IT2167[21] and the decisions of the Supreme Court of Victoria in Commissioner of Taxation v Groser and the Supreme Court of Queensland in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kowal.[22]

  2. In the Second Objection Decision the Respondent said that[23]:
    (a) In relation to deductions for work-related car expenses, the Respondent had ‘reviewed the audit decision’ for the 2020 car expenses and ‘the audit has accepted your log book.’ However, the Respondent also said that at audit the Applicant’s log book[24] was ‘determined as invalid’ due to multiple errors, and the Applicant had not provided other travel records in support of his claims. Further the Respondent did not believe that the Applicant had adequately substantiated certain car related expenses such that he had not demonstrated that these car expenses were ‘necessarily incurred’ in producing his employment income. The Respondent therefore allowed car expenses on the basis of the cents per kilometre method and rejected the Applicant’s claim for additional deductions based on the log book method.

    (b) In relation to deductions for rental expenses, the Respondent again referred to its public guidance in IT2167 which it viewed as applicable to the Applicant even though it accepted that the Applicant had not leased the Meadow Heights Property to a ‘relative’ of his. The Respondent’s view was that the Applicant had let the property ‘for less than normal commercial rates’ and therefore the amount of rental deductions allowed for the 2021 and 2022 years should be limited to the amount of rental income earned. The Respondent also raised a further issue as to whether the Applicant and family members were also using the Meadow Heights Property during this period such that the entirety of the property was not leased out. The Respondent concluded ‘We found that there is insufficient evidence to show whether the purpose of producing assessable income dominant the purpose of private and domestic uses.’[25] There appears to be one or more typographical errors in this sentence but the Tribunal has assumed that the Respondent was either suggesting that the Applicant had mixed income producing and private purposes in relation to the property, or that the predominant use of the property was for the Applicant’s private or domestic purposes.

  3. Following the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision the Tribunal understood that the following matters were in dispute between the parties:
    (a) Whether the Applicant was entitled to deduct a greater amount in each of the Relevant Years for expenses relating to the Meadow Heights Property.

    (b) Whether the Applicant was entitled to use the ‘log book method’ rather than the ‘cents per kilometre’ method to calculate the amount of motor vehicle expenses he may deduct for the 2021 and 2022 years, or was otherwise entitled to deduct a greater amount of motor vehicle expenses in those years.

    (c) Whether administrative penalties should have been imposed for the 2021 and 2022 years, and if so, whether any of those penalties should be further remitted.

Issues as described during Tribunal processes

  1. After the Applicant made his application to the Tribunal a further issue was raised by the Respondent in relation to the Meadow Heights Property, namely whether the Applicant’s approach to the taxation treatment of amounts received under insurance policies over the property was correct. This resulted in the summons requests described above.
  2. Also after the commencement of this proceeding the Respondent further articulated the reasons for its position in relation to the Applicant’s claims for rental expense deductions. Some aspects of these reasons differed to those set out in its objection decisions. This included alternative arguments in support of the Disputed Assessments. Although the Respondent appeared to be contending that there was a basis for denying any deductions in one or more of the Relevant Years, they confirmed to the Tribunal that they were not proposing to disallow the amount of rental expenses previously allowed to the Applicant in the Relevant Years.
  3. The parties also told the Tribunal that the issue of car expenses for the 2022 year was no longer in dispute between the parties.[26] That is, only the quantum of deductions for car expenses for the 2021 year was to be considered by the Tribunal.
  4. The Applicant’s submissions included the contention that he should not be liable for a penalty in relation to the 2020 year. However, the Respondent had not imposed any administrative penalty on the Applicant for that year, and no penalty assessment was issued to the Applicant for that year.

The Applicant’s burden of proof

  1. Subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953 provides that upon a review by the Tribunal of a reviewable objection decision:
    ‘(b) the applicant has the burden of proving:

    (i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment -- that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been...

  2. Each of the First Objection Decision and the Second Objection Decision is a ‘reviewable objection decision’ and the ‘taxation decisions’ to which the objections related are the Disputed Assessments and Penalty Assessments.[27] This means that the Applicant must prove, on the balance of probabilities[28], both that the Disputed Assessments and Penalty Assessments are ‘excessive or incorrect’ and also what the amount of those assessments should have been.

ISSUE 1: DEDUCTIONS FOR A RENTAL PROPERTY

Relevant law

  1. Paragraph 8-1(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (‘ITAA 1997’) provides that a taxpayer can deduct from their assessable income ‘any loss or outgoing to the extent that ... it is incurred in gaining or producing [their] assessable income.’
  2. Subsection 8-1(2) provides that a taxpayer cannot deduct a loss or outgoing to the extent that it is a loss or outgoing of capital (or of a capital nature) or of a private or domestic nature.[29]
  3. There is a significant body of case law which confirms the principle that rental income derived by a taxpayer, being income derived from the use of property of the taxpayer, will be characterised as assessable income of that taxpayer, and the Tribunal does not understand this proposition to be questioned by either party.
  4. Numerous case authorities have also considered the application of the words ‘to the extent that’ in subsection 8-1(2) and its predecessor provision, including where a loss or outgoing is incurred in circumstances where there may be mixed income producing and private or domestic purposes or factual background.[30]

Positions and contentions of the parties

  1. The Applicant has disclosed in his income tax return for each of the Relevant Years (and in earlier years of income) amounts of assessable income comprising ‘rent’ derived by him in relation to the Meadow Heights Property. The Tribunal understands that the Respondent has not directly challenged the proposition that the Applicant derived rental income in the Relevant Years (although the Respondent contends that such rent may not have reflected prevailing market value rentals).
  2. In each of the Relevant Years the amount of rental expense deductions claimed by the Applicant exceeds the rental income returned, sometimes by a substantial margin. The Respondent has allowed the Applicant deductions for a portion of the outgoings characterised as ‘rental expenses’ by the Applicant in each of the Relevant Years. The Respondent allowed the Applicant deductions for rental expenses up to the amount of the rental income declared by the Applicant in each year, but no more. This approach reflects public guidance of the Respondent in IT2167 which in turn referred to the decisions in Groser and Kowal.[31]
  3. The Respondent subsequently told the Tribunal that it has doubts as to whether the Meadow Heights Property was genuinely tenanted, and also believes that the Applicant may have used that property for his personal purposes during the Relevant Years. This implies that the Respondent does not regard the Applicant as entitled to any deductions for rental expenses under section 8-1.[32] Nevertheless, the Respondent has said that it will not go back and disallow any of the rental expense deductions that have previously been allowed following the audit and objection processes. However, it does not agree that the Applicant should be allowed to claim any further deductions for rental expenses in any of the Relevant Years.
  4. The Applicant submits that he should be entitled to deductions for the full amount of all rental expenses claimed by him in relation to the Meadow Heights Property in each of the Relevant Years.
  5. The Applicant’s position is that throughout each of the Relevant Years he rented the Meadow Heights Property to Mr A, Mr A paid him rent (which was included in the Applicant’s assessable income), the Applicant incurred certain outgoings and expenses during the Relevant Years in relation to the Meadow Heights Property and therefore the Applicant should be entitled to claim the full amount of those outgoings and expenses as deductions in those years. He says this is the case regardless of whether the amount of the outgoings and expenses exceeded the rent he received from Mr A in any of the Relevant Years. He refutes the Respondent’s suggestion that he lived at the Meadow Heights Property himself during any of the Relevant Years, or otherwise used it for personal purposes. He also rejects the Respondent’s contention that any rent he received from Mr A was below market rates.
  6. On an initial reading, the Respondent’s position might appear to contain some internal inconsistencies, however, the Tribunal understands that the Respondent is taking an administrative approach that it will not seek to wind back or disallow previous assessments in a manner that might be adverse to the Applicant, even where it believes previous assessments might have been overly generous to the Applicant. The Tribunal also understands that the Respondent has made submissions on certain issues in the alternative, should the Tribunal not accept its primary submissions.
  7. The Respondent submitted that there was no ‘genuine’ tenancy arrangement, or no evidence satisfactorily demonstrating a tenancy, between the Applicant and Mr A in relation to the Meadow Heights Property in any of the Relevant Years. On that basis, the rental expenses claimed by the Applicant could not be viewed as incurred in the gaining or producing of assessable income by the Applicant. This also presumably raises a question around the characterisation of the amounts returned by the Applicant as ‘rental income’ in the Relevant Years, but that was not identified as a question for the Tribunal in circumstances where the Respondent would accept in the Relevant Years an outcome under which deductions up to the amount of such income were allowed, thus negating the income amount.[33]
  8. The Respondent submitted alternatively that the Applicant was living at or otherwise using the Meadow Heights Property for his personal purposes at some times during the Relevant Years. To the extent that this was the case the Applicant could not deduct expenses he incurred in relation to that property, because they would be outgoings of a private or domestic nature.
  9. Ultimately the Respondent relied on the Applicant having the burden of proof in relation to the Disputed Assessments and said that the Applicant had not provided evidence which demonstrated a genuine tenancy agreement with Mr A on commercial terms. Nor had the Applicant demonstrated that he was not using the Meadow Heights Property himself during the Relevant Years.
  10. The factual and evidentiary matters which the parties identified as relevant to these questions and the Tribunal’s view are as follows.

Documentation of existence and terms of tenancy

  1. The Applicant told the Tribunal that sensitive personal issues involving Mr A meant that Mr A’s residence at the Meadow Heights Property was a confidential matter.[34] It is not necessary for the Tribunal to name Mr A in its written reasons for decision and the Tribunal will only refer to such information as is necessary to explain its reasoning.
  2. The Applicant acknowledged that there was another address where Mr A’s family resided and which might be regarded as his official residence, or the place where the wider community believed he resided. However, due to the personal matters, the Applicant told the Tribunal that Mr A physically lived at the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years.
  3. Copies of a ‘Residential Tenancy Agreement’ for each of the periods 18 August 2019 to 17 August 2020[35], 18 August 2018 to 17 August 2020[36], 18 September 2020 to 17 August 2021[37] and 18 September 2021 to 17 August 2022[38] were provided to the Tribunal. These documents were prepared on a standard form provided by Consumer Affairs Victoria for the purposes of section 26 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1997 (Vic). The first three of these documents provided for a bond of $3000[39] and a periodic rental of $200[40] but with a cash payment in advance of $10,000 or $10,400 for one year of rent. The fourth document provided for a bond of $3000 and a periodic rental of $300 with a cash payment in advance of $15,000 for one year of rent. The latter two agreements contained ‘additional terms’ referring to the tenant being required to grant access to tradespeople as required and repairs or renovations to be undertaken.
  4. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the reason for the two apparently overlapping tenancy agreements was that initially the Applicant and Mr A wanted a longer-term lease with guaranteed income for a specified period, however the Applicant’s accountant raised concerns about this and a one-year lease was instead agreed. The Applicant’s accountant mistakenly sent a copy of both documents to the Respondent during the audit process.[41] The Applicant explained the apparent gaps in the tenancy agreements in August/September 2020 and August/September 2021 as being simple errors, with his accountant holding his copy of the contract and him signing when they caught up. He said that Mr A’s tenancy was ongoing during the Relevant Years.[42]
  5. The Applicant said that the bond and rental amounts were paid by Mr A to the Applicant in cash. The Applicant said that when he first rented the property to Mr A he contacted the Respondent to ask if it was permissible to deal in cash and had been told that it was, but that he should keep an invoice or receipt.[43] The Applicant’s bank statements showed electronic transfers of varying amounts from Mr A to the Applicant, but these were apparently for items other than rent.[44] No other documentation corroborating the payment of rent was provided. The Applicant said that Mr A would have been able to confirm the reasons for the use of cash in relation to the rental arrangement, but again this arose from the personal matters which required his residence at the Meadow Heights Property to remain a private matter.
  6. The Tribunal has some concerns about the reliability of the standard form Residential Tenancy Agreements as evidencing the existence and terms of the claimed tenancy at the Meadow Heights Property. Leaving aside matters of the legality or validity of these documents under the Victorian residential tenancies legislation, in the absence of any corroborative information other than the Applicant’s oral evidence the Tribunal is not entirely comfortable that the ‘tenancy agreements’ were entered into on the dates or for the periods set out in those documents. There are numerous discrepancies and inconsistencies within the documents. The accountant was not called to give evidence to confirm how these documents were prepared or any advice he provided in relation to the tenancy. As discussed below Mr A also did not appear.
  7. The Tribunal places limited weight on these documents as evidencing the existence of a residential tenancy at the Meadow Heights Property during any of the Relevant Years. In the absence of any material corroborating the payment of the rental amounts, the Tribunal has difficultly placing any weight on the tenancy documents as evidencing at least the manner and timing of rental payments. The Tribunal cannot place weight on the tenancy documents in relation to demonstrating what repairs or renovations might have been done, or when such work might have been undertaken.

Relationship between the Applicant and Mr A

  1. At some point during the audit or objection processes of the Respondent, and possibly due to a comment made by the Applicant’s then tax agent, there was a suggestion that the Applicant and Mr A were related. The Applicant said that this was incorrect and he was concerned that the Respondent might have placed unnecessary weight on this factual error in forming its views around the issues of genuine tenancy, whether rent was paid at market rates and whether the Applicant also used the Meadow Heights Property.
  2. The Applicant explained to the Tribunal that he and Mr A were members of a relatively small community of Islamic Egyptians in Melbourne. Even in the absence of family ties or pre-existing friendships, the members of that community were aware of each other and sought to support and assist fellow community members. He had been introduced to Mr A on that basis and was willing to rent the Meadow Heights Property to Mr A when he became aware of Mr A’s personal difficulties. Renting directly to Mr A saved the Applicant from having to retain and pay commission to a real estate agent and also provided some flexibility as to the terms of their arrangement.
  3. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence that Mr A is not related to the Applicant and that they are not particularly close friends. The Tribunal understood that the Respondent no longer contended that the two might be related, or even that they were close friends. The Tribunal understands the point being made by the Respondent was that the Applicant and Mr A were not complete strangers or at arm’s length but had some sort of prior acquaintance, and this was a relevant consideration in relation to the question of whether rent paid by Mr A was at market value.[45] The Applicant acknowledged that his arrangement with Mr A included some matters that might not ordinarily be found in a residential tenancy agreement, such as the Applicant’s ongoing use of the Meadow Heights Property as a mailing and delivery address.
  4. Even accepting the Applicant’s evidence about the nature of his acquaintance with Mr A, the Tribunal believes the fact that they had some personal (community) connection and the absence of any evidence demonstrating an arm’s length arrangement in relation to the use of the Meadow Heights Property, is a matter that is relevant to the Tribunal’s consideration of whether any tenancy arrangement was on commercial terms.

Witness statement of Mr A

  1. The Applicant filed a copy of a one page letter, dated 25 June 2023 and addressed ‘to whom it may concern’ and apparently signed by Mr A. The letter refers to Mr A having lived at the Meadow Heights Property for more than three years and that Mr A’s decision to lease the property directly from the Applicant was prompted by personal issues that required a change of residence for Mr A. The letter also said that the Meadow Heights Property, while liveable, had undergone significant repairs and maintenance during Mr A’s tenancy, which improved the condition of the house such that the rent had been increased by 50% at some point. The letter contained a mobile phone number and email address to contact Mr A should further information be required.[46]
  2. In its hearing certificate the Respondent identified Mr A as a witness that it wished to cross examine. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he had spoken to Mr A and informed him of the Respondent’s request. At the commencement of the hearing it became apparent that there had been confusion as to who was responsible for confirming Mr A’s participation and no arrangements had been made for Mr A to attend at the Tribunal.[47] As the hearing was scheduled across three days, the Tribunal indicated that if contact could be made with Mr A, the Tribunal would be willing to accommodate his participation at any time prior to oral closing submissions. The Tribunal then tried to contact Mr A on the email contact given in the letter to provide him with details of the hearing. The Applicant also tried contacting Mr A on the mobile number in the letter.[48] Further efforts were made by the Tribunal and the Applicant to contact Mr A by email and phone into the second day of the hearing.[49] By the third day of the hearing no contact had been made with Mr A and it became apparent that he would not be giving evidence at the hearing. The Member told that parties that she would view Mr A’s letter as part of the factual matrix in the proceeding, in that she would not immediately disregard the letter but would need to consider what evidentiary weight it carried in the context of other evidence provided to the Tribunal.[50] The Tribunal accepts that as a non-legally trained person the Applicant may not have been aware that he could ask for a witness to be issued a summons by the Tribunal and that he may have assumed based on the Respondent’s hearing certificate that either the Respondent or the Tribunal would make the necessary arrangements for Mr A to attend.
  3. The Respondent submitted that in the absence of Mr A being available for cross-examination on his letter the Tribunal should reject the contents of that letter as having any evidentiary value. The Respondent said that the Tribunal could not even be sure that Mr A wrote that letter or made those statements.
  4. As indicated above, the Tribunal is not willing to reject Mr A’s letter altogether, but acknowledges that it must approach the letter with some caution from an evidentiary perspective. The letter has been described as a witness statement, but it was not in the form of an affidavit or statutory declaration. The Respondent’s counsel noted that the signature for Mr A on the letter appeared to be computer generated. Use of an electronic signature is not necessarily a matter for concern, however, the Tribunal acknowledges that in the absence of hearing from Mr A directly or seeing other corroborative material it cannot be completely certain of the provenance of the letter.
  5. The Tribunal also notes that the statements made in the letter address only limited aspects of the claimed tenancy of Mr A. The letter states that Mr A had been a tenant at the Meadow Heights Property ‘for the past of more than three years.’ As the letter was dated June 2023, it does not indicate how long before June 2020 Mr A’s tenancy commenced, noting that the Relevant Years commenced on 1 July 2019. It does not confirm when or if he signed the Residential Tenancies Agreements. Although there is a reference to repairs and renovations at the property which supported a rental increase there is no detail on what work was undertaken or when it occurred. It is not clear whether Mr A is stating that he lived at the property full time or throughout the Relevant Years. There is no indication of any other terms of the tenancy, such as the Applicant’s use of the property as a mailing address.

Other evidence of Mr A residing at the Meadow Heights Property

  1. The Applicant told the Tribunal that Mr A physically lived at the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years. A photograph said to be of Mr A and one of his children at a birthday party held for the child at the Meadow Heights Property was included in the documents filed with the Tribunal. It was not clear when this photograph was taken but the Applicant told the Tribunal that he asked Mr A to provide some photographs showing the condition of the property prior to renovations.[51]
  2. The Tribunal was provided with copies of invoices from various utility companies which supplied water, gas and electricity to the Meadow Heights Property at times during the Relevant Years. Many of these invoices are in the name of the Applicant, or the Applicant and his former father-in-law,[52] although there are a handful of electricity and gas bills addressed to Mr A dated from May 2022 to November 2022.[53] Those dates occurred after the Respondent commenced its audit in February 2022, and the majority of this period is after the Relevant Years. The Tribunal does not place significant weight on the electricity and gas accounts from 2022 as evidence of Mr A’s claimed tenancy across (and before) the Relevant Years.
  3. There does not appear to be any further evidence of Mr A living at the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years. The Tribunal appreciates that this may reflect Mr A’s need for privacy, however, from the Applicant’s perspective this creates a significant challenge in proving the existence of a genuine tenancy arrangement.

Statements in insurance and other third party documents

  1. The documentary evidence filed with the Tribunal included material such as copies of a home and contents insurance policy taken out by the Applicant in relation to the Meadow Heights Property, a Victorian driver’s licence and car registration information and loan documents. The Respondent drew the Tribunal’s attention to statements in those materials which it believed raised questions about whether the Meadow Heights Property was genuinely an investment property in the hands of the Applicant and suggested that he used the property for his personal purposes. The Respondent also suggested that statements made in those documents cast doubt on the credibility of the Applicant’s evidence more generally.
  2. In relation to the home and contents policy, the ‘package type’ of the policy was stated to be ‘residential.’ The description of the insured address noted that the Applicant had told the insurer that the property was ‘Owner: Primary Residence.’ The insured party was required to tell the insurer if ‘you are going to lease all or part of your insured address to someone else’ and notify the insurer immediately if any of the information relating to the insured address was inaccurate, incomplete or had changed.[54] The policy contained an ‘Important Notices’ section which expressly stated that an insured person was required to answer the insurer’s questions fully and accurately, and legal consequences followed for failure to do so.
  3. The Applicant said that he and his former wife had taken out the home and contents policy around 2013 at the time of purchasing the Meadow Heights Property and he had held the same policy ever since. He did not give any thought to reviewing or changing anything in it subsequently. The Applicant said that Mr A had been present when assessors from the insurance company came to the property to assess claims, and he had identified Mr A as the tenant.[55]
  4. Even making allowance for the Applicant inadvertently failing to review his insurance arrangements, if the Meadow Heights Property was let to Mr A during and before the Relevant Years the information in the home and contents policy was incorrect and this continued for over an extended period of some years. The Tribunal observes that the Applicant did apparently request a change to the home and contents policy in March 2021 however he did not correct or update any information relating to the use of the property, including any lease arrangements and/or the fact that it was not his primary residence.[56] The Tribunal was not provided with any evidence to suggest that the Applicant had informed the insurer that he had been leasing the property, and not living there himself, for a number of years. In this regard the Tribunal does not accept that the Applicant could rely on a claims assessor having seen Mr A at the property, even if he had been identified as a tenant or resident when the assessor visited (and no further evidence was provided relating to this visit).
  5. The Respondent also referred to the Applicant’s motor vehicle insurance policy recording his address as the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years[57], and continuing to provide that address to VicRoads for the purposes of his driver’s licence for some years after he said that he had ceased to live there. He used his driver’s licence for other proof of identity purposes.[58]
  6. The Applicant and his former wife had obtained a home loan with Commonwealth Bank when they originally bought the Meadow Heights Property. The Applicant had to refinance that loan following the marriage breakdown. He said that he retained a mortgage broker to assist him, and he passed all of the relevant documents and information to the broker who then dealt with potential financiers, including Westpac who lent to the Applicant in March 2021 under its Flexi First Option Home Loan product, and provided further refinancing to the Applicant around June 2023.[59] The Applicant also told the Tribunal that these refinancings occurred during a particularly difficult time in his life.[60]
  7. The first Westpac refinancing in 2021 was a ‘home loan’ product rather than an investment loan. Although the documentation relating to the second Westpac refinancing is dated after the Relevant Years, the Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s submission that this material creates significant doubt around the Applicant’s contentions that the Meadow Heights Property was tenanted and that he did not live there or otherwise use it for personal purposes. The loan summary from 2023 clearly refers to the Meadow Heights Property being the Applicant’s home address (as well as his mailing address), that the property was owner occupied and that he would not be renting or boarding after the settlement of the loan.
  8. The Applicant explained that he had left matters relating to the documentation of the Westpac refinancing with the mortgage broker. His area of professional expertise was with pharmaceutical development and not in finance. He did not deal directly with Westpac but signed the documents provided to him by the broker. He also said that his tax returns would have been part of the documentation provided to Westpac and the tax returns disclosed the rental status of the property.[61] Even if the Tribunal were to accept that the Applicant was unaware of the terms of the Westpac loans and the representations made by him or on his behalf in the documentation, the Tribunal believes that he should have taken steps to understand the legal documentation he had signed. The documentation is drafted in ‘plain English’ and matters such as his home address and rental status are basic factual matters rather than complex issues of finance or law. The Tribunal was unable to confirm whether the Applicant’s tax returns or other tax related information was provided to Westpac. Even if it had been, it was a matter for the Applicant to confirm the accuracy of the loan documentation.
  9. The Tribunal believes that the evidence relating to the terms of the home and contents insurance policy, the motor vehicle insurance policy and home loan terms is inconsistent with the Applicant’s position and the Tribunal does not believe that the explanations given by the Applicant adequately address these concerns.

The Applicant’s residential arrangements during the Relevant Years

  1. The question of where the Applicant was living during each of the Relevant Years is relevant in the context of the Respondent’s contention that the Applicant may have been using the Meadow Heights Property for private or domestic purposes during this period, and possibly living there himself.
  2. Although the Applicant’s property settlement following his divorce was finalised in November 2020, the Applicant told the Tribunal that he had been separated and living apart from his former wife some time prior to that. He said that from the time he was unable to live with his family he lived at various addresses in at least five suburbs of Melbourne, in shared accommodation and sometimes for short periods of time.[62]
  3. The Applicant provided a copy of a residential tenancy agreement with him as the tenant for a property in Carlton covering the 12 months from 1 July 2018 to 30 June 2019, together with a copy of utility accounts addressed to him at that location during that period. For reasons that are not clear the Applicant stated in the residential tenancy agreement that his address, at least at the time of signing the agreement, was the Meadow Heights Property. The Applicant also provided copies of tollway statements sent to him at the Carlton address after June 2019.[63] The Applicant told the Tribunal that he actually left the Carlton address before the original lease end date and moved in with a friend at an address in Westmeadows from July 2018, where he stayed for approximately eight months before moving in with another friend at an address in Roxburgh Park where he remained for around three months before moving again to an address in Doncaster.[64]
  4. The Applicant had indicated in correspondence to the Respondent that he lived at the Doncaster address for approximately three years, from 1 August 2019 to June 2022, which is the majority of the Relevant Years. He said that his contribution to costs was to pay for food and groceries.[65] He also said that during this period he spent a lot of his time at the home of his then new partner, who had a home in another suburb.[66]
  5. The Respondent’s counsel referred to the bank statements of the Applicant which had been issued during the Relevant Years.[67] There was no indication of any purchases made by the Applicant in Doncaster during this time, with frequent purchases of fast food and groceries being made in suburbs in north-western Melbourne. The Applicant explained that as a Muslim he ate halal food, which was difficult to find in Doncaster. Halal foods, including halal options at selected fast food chains, were readily available in the north-western suburbs and he would visit his children and former partner in those locations, making it convenient to shop there.[68] The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s explanation that religious considerations meant that he would travel to the north-western suburbs for food purchases. Although the Respondent’s counsel suggested that there were no purchases shown in the Applicant’s bank statements as having been made in Doncaster,[69] the Tribunal has reviewed those bank statements and found at least 17 purchases from a supermarket, restaurants, cafes and other retail outlets in Doncaster between January and April 2020 and then again between February and October 2021. Nevertheless, the Tribunal agrees that it is unusual for such a limited number of purchases to be made in the suburb where the Applicant said he was living for almost three years.
  6. Other than the tenancy agreement for the Carlton address (which the Applicant said he terminated early, and which pre-dated the Relevant Years) the Applicant was not able to provide copies of any documentation such as residential rental agreements or correspondence confirming his subletting or share accommodation arrangements in any of the other addresses he said that he lived in during the Relevant Years. He did not provide evidence from third party witnesses who might confirm him living, even briefly, at any of these houses.[70]
  7. On this basis, although the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant’s marital breakdown meant that he was not able to live with his former wife and children during this period, it is not possible for the Tribunal to make any positive findings that the Applicant lived at any of the Carlton, Westmeadows, Roxburgh Park or Doncaster properties or at the home of his former partner, during the Relevant Years.

The Applicant’s use of the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years

  1. The Respondent also contended that the Applicant had lived at or otherwise used the Meadow Heights Property for his personal purposes during the Relevant Years. This was relevant to the question of whether Mr A was a genuine tenant at that property (such that the Applicant’s expenses for the Meadow Heights Property were incurred in gaining or producing rental income) as well as whether the Applicant’s property expenses were (to some extent) of a private or domestic nature.
  2. The Applicant said that when he and his former wife originally bought the Meadow Heights Property it was intended that they live there. However, about a year later his wife ceased working and as they were unable to afford to keep the property on one salary, the family moved back in with his former wife’s parents, and they intended to use the Meadow Heights Property as an investment. They started having marital difficulties but could not dispose of the Meadow Heights Property while family law and property settlement matters were in dispute. Around this time and prior to Mr A becoming the tenant at the Meadow Heights Property, the property had been rented to another person known to the Applicant, through the Egyptian community.[71] The Applicant obtained full title to the property upon finalisation of the family law matters.
  3. The Applicant readily acknowledged that he had given the Meadow Heights Property as his mailing address for some purposes, with the permission of Mr A, during the Relevant Years. He explained that as he was moving from house to house and sharing a home with other people, the Meadow Heights Property was a secure and stable mailing address. This was particularly important because from around 2014 he had been seeking to bring his mother to Australia and the immigration agent had advised that he needed a ‘constant address’ for correspondence from the government, with the immigration process potentially taking up to ten years.[72]
  4. The Applicant noted that mail, including electoral information, continued to be sent to his former wife at the Meadow Heights Property for some time after she had ceased to live there, and even after she had returned to live in Egypt. Similarly, mail addressed to his former father-in-law was also received at the Meadow Heights Property. The Applicant said that this indicated mailing addresses were not determinative of where a person was living.
  5. The Applicant also agreed that he had arranged for deliveries of goods he had purchased to be delivered to the Meadow Heights Property. This included items of furniture delivered in March 2022. The Applicant said that he had been travelling interstate at the time and would have collected the furniture on his return. A new mobile phone purchased by the Applicant in around September 2019 was also invoiced to that address. The Applicant said that the telephone was not physically delivered to the address, and that he had collected it from the retailer. The address on the invoice was taken from his driver’s licence which had to be shown for identification when collecting it. The Respondent queried the plausibility of these explanations.[73]
  6. The Applicant acknowledged that he had left furniture belonging to him at the Meadow Heights Property. He said that upon the breakdown of their marriage, his former wife did not want to keep furniture associated with their former home and told him to take it. As Mr A had not fully furnished the Meadow Heights Property, the Applicant asked Mr A if he could leave the furniture at the property and Mr A agreed to this. Mr A was able to use some of this furniture, although the Applicant said it was clear between them that the property was not being leased on a furnished basis.[74] The Tribunal is willing to accept the Applicant’s explanation of this matter and does not believe that the fact of some of his furniture being at the Meadow Heights Property is of itself particularly probative of him living there.[75]
  7. The Applicant was asked about his visits to the Meadow Heights Property during the time of Mr A’s tenancy, particularly those of a social nature. The Applicant said that he was invited to gatherings hosted by Mr A during the Relevant Years, but this was as a guest of Mr A along with other members of the Egyptian community. The Applicant was also asked about a photograph apparently taken at the Meadow Heights Property which showed his son’s name marked into a wall panel. He told the Tribunal that in accordance with Egyptian Islamic cultural practices a celebration is held for the first son born in a family when the child reaches six years of age. For that reason the Applicant and his family returned to the Meadow Heights Property in 2011 to hold this celebration, before renting the property out from 2012.[76] The Tribunal does not believe the limited evidence of these matters assists the Tribunal in determining the level or manner of use by the Applicant of the Meadow Heights Property.
  8. The Tribunal has referred above to invoices from utility companies which supplied water, gas and electricity to the Meadow Heights Property during the Relevant Years being in the name of the Applicant, or the Applicant and his former father-in-law, except for a handful of accounts issued to Mr A between May and November 2022. The Tribunal believes this evidence tends slightly in favour of finding that the Applicant was either living at, or using the Meadow Heights Property for personal or domestic purposes, during at least some periods within the Relevant Years.

Was the rent a commercial or market value amount?

  1. At the outset the Tribunal observes that very limited reliable evidence was provided by either party as to what a proper ‘market value’ rental might be, or what a ‘commercial’ rental agreement might otherwise look like. This has created a challenge for the Tribunal in making findings on this issue. The role of the Tribunal does not include undertaking valuation exercises.
  2. In the First Objection Decision the Respondent referred to its public ruling IT2167 and the decisions in Groser and Kowal, and affirmed the audit finding that the Meadow Heights Property was not let in a manner ‘consistent with normal commercial practices.’ This appears to encompass a finding that the rent was not ‘within market value’ because the Respondent referred to an argument put by the Applicant that Mr A did not have access to all of the house.
  3. The Second Objection Decision contained a sub-heading ‘Rental deductions – limitation for rent below market valuation’ but did not elaborate on the basis for the Respondent’s view, stating that:
    49. In our initial phone conversations with photos of the conditions of your property that was leased – JPG and PDF are acceptable.

    50. Include real estate research amount for rent in that area – either a screenshot, printed materials that you saved and/or diary of your records.

  4. The Tribunal is not clear what these sentences mean and whether there are typographical errors or missing content. The audit decision which was considered by the Second Objection Decision is in a short form format and says no more than ‘We apportioned your other rental deduction claims to income earned as the property was rented at a non-commercial rate.’ The Tribunal accepts that there were likely discussions between the Respondent and the Applicant (or his then tax agent) during the audit and objection processes which may have elaborated on these findings. The Tribunal also appreciates that its role in conducting a merits review is not to investigate or critique the processes by which the Respondent reached the Disputed Assessments. However, an understanding of the reasons why the Respondent determined that there was a non-market rental such that apportionment of deductions was required appears relevant to the ability of the Applicant to discharge his burden of proof, and to the ability of the Tribunal to assess the merits of the Applicant’s evidence and arguments.
  5. When the Respondent filed its Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) in April 2023[77], there was a reference to the Applicant having been asked to provide evidence about what parts of the Meadow Heights Property were available to the tenant and how the rental was determined. The Respondent also drew attention to the fact that the amount of rental charged for the property had not changed in 14 years. The SFIC otherwise repeated the Respondent’s contentions that any tenancy arrangement was not consistent with commercial practices and that the Applicant was required to prove that the terms of letting were commercial.
  6. Around this time, and after the second audit had been commenced, the Respondent obtained property data and an Estimated Rental Amount Report provided by CoreLogic dated 23 April 2023.[78] CoreLogic makes clear that its reports are based on publicly available statistical data, and do not represent a professional appraisal of the property concerned. The Respondent did not suggest that the CoreLogic report was a formal valuation or that CoreLogic had physically inspected the property.
  7. When the Respondent filed its second SFIC filed in July 2024[79] it referred to commercial practices ‘in the area’ and the rent appearing ‘on its face, far lower than what the average commercial rate would be to rent an ordinary property of the size and location of the Property’ presumably reflecting the CoreLogic information.
  8. The CoreLogic report suggested a rental range for the Meadow Heights Property of between $454 and $614 per week, expressing ‘medium confidence’ in this figure. The report identified four comparable properties, three of which were in the suburb of Meadow Heights and one of which was in the same street as the Meadow Heights Property. The weekly rentals for these properties varied between $390 and $1,100, with the property in the same street as the Meadow Heights Property attracting the lowest rent and the property in a neighbouring suburb attracting the highest.
  9. There was significant debate during the hearing about the reliability of the CoreLogic report. The Applicant said that the report, having been obtained in 2023, was based on information outside the Relevant Years. The Respondent said that the date of the CoreLogic report was not that far off the Relevant Years. The Applicant said that the properties in the report were not truly comparable to the Meadow Heights Property, with the neighbouring suburb being regarded as a more elite location, skewing the average or range referred to in the report. The Respondent said that the Applicant was a pharmacist and not a qualified valuer and in any event the Applicant bore the burden of proof, so the Respondent was not required to put forward evidence. There was discussion as to the size of land and condition of the comparable properties.[80]
  10. After listening to the parties engage extensively over the CoreLogic report during the hearing, and having regard to the CoreLogic materials in the context of the other evidence, the Tribunal makes the following observations. The Tribunal agrees that the case authority in Groser and Kowal set out principles that are relevant to this proceeding, and thereafter it is a question of how those principles apply to the facts of this proceeding. The Respondent is correct that the Applicant has the burden of proof and therefore is required to satisfy the Tribunal that any rent paid by Mr A was commercial or within market value. However, the Tribunal believes that it was relevant to hear any concerns raised by the Applicant in relation to the CoreLogic report, not only as a matter of procedural fairness but because it is relevant to the Applicant understanding what he might need to do to discharge his burden of proof.
  11. The CoreLogic report is not a market valuation or other professional appraisal, nor was it put forward as such. Although the Tribunal does not place significant weight on the CoreLogic report as probative of the precise market value rental for the Meadow Heights Property, the Tribunal notes that if the Applicant is correct that the non-Meadow Heights Property was not a suitable comparable property, then it is correct that the inclusion of that property might skew the rental average or range proposed in that report. However, even if the non-Meadow Heights Property is excluded, there is still a significant difference between the rent identified by CoreLogic for the cheapest of the comparable properties ($390 weekly, for the property in the same street as the Applicant’s property) and the $200 weekly which the Applicant said that he received from Mr A.
  12. The Tribunal accepts as a general proposition that a rent that is lower than that charged for other properties in a location may be explicable by reference to a tenant not having access to the full extent or amenities of the premises, or the property being in a much poorer condition than other rental properties in the same location. The Applicant described to the Tribunal some of the areas of the Meadow Heights Property which had required repairs, maintenance or even full renovation, such as replacement of the kitchen and bathroom. He said that he had been unable to do significant work prior to his divorce because his former wife did not want to undertake major renovations.[81] Copies of invoices for works said to have been undertaken at the property during the Relevant Years were filed with the Tribunal. The Applicant also provided several photographs he said were taken of various areas of the Meadow Heights Property, including ‘before and after’ photographs of kitchen, bathroom and lounge areas. The photographs also included images described as ‘damage or during work’ relating to the flooring, roof and pool area.[82] Some works may have been undertaken in response to storm or other damage, discussed further below in relation to the treatment of insurance payouts.
  13. The Tribunal has already found that there is a lack of evidence about key elements of Mr A’s association with the Meadow Heights Property. This also creates a challenge in demonstrating how much of the property he used or was able to use, and how his enjoyment of the property might have been affected by repair or maintenance issues (if he was a tenant during the Relevant Years). Mr A’s letter included some general statements that ‘significant’ repairs and renovations had been done on the property during his tenancy such that he was happy to pay additional rent when those works were completed. Even if the invoices and photographs are accepted as evidence of work having been undertaken, the Tribunal does not believe that this sufficiently demonstrates the basis upon which $200 per week was a commercial amount of rent. The Tribunal does not have sufficient information upon which it could make a finding as to how much of the property was not available to Mr A in any of the Relevant Years or the extent to which his enjoyment of the property might be affected by maintenance concerns. In fact, Mr A’s letter says that the Meadow Heights Property was old but ‘liveable’ and he had access to all of the property.

The Applicant’s business model

  1. The Applicant prepared a summary of what he described as his ‘business model for the Meadow Heights Property as an investment property and took the Tribunal through this model at the hearing.[83] The Tribunal has also considered this business model in the context of whether the Applicant has demonstrated a commercial basis for the claimed tenancy of Mr A. The Applicant said that even if the rent he charged Mr A was low, this return from the Meadow Heights Property was better than other alternatives.[84]
  2. The Applicant’s business model compared his potential returns in each of the Relevant Years (and the 2023 year) had he used an agent to rent the Meadow Heights Property as opposed to dealing directly in cash with Mr A. Having the full year of rent paid in cash in advance reduced the risk of the delays in payment of rent or the tenant vacating without paying the full year. The model assumed that the capital works he needed to undertake on the property could not have been undertaken with a tenant other than Mr A, and these capital works also resulted in an increase in the value of the property which would not otherwise have arisen. The Applicant also said that the location of the Meadow Heights Property made it appropriate for redevelopment from a single dwelling to townhouses.[85]
  3. The Respondent asked the Applicant when he expected his business model to be cash positive, as the deductions he had claimed for expenses relating to the Meadow Heights Property were greater than the rental income received, often by a substantial margin, in every income year from 2005 to 2022. The Applicant referred to his disagreement with his former wife as the reason why he was unable to commence significant works until after their divorce.[86] The Applicant also described an iterative process by which works to improve the condition of the property would increase the amount of rent he could charge, which in turn would increase his borrowing capacity in relation to the property. The Applicant said that such borrowings might fund a redevelopment into a multi dwelling property, which would further increase rental return. He viewed this as potentially achieving two goals, in terms of yield and value.[87] The Applicant referred to capital growth as one advantage or outcome.[88]
  4. Some matters underpinning the Applicant’s business model can be accepted as relatively uncontroversial. For example, renting a property directly to a tenant would save the cost of agent fees and commissions. Improvement of the condition of a property may be a factor leading to increased rental return. An increase in the value of a property may enhance the owner’s ability to borrow against the equity in the property. The Tribunal views the Applicant’s business model as an explanation or illustration of his reasoning and submissions relating to the Meadow Heights Property as an investment property. In this regard, the Tribunal found the model and the Applicant’s explanation of assistance in understanding his position and giving context to some of the issues in dispute with the Respondent. However, the Tribunal cannot place significant reliance on the business model of itself as evidence of the commerciality of the rental amounts for the Meadow Heights Property in the Relevant Years or the factual issues sitting behind it, such as Mr A’s claimed tenancy and the nature and cost of works undertaken at the property.
  5. To the extent that the Applicant identified capital growth as a purpose for undertaking works, this raises a separate question as to the extent to which the costs incurred were deductible, if an outgoing might be directed even partly to a matter of a capital nature.

Conclusions in relation to rental expenses

  1. Subsection 8-1(1) of the ITAA 1997 expressly provides that losses or outgoings will be deductible ‘to the extent that’ they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, and subsection 8-1(2) provides that losses or outgoings will not be deductible ‘to the extent that’ they are a loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature. Therefore, it is open to the Respondent, where a taxpayer has returned an amount of assessable income, to form a view that there is a nexus between only a portion of deductions claimed and the amount of income. The task for the Applicant is to prove that the assessments are ‘excessive’ (or incorrect) and what they should be, and the role of the Tribunal includes determining whether he has discharged that onus. It is not the role of the Tribunal to make further inquiries into how the Respondent reached those assessments. It is also not necessary for the Tribunal to reconcile the various alternative arguments of the Respondent, including the contention that the Meadow Heights Property might never have been a genuine rental property of the Applicant. The Tribunal’s view is that while appreciating the difficulties that the Applicant has faced in obtaining evidence to support his case, the Applicant has not discharged that burden of proof in that he has not produced sufficient evidence for the Tribunal to be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that he is entitled to further deductions beyond those already allowed. In particular the Tribunal is not satisfied as to the existence or terms of Mr A’s claimed tenancy, the nature of the Applicant’s use of the property and the commerciality of the amount of rent said to have been paid by Mr A.
  2. The Tribunal therefore does not believe it has a basis for varying or setting aside the First Objection Decision or Second Objection Decision insofar as they relate to the Disputed Assessments and the treatment of the Meadow Heights Property.

ISSUE 2: INSURANCE PAYMENTS

  1. The Applicant held home and contents insurance in relation to the Meadow Heights Property and during the Relevant Years received insurance payments totalling more than $220,000. The Applicant said that he also incurred costs in undertaking certain repairs and maintenance that followed the insured events. The Respondent queried the manner in which the Applicant had treated these amounts in his income tax returns for the Relevant Years, and in particular whether the insurance payouts could be income of the Applicant.
  2. The Respondent viewed this issue as arising only in circumstances where the Tribunal agreed with the Applicant’s position that the Meadow Heights Property was genuinely tenanted.[89] The Tribunal understands that this is because insurance payouts received by the Applicant in relation to the Meadow Heights Property would not be regarded as assessable income, and the Applicant would not be entitled to deductions for expenses relating to that property where it was not a ‘rental property’ of the Applicant. In that case the Meadow Heights Property would most likely be characterised as a capital asset of the Applicant and/or the receipts and outgoing would be of a private or domestic nature.
  3. The treatment of amounts paid under the insurance policy was an issue that was not covered in either of the First Objection Decision or Second Objection Decision, or apparently in the audits that led up to those objection decisions. The issue was apparently raised for the first time after the Applicant lodged his application with the Tribunal. The Tribunal understands that although insurance payouts were not expressly included as assessable income in the Disputed Assessments as issued, the Respondent relied on the Applicant having the burden of proving that his assessments in each of Relevant Years were excessive (or incorrect) and what they should have been.
  4. The Respondent referred to a questionnaire provided to the Applicant during the Second Objection process about the status of the Meadow Heights Property as a rental property, in which the Respondent asked whether the Applicant was expecting any form of insurance or other monetary recovery in relation to repairs and maintenance that had been undertaken during 2021 and 2022. The Applicant replied ‘No’ with an explanation that wear and tear was not covered by insurance policies.[90] During the hearing there was debate between the Respondent’s counsel and the Applicant as to whether the Applicant had not properly disclosed the insurance payouts during the objection process. The Applicant said that he had provided a copy of the policy to the Respondent at audit.[91]
  5. Four insurance payouts were identified by the Applicant:[92]
    (a) In November 2019 a storm caused damage to the building and contents at the Meadow Heights Property. The Applicant received three payments from his insurer in January and February 2020 in relation to this incident.

    (b) In January 2021 a motor burnout caused damage to the building and contents at the property. The Applicant received three payments from his insurer in February 2021 in relation to this incident.

    (c) In June 2021 there was a theft from the property. He said that Mr A had arranged a dinner for several members of the Egyptian community, including the Applicant, and while they were at dinner the Applicant’s car and the car of another attendee were stolen. The Applicant had left a number of personal items in his car including jewellery and computer equipment. He said that he was told to claim this on the home insurance policy rather than his car insurance because the car was parked within the boundary of the Meadow Heights Property.[93] The Applicant received a payment from his insurer in August 2021 under his home contents insurance.

    (d) In October 2021 another storm caused damage to the building and contents at the property. The Applicant received eight payments from his insurer in February and March 2022 in relation to this incident. Some of these amounts were described by the insurer as referable to appliances, furniture and other chattels in the house. The payments also included an amount of approximately $24,000 for ‘alternative accommodation’ which the Applicant said he passed to Mr A to cover Mr A’s housing costs while the Meadow Heights Property was unavailable. He said that during this period Mr A continued to pay rent to him.

  6. The Applicant told the Tribunal that these insurance payouts were used only to fund repairs to the property, [94] except for the amount received for ‘alternative accommodation.’
  7. The Applicant said that his tax agent had incorrectly advised him that the insurance receipts were not income, but as he did not claim any deductions for the corresponding repair costs there was no shortfall of tax payable in any of the Relevant Years. He had since prepared an updated tax calculation which he had filed with the Tribunal.[95] He said that insurance payments received as compensation for personal items were not taxable (that is, not income).
  8. The Applicant provided to the Tribunal invoices relating to the repairs and a declaration from a pool contractor about repairs at the property. Some of the photographs included with his written submissions, which he said were taken at the property, were labelled ‘damage or during work.’
  9. In relation to the payment for alternative accommodation, the Respondent queried the basis upon which the Applicant paid the amount on to Mr A. As discussed above, the insurance policy was not a ‘landlord’ policy. The Respondent also queried whether there was any evidence of the Applicant having paid this amount on to Mr A. The Applicant was able to identify some smaller payments to Mr A in his bank statements but could not demonstrate what they were for, nor where or how the remainder of the alternative accommodation payment was made available to Mr A.[96]
  10. The Respondent said that based on the limited documentation provided by the Applicant, it was not possible for the Tribunal to be satisfied on matters such as what work was undertaken at the Meadow Heights Property and the reason for such work, the nexus between insurance payouts and expenses claimed, and whether work undertaken at the Meadow Heights Property should be characterised as (deductible) repairs and maintenance or (non-deductible) capital improvements. A further issue was whether any insurance payouts might instead relate to a ‘depreciating asset’ such that the capital allowances rules of Division 40 of the ITAA 1997 applied.
  11. Other than the documentation from the insurance company that confirms the payments were made to the Applicant (and which the Applicant does not dispute) there is only limited evidentiary material that assists the Tribunal in forming a view on the character of the insurance payouts and the work undertaken. The Applicant provided invoiced issued by a concreting company, one dated August 2020 and the other November 2020 which appear to relate to the same work (repair of three showers).[97] This does not indicate whether the work was a repair in response to the storm damage in November 2019 or a renovation or capital improvement. The Applicant also provided a receipt and a statutory declaration from a tradesman who said he had worked on the repairs to and retiling of the swimming pool at the property.[98] This receipt had to be reissued after the Applicant’s car was stolen as the original receipt had been in the car, and the tradesman said in his declaration that he had not been able to locate the original invoice book so had prepared the invoice from memory and was not sure about the date of the work.
  12. At a technical level, this means that if the Applicant had been required to prove his position as it related to the treatment of the insurance payouts, he would not have discharged his burden of proof. The Tribunal has previously found that he has not satisfied the Tribunal as to the existence of a tenancy arrangement at the Meadow Heights Property. On that basis the issue of the treatment of the insurance payouts would not arise because the payouts would not be assessable income to him and he could not claim deductions for the repairs and maintenance funded by the payouts. That situation apparently reflects the position he took in his income tax returns and does not need to be disturbed.
  13. The Tribunal wishes to be clear that its role is only to review the reviewable decisions and the taxation propositions within those decisions. The Tribunal does not propose to make any further comments on the Applicant’s engagement with his insurer in relation to his policy.

ISSUE 3: MOTOR VEHICLE EXPENSES

Relevant law and guidance

  1. Car expenses may be deductible in accordance with the rules contained in Division 28 of the ITAA 1997 to an individual who owns or leases a car, or hires a car under a hire purchase arrangement: sections 28-10, 28-12. A ‘car expense’ is a loss or outgoing to do with a car, a loss or outgoing to do with operating a car or the decline in value of a car: section 28-13.
  2. Division 28 provides two alternative methods for working out the amount of deductions for car expenses:
    (a) The ‘cents per kilometre’ method, which uses a formula under which the number of ‘business kilometres’ travelled by car in an income year is multiplied by a rate of cents per kilometre (as set out in a legislative instrument). This method only applies for the first 5000 kilometres travelled, and no deduction is allowed for further kilometres travelled. Business kilometres are the kilometres travelled in the car by the individual in the course of producing their assessable income or travelling between workplaces. Under this method business kilometres are calculated by making a ‘reasonable estimate.’ Formal substantiation of car expenses is not required under this method: Subdivision 28-C[99].

    (b) The ‘log book’ method under which each car expense is multiplied by a ‘business use percentage’ which in turn is based on the proportion of ‘business kilometres’ travelled compared to total kilometres travelled in the period. Business kilometres under this method are calculated on the basis of a ‘reasonable estimate’ having regard to specified matters including log books, odometer readings and other records. Substantiation of the car expenses is also required: Subdivision 28-F.[100] A log book and odometer records must be kept.[101] Subdivision 28-G contains provisions about how a log book must be kept and Subdivision 28-H contains rules about how odometer records must be kept. Subdivision 28-I contains rules about the retention of log book and odometer records.

  3. A person is permitted to choose one method for all of the car expenses relating to a particular car in an income year. However a different method can be used for the same car in different years, and different methods can be used for different cars owned or leased by the person in the same year. Further, a person can change their choice for a car in an income year: section 28-20. Subsection 28-20(2) gives the example of circumstances where a person had chosen to use the log book method for a car in an income year, but changes to the cents per kilometre method after their original claim is disallowed at audit by the Respondent.
  4. Although the Tribunal was unable to find any document where this was expressly stated, the parties in this matter have proceeded on the basis that the Applicant owned or leased a Volvo car in each of the Relevant Years, which is the vehicle in relation to which expenses were claimed.[102]
  5. The Respondent also appears to accept that the Applicant used this car for purposes related to his employment to some extent or at some times, including travel to attend corporate/board meetings and to visit staff conducting development and testing work at locations other than his employer’s premises (such as universities).[103] In relation to the 2020 year, in the First Objection Decision the Respondent accepted or assumed that the car expenses claimed by the Applicant were incurred by him in gaining or producing his assessable income. In relation to the 2021 and 2022 years, in the Second Objection Decision the Respondent said that the Applicant had not ‘sufficiently demonstrated the nexus that car expenses were necessarily incurred in producing your employment income.’[104]
  6. The Tribunal observes that the phrase ‘necessarily incurred’ is used in section 8-1 of the ITAA 1997 in the context of losses or outgoings ‘necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purposes of gaining or producing’ assessable income.[105] There was no suggestion that the Applicant was carrying on a business. As described above, the relevant test of deductibility for the Applicant is the extent to which he incurred a loss or outgoing ‘in gaining or producing [his] assessable income.’[106]
  7. In any event, the dispute before the Tribunal largely centred on whether the Applicant could use the log book method for working out car expense deductions in the Relevant Years.
  8. In this regard the Respondent said:
    (a) It did not accept any of the log books provided by the Applicant for any of the Relevant Years as being a true representation of travel undertaken. The Respondent identified items which it believed were erroneous in the logbook for the 2020 year, which led it to believe that the log book had not been completed contemporaneously.

    (b) In relation to the 2020 year, the Respondent had reduced the amount of car expenses allowed at audit on the basis that the Applicant had been unable to substantiate certain expenses but had otherwise allowed the use of the logbook method by the Applicant in that year. At objection, the Respondent found that the Applicant had not satisfied the requirements of the log book method and any deduction for that year should have been limited to an amount worked out under the cents per kilometre method. However, the Respondent decided that it would not further reduce the Applicant’s deduction for car expenses in that year. That is, the outcome of the First Objection Decision was that the Applicant could not deduct the car expenses he had claimed where they had not been substantiated, but the Respondent did not reduce his deduction to the amount that would arise under the cents per kilometre method.

    (c) Presumably on that basis, the Respondent would accept that the Applicant was entitled to a further car expense deduction in the 2020 year relating to the decline in value (depreciation) of the car in that year. The additional amount to be deducted should reflect the 73% ‘business percentage’ put forward by the Applicant for that year.

    (d) In relation to the 2021 year, the Respondent viewed the ‘cents per kilometre’ method as being the appropriate method for working out car expenses on the basis that a log book which complied with Subdivision 28-G of the ITAA 1997 had not been kept in relation to that year. In particular, the Respondent contended that the log book documents put forward by the Applicant had not been demonstrated as reliable and it was not possible to determine whether they were contemporaneous with the travel said to be recorded in them. By way of example, the Respondent pointed to certain ‘errors’ it had identified from the face of those documents which it said were indicative of a lack of reliability and it was not possible for the Respondent (or the Tribunal) to be sure as to whether further errors were contained in the documents. The Respondent also told the Tribunal that the log book documents did not distinguish between travel relevant to the Volvo and that involving other vehicles.

    (e) In relation to the 2021 year, in addition to the log books not being accepted as valid, the Applicant had not demonstrated that he had incurred certain car expenses. The Respondent did not believe that certain motor vehicle expenses had been substantiated. The Respondent did not accept invoices that had been provided by the Applicant in relation to claimed car repairs, fuel expenses had not been substantiated and statements relating to freeway tolls referenced multiple car registration number plates.

    (f) In relation to the 2021-year, tollway expenses claimed by the Applicant should be excluded from any deductions worked out under Division 28 because deductions for these items had already been allowed to the Applicant separately as ‘work-related travel expenses.’

    (g) The car expense deductions for the 2022 year were no longer in issue between the parties.[107]

  9. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he:
    (a) Had provided the Respondent with a letter from his employer confirming that he was required to use the car for (predominantly) business purposes, including following organisational changes requiring more travel.[108]

    (b) Kept a log book in each of the Relevant Years that correctly recorded and was a true representation of his business travel. Even if he had made a few errors in the log book for 2020, this was in the context of approximately 1900 entries, which was an error rate of less than 1%.[109] A clear basis had not been provided by the Respondent for its refusal to accept the log books as valid, regardless of what was said in the First Objection Decision.[110]

    (c) Had asked both the Respondent and his accountant if he could keep a log book in an electronic format and they had confirmed that he could, but subsequently the Respondent requested the log book in PDF format and difficulties were experienced in sending it to the Respondent.[111]

    (d) Understood from the Respondent’s online guidance that if he had a log book accepted in one year, he could use the same percentage of business/personal use for the next five years.[112]

    (e) Agreed that car expense deductions for the 2022 year were no longer in contention.[113]

  10. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent that although the basis for the Respondent’s decision not to further reduce the amount of the Applicant’s car expense claims for the 2020 year may not have been fully explained, the First Objection Decision did clearly state that:
    (a) The Respondent did not ‘accept’ the Applicant’s log book for the 2020 year, did not believe that that log book was a ‘true representation’ of the travel undertaken in that year and had formed the view that the Applicant had ‘not kept a valid logbook.’ This indicates that the Respondent did not accept that the Applicant had satisfied the criteria in Subdivision 28-F for applying the log book method.

    (b) The Respondent believed that the Applicant should have only been allowed deductions for car expenses under the cents per kilometre method.

  11. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that regardless of what the First Objection Decision said, one of the Respondent’s audit officers had said to him that, although the audit team had ‘speculations’, they had passed the log book for 2020 as valid. The Tribunal explained to the Applicant that it was the First Objection Decision which was subject to review by the Tribunal.[114] The Tribunal notes that the audit outcome letter and accompanying reasons dated 2 June 2022 is a short form document of 3 pages which does not comment on whether the log book was taken to comply with Subdivision 28-G or not. The Tribunal is not saying that it disbelieves the Applicant, but the First Objection Decision must be accepted as the expression of the Respondent’s position in preference to a prior conversation with an audit officer.
  12. In any event, although this background is helpful in clarifying the parties’ positions and contentions, the question for the Tribunal is whether the Applicant has demonstrated, on the balance of probabilities, in any of the Relevant Years that he was entitled to use the log book method and to deduct the amounts of car expenses worked out under that method as claimed by him. Debate about what the Respondent might have previously communicated to the Applicant is not directly relevant in answering this question. The Tribunal will have regard to the documentary and other evidence provided by the parties in this proceeding, noting that the Applicant bears the burden of proof.
  13. The Applicant is correct in stating that for the purposes of the log book method, where a person has kept a log book for a particular income year, they are generally not required to keep a new log book for the next four years: section 28-115. However, this is subject to the ‘log book’ being recognised as a log book for the purposes of Division 28. That is, where a document described by a person as a log book does not meet the requirements of that Division, and in particular Subdivision 28-G, its contents cannot be relied upon by the Applicant in subsequent years of income. When the Respondent says that the Applicant’s log book records are not ‘accepted’ or ‘valid’ it is indicating the Respondent’s position that they do not meet the requirements of Division 28.
  14. In relation to the issue of whether the Applicant kept log books which were compliant with Division 28, the requirements for keeping a log book include that it record journeys made in the car during the log book period in the course of producing the taxpayer’s assessable income, and that entries in the log book record particular details such as the date of the journey, the kilometres travelled, the reason for the journey and odometer readings at the start and end of the journey. An entry must be made at the end of the relevant journey or as soon as possible afterwards.[115]
  15. The Respondent pointed to certain entries in the Applicant’s log book which it said raised doubts about whether the entries were completed contemporaneously (that is, at the end of the journey or as soon as possible afterwards) and/or whether the entries accurately recorded basic details such as distance travelled.
  16. For example, there were two trips recorded between the same locations on separate dates where a tollway was used for one and not for the other.[116] The Applicant said that one of those trips was on a weekday and the other on a weekend, and he would use an online tool such as Google Maps before any journey to work out the best route for that day, which might be different from day to day. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s explanation for this item as plausible.
  17. However, the Respondent also referred to trips that appeared in invoices issued by a tollway company that did not appear in the log book documents prepared by the Applicant. This suggested to the Respondent that entries in the log book documents were not made by the Applicant contemporaneously with the relevant journeys and that the log book documents were not accurate. The Applicant explained that because he drove so much for work, he preferred to allow others to drive on holidays and weekends and he would hand his ‘e-tag’ to the person driving (for use in their car) by way of his contribution to the cost of the trip.[117] The Tribunal does not immediately reject the Applicant’s explanation as being untruthful but observes that in circumstances where the Applicant shared his e-tag with other people for use in vehicles other than the Volvo, this potentially creates a challenge for him in terms of his being able to demonstrate the accuracy of his log book documents. As noted previously, the Applicant bears the onus of proof before the Tribunal in this proceeding.
  18. In relation to log book documents put forward by the Applicant for the 2021 year in particular, the Respondent identified apparent discrepancies between the tollway company invoices and the entries in the log book document. The tollway company statements showed several trips in a vehicle with the registration of the Volvo car taken on tollway roads in June 2021 which were not recorded by the Applicant in his log book document. Further, the odometer readings shown in the Applicant’s log book document had not changed across those dates as would have been expected where the Volvo had been driven, whether for business or personal purposes.[118] The Applicant acknowledged that at least some of these items appeared to be mistakes on his part and were not intentional.[119]
  19. The Tribunal is willing to accept that the Applicant was not intending to mislead the Respondent when he apparently failed to record these trips, and that this was most likely inadvertent. The Tribunal also acknowledges that the particular trips of concern identified by the Respondent during the hearing were a very small proportion of the overall entries in the log book documents. However, the Tribunal agrees with the Respondent that the point is that in having found some errors in the log book documents as prepared by the Applicant, the overall reliability of the document as a log book is unclear.
  20. Subdivision 28-G does not prescribe a particular level of accuracy or allowable margin of error. However, in this case the errors identified go to fundamental information required by Subdivision 28-G such as the existence of a journey, rather than, for example, being a minor or transcription error such as recording 100 kilometres rather than 101 kilometres for a particular journey. Subsection 28-125(2) provides that a journey ‘is recorded by making in the log book an entry specifying’ the things in that subsection. Subsection 28-95(5) provides that a ‘reasonable estimate’ of business kilometres for the purpose of applying the log book method must take into account log book and odometer records.
  21. Subsection 28-125(1) does contemplate that a journey might not be recorded, and this would practically be contrary to the interests of the taxpayer because it would potentially produce a lower business use percentage. However, it may also result in other distortions or defects in information which is relevant to the ability to form a ‘reasonable’ estimate for the purposes of the log book method.
  22. In these circumstances the Tribunal must find that the log book documents prepared by the Applicant for the 2020 and 2021 years do not meet the requirements of Subdivision 28-G and therefore the Applicant is not able to use the log book method for working out any deductions for car expenses for either year. The Tribunal notes that the Respondent has determined administratively that it will not seek to further reduce the amount previously allowed to the Applicant for the 2020 year and that it will allow a further amount to the Applicant in that year equal to 73% of the decline in value for the Volvo car in that year. Therefore, the impact of the Tribunal’s finding will be felt in relation to the 2021 year where the cents per kilometre method will be applicable.
  23. The Respondent also submitted that if the Tribunal found that the Applicant was able to use the log book method in the 2021 year, then the Tribunal should find that he had not substantiated certain car expenses claimed by him. Although it is not strictly necessary for the Tribunal to make findings in relation to substantiation of those expenses, the Tribunal makes the following observations in response to the submissions of the parties on this matter:
    (a) The Applicant submitted that the Respondent had provided incorrect or misleading guidance to him by telling him that it was permissible for him to pay tradespeople in cash (provided receipts and invoices were kept) but subsequently questioning such payments made by him. The Tribunal does not believe that the advice of the Respondent was incorrect or misleading. The concerns raised by the Respondent in this matter were not based on the fact of payments being made in cash, but on the reliability or absence of corroborating documentation including invoices and receipts.

    (b) The Tribunal does not believe that the existence of spelling or grammatical errors in the invoices for mechanical repairs of itself demonstrates that the invoices are not a true record of amounts incurred. It is quite possible that the owner of that business or the person preparing their accounts may lack English language proficiency.

    (c) However, the Tribunal would have difficulty in finding that the mechanical repair expenses had been incurred by the Applicant on the basis of those invoices alone, in circumstances where invoices do not specify the vehicle to which the repairs relate and there is no corroborating evidence demonstrating how or when (or if) the invoices were paid by the Applicant. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he would pay the mechanic in cash if the business owner was there in person when the Applicant attended the workshop, or he would Pay ID the owner if he was not on site. The Applicant also explained that he took not only the Volvo car to that mechanic but also his daughter’s car.[120] There were clearly some amounts in the bank statements of the Applicant which showed that he had transferred sums of money to the mechanic, however, it was not possible to clearly link those transfers to the invoices put forward by the Applicant as relevant to the Volvo car.

    (d) Again, the Tribunal is not suggesting that the Applicant had made false statements to the Respondent or the Tribunal[121], however, in the context of the Applicant having the onus of proof, the Tribunal would not have been able to find that the Applicant had incurred those expenses in any particular year on the basis of the invoice documents and the Applicant’s oral evidence alone.

  24. There was a further question around the correct calculation of an additional amount of car expense to be allowed under Division 28 to the Applicant in the 2020 year arising in relation to the decline in value of the Volvo. Both parties accepted that, regardless of their position on the validity of the log book, the Applicant would be allowed car expense deductions for that year as if the log book method had been applied and using a ‘business use percentage’ of 73%. On the first day of the hearing there was disagreement as to the particular dollar amount to which that 73% was to be applied, based on whether the full amount of depreciation was included in car expenses for the year and 73% applied to that, or whether 73% of the depreciation was worked out first and added to the other car expenses. The amount by which the parties’ respective calculations varied was approximately $1,400 and the Respondent indicated that this might instead be due to other vehicle expenses not being accepted as substantiated. Rather than use hearing time to debate what might essentially be a calculation matter, the parties agreed to discuss this issue and revert to the Tribunal before the end of the hearing.[122]
  25. On the second day of the hearing the Respondent took the Applicant and the Tribunal through various documents dealing with this matter and said that in the interests of resolving this point they would be willing to allow, as a deduction, the amount claimed by the Applicant in his income tax return for the 2020 year (and which was an issue in the First Objection) but did not believe it was appropriate to allow the Applicant the greater amount he had set out in his subsequent submissions to the Tribunal. The Applicant said that he was happy with this. The Tribunal therefore believes that the quantum of deduction to be allowed to the Applicant for car expenses for the 2020 year is no longer in dispute.[123]

ISSUE 4: ADMINISTRATIVE PENALTIES

Relevant law and guidance

  1. Subdivision 284-C in Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 contains provisions for administrative penalties relating to ‘statements.’ This includes the imposition and calculation of penalties for the making of false or misleading statements made by a taxpayer to the Respondent.
  2. Subsection 284-75(1) provides that a person will be liable for an administrative penalty where they make a statement to the Respondent and that statement is ‘false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it.’ This provision also applies to a statement made by the person’s agent as if it had been made by the person: section 284-25.
  3. However, a person is not liable to a penalty under subsection 284-75(1) where:
    (a) The person or their agent took ‘reasonable care’ in connection with the making of the statement[124]; or

    (b) The person engaged a registered tax agent, gave the registered tax agent all relevant taxation information, the registered tax agent made the statement and the ‘false or misleading’ nature of the statement did not arise from ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the agent or ‘recklessness’ by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law.[125]

  4. Sections 284-85 and 284-90 contain provisions for working out the amount of the penalty to be imposed. The items in the Table in subsection 284-90(1) establish how the ‘base penalty amount’ is to be determined where penalties are imposed. In circumstances where a false or misleading statement has been made which resulted in a tax shortfall amount[126] this includes:
    (a) A base penalty amount equal to 25% of the shortfall amount in circumstances where the shortfall amount resulted from a failure by the taxpayer or their agent to take reasonable care to comply with a taxation law.

    (b) A base penalty amount equal to 50% of the shortfall amount where the shortfall resulted from recklessness by the taxpayer or their agent as to the operation of a taxation law.

    (c) A base penalty amount equal to 75% of the shortfall amount where the shortfall amount resulted from intentional disregard by the taxpayer or tax agent of a taxation law.

  5. A base penalty amount may be further increased in circumstances a penalty has been imposed on the taxpayer in a previous year: see subsection 284-220(1).
  6. The taxation legislation does not provide a separate definition of ‘recklessness’, however the Federal Court has stated that:[127]
    Recklessness in this context means to include in a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and that a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to a gross carelessness.

And[128]:

‘the context makes it clear that recklessness means something more than failure to exercise reasonable care ... but less than an intentional disregard of the Act.’

  1. Further, there is a line between ‘recklessness’ and dishonesty.[129]
  2. The Respondent has issued guidance[130] which sets out its interpretation of concepts and terminology used in Division 284 such as ‘reasonable care’, ‘recklessness’ and ‘intentional disregard.’ The Tribunal is not bound by the Respondent’s public guidance or instructions for its staff, however, such material may assist in understanding a penalty position taken by the Respondent, and consistency of decision-making is desirable. In some instances this guidance refers to case authority which may be binding on or precedential for the Tribunal.
  3. However, under subsection 298-20(1) in Schedule 1, where a penalty has been imposed on a person ‘[the Respondent] may remit all or a part of the penalty.’ The TAA 1953 does not specify particular matters which the Respondent (or the Tribunal standing in the shoes of the Respondent) must take into account when considering whether to remit all or part of a penalty. Case authority suggests that the Tribunal should consider whether it is appropriate in ‘all the circumstances’ to remit all or part of the penalty[131] and that the discretion to remit a penalty must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 1953.[132]
  4. The Respondent has also issued guidance for its staff as to the factors which the Respondent will take into account in determining whether remission of all or part of a penalty is appropriate.[133] Again, the Tribunal is not bound by this guidance but observes that many of the factors set out in the guidance reflect matters considered by courts and tribunals in previous cases.

Was the Applicant liable for a penalty?

  1. The Respondent imposed an administrative penalty on the Applicant in each of the 2021 and 2022 years on the basis that:
    (a) The Applicant or his tax agent made a false or misleading statement in each of those years. The false or misleading statement comprised the income tax return that was lodged on an incorrect basis. The Applicant (and his agent) had not taken ‘reasonable care’ in the preparation of his returns as demonstrated by such matters as an incorrect ‘business use percentage’ being applied to car expenses, log books not being a true representation of travel undertaken, rental income being below market value, lack of substantiation and apportionment of rental expenses and other items which were not directly part of the dispute before the Tribunal. Further, the Applicant had been put on notice during the first audit for the 2020 year that the Respondent had concerns regarding the commerciality of the rental arrangements for the Meadow Heights Property and his substantiation of claims. He had been warned in the audit outcome letter that penalties might apply if he made future errors.

    (b) Those false or misleading statements resulted in a tax shortfall amount.

    (c) The base penalty amount in each of those years should be worked out on the basis that the shortfall amount resulted from ‘recklessness’ of Applicant (or his agent) as to the operation of a taxation law. The Respondent again referred to matters such as incorrect items in the Applicant’s income tax returns, deficiencies within log books, inadequate substantiation of expenses and claims, and the warning in the earlier audit letter as also demonstrating ‘recklessness’ on the part of the Applicant (or his agent) in the 2021 and 2022 years.

    (d) The Respondent acknowledged that the audit outcome letter from the first audit for the 2020 year, which contained the ‘warning’, was sent to the Applicant after he had lodged his income tax return for the 2021 year.[134] However the Respondent said that the Applicant could have sought to amend his 2021 return to take account of matters raised in the audit letter.

    (e) Conduct of the Applicant’s tax agent was imputed to the Applicant. As the behaviours in the present case should be regarded as ‘reckless’, the Applicant cannot rely on subsection 284-75(6) to remove the liability for a penalty.

  2. The Respondent again referred to the Applicant’s burden of proof under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) and said that the Applicant had not demonstrated, on the balance of probabilities, that the Penalty Assessments were excessive or incorrect, and what they should have been.
  3. It is important to understand that a person may be taken to have made a ‘false or misleading statement’ in circumstances where they are not regarded as having been dishonest.
  4. The Tribunal agrees that it would technically have been possible for the Applicant to seek an amendment to his original assessment for the 2021 year after receiving the first audit outcome letter dated June 2022 containing the Respondent’s ‘warning.’ However, the Tribunal notes that the Respondent did not commence the first audit (being the audit of the 2020 year) until February 2022. The Applicant lodged the First Objection in August 2022, which was a clear indication that the Applicant disputed the correctness of the outcome of the first audit. In those circumstances the Tribunal does not believe it can be said that the Applicant made a false or misleading statement that was ‘reckless’ at the time he lodged his income tax return for the 2021 year because he (or his tax agent) could not have been aware of the Respondent’s concerns until February 2022 at the earliest, and did not have the outcome of the first audit until June 2022.
  5. In relation to the Applicant’s failure to seek an amendment to his assessment for the 2021 year, the Tribunal queries whether a ‘statement’ was made by the Applicant to the Respondent in these circumstances. In terms of ‘recklessness’, the parties were still working through the issues arising from the 2020 year until at least the time when the Respondent issued the First Objection Decision in October 2022. The Tribunal again notes that the audit outcome letter for the first audit adopted a ‘short form’ format and a fuller explanation of the basis for the Respondent’s decision in relation to the 2020 year apparently first appears in the First Objection Decision. It was clear to the Tribunal from the discussion at the hearing that the Applicant did not fully understand that, for example, the Respondent had refused to accept the 2020 log book as valid even though he was assessed as if it had been. Thereafter, there was only a limited period until the Respondent commenced its formal audit of the 2021 and 2022 years in May 2023.
  6. On this basis the Tribunal believes that the Applicant’s actions (or lack of action) in relation to his income tax return for the 2021 year is better described as a ‘failure to take reasonable care’ or possibly negligence rather than gross carelessness or gross indifference as to whether his income tax return was correct. The Tribunal believes that the administrative penalty for the 2021 year should be imposed at the rate of 25% on the basis of a failure by the Applicant (or his then agent) to take reasonable care to comply with a taxation law, rather than 50% on the basis of recklessness. The Tribunal was not provided with evidence about the information that the Applicant might have provided to his tax agent for that year and therefore the Tribunal cannot make a finding that the Applicant was not subject to a penalty at all under subsection 284-75(6).
  7. However, the situation is different for the 2022 year. By the time that the Applicant lodged his income tax return for that year (in April 2023) he had received both the first audit outcome letter of June 2022 (with the ‘warning’) and the First Objection Decision which affirmed and explained more fully the Respondent’s position on key issues. There had also been an extended period of engagement between the Applicant and Respondent before he lodged his income tax return for the 2022 year. Whether or not he or his tax agent agreed with the Respondent’s position, they should have been aware of a ‘real risk’ by that time that the Applicant’s approach was incorrect or that a reasonable person in his position would see that his approach did not reflect the relevant taxation laws. The Tribunal therefore believes that the administrative penalty for the 2022 year was correctly imposed at the rate of 50% on the basis of recklessness by the taxpayer (or his agent) as to the operation of a taxation law. The Tribunal observes that subsection 284-75(6) does not operate where a false or misleading statement was made in circumstances of recklessness.

Should all or a part of the penalty be remitted?

  1. The Applicant said that no penalties should be applied to him.
  2. The Applicant told the Tribunal about health challenges he has faced in recent years, together with the distress caused by his relationship breakdown and family law matters. He said that the period of the audits coincided with the time of his divorce and his children being taken overseas. He said that he tried to the best of his ability to provide documents.[135]
  3. The Respondent acknowledged that the Applicant had faced personal difficulties, but on the basis of information available they understood that by October 2020 the family law litigation had concluded and the Applicant’s former wife and children had left Australia prior to that. The Applicant’s tax return for the 2021 year was lodged in November 2021 and for the 2022 year in April 2023. The Respondent also observed that the Applicant had the assistance of a tax agent in filing his tax returns.
  4. The Tribunal might have accepted a more direct impact on the Applicant’s ability to attend to his tax affairs in relation to the assessment for the 2020 year as he would have been occupied with the family law matters at this time. However, the Respondent did not impose any penalties for that year. Although the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant would have felt ongoing distress from his family circumstances, the impact of that on his ability to ensure compliant returns for the 2021 and 2022 years was less clear, particularly when he had engaged a registered tax agent. The Tribunal also understands that the Applicant’s health issues, whilst significant, largely emerged after the time during which his returns were being prepared, again with the assistance of a professional tax agent.
  5. The Tribunal therefore does not believe the Applicant’s health and family circumstances weigh significantly in favour of further remission of penalties. The Tribunal observes that the Respondent took into account issues involving the tax agent by agreeing to a partial remission of penalties prior to the Tribunal proceedings.
  6. The Applicant said that the Respondent had provided him with misleading guidance. He also said that the Respondent had taken inconsistent positions, for example in basing its decisions relating to the 2020 year on an application of IT 2167[136] which assumed that the Meadow Heights Property was rented, but then querying the existence of a tenancy arrangement for the 2021 and 2022 years.
  7. The Tribunal does believe that some of the communications and correspondence from the Respondent to the Applicant have been confusing and positions taken in the second audit and Second Objection Decision may have changed from those taken in the first audit and First Objection Decision. However, the Tribunal also acknowledges that some changes in position relating to rental expenses for the Meadow Heights Property may have arisen as a result of the Respondent obtaining further information, and the Respondent may have made certain allowances to the Applicant which arguably it was not required to make.[137]
  8. The Respondent had also remitted half of the administrative penalties imposed on the Applicant for the 2021 and 2022 years (and the 20% uplift factor for the 2022 year) before an application for review was made to the Tribunal in relation to those years. Although this does not mean that the Respondent had determined that the Applicant was not ‘reckless’, the practical outcome of the remission was that the amount of penalties payable by the Applicant were 25% of the shortfall amount rather than 50%. The Respondent remitted this amount having regard to the Applicant having retained, and apparently relied on the advice of, a registered tax agent in circumstances where the tax agent’s advice may not have been adequate.
  9. The Tribunal agrees that the Respondent was correct to remit a part of the penalties on the basis of perceived inadequacies in the advice provided to the Applicant by his tax agent, and believes that the same approach should be taken by the Tribunal for that reason. Therefore:
    (a) In relation to the Penalty Assessment for the 2021 year, the Tribunal finds that in addition to the reduction of the base penalty amount to a rate of 25% of the shortfall amount as set out above, half of that amount should be remitted under section 298-20.

    (b) In relation to the Penalty Assessment for the 2022 year, the Tribunal affirms the approach taken by the Respondent. That is, the base penalty amount is to be calculated at 50% of the shortfall amount with an uplift of 20%, but the uplift amount and half of the base penalty amount should be remitted.

  10. The Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant has identified circumstances, particularly those that might lead to a harsh or unjust outcome, warranting any further remission of the penalty in either of the 2021 or 2022 years.

FURTHER MATTERS

  1. The parties’ respective written closing submissions also raised the following matters:
    (a) The Respondent made submissions criticising the Applicant’s credibility as a witness, including references to self-serving testimony or untruthful evidence or information having been provided by the Applicant to the Tribunal and to third parties.

    (b) The Applicant made submissions that the Respondent’s audit and review processes were affected by repeated factual errors on the part of the Respondent, that the Respondent provided misleading guidance, that there were questions about the independence of audit processes and that concerns arose about the integrity of the Respondent’s decision-making processes. The Applicant also submitted that the Respondent had not provided him with procedural fairness, in that he was not given a proper opportunity to respond during audit and objection processes to adverse information which appeared material to the objection decisions. The Applicant also said that he was pressured by the Respondent to obtain legal representation in this proceeding.

  2. The Tribunal did not find these submissions of any further assistance to it in reaching its decision. As set out in the reasons above, the Tribunal has stated its findings on disputed factual matters, provided its view on the reliability of evidence provided to the Tribunal and identified matters it believes are relevant to the imposition or remission of administrative penalties. The Tribunal does not believe it is necessary or appropriate to otherwise make credibility findings specific to the Applicant. This merits review by the Tribunal of the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision has provided an opportunity for the Applicant to put forward evidence or respond to evidence of the Respondent, as relevant to those reviewable decisions, across three hearing days and within multiple written submissions. The Tribunal’s processes are designed to support self-represented applicants and the Tribunal has been mindful throughout this proceeding that the Applicant did not have legal or other formal representation.
  3. Also as set out above, the Tribunal is obliged to have regard to the statutory burden of proof imposed on the Applicant under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953 and the particular matters which the Applicant is required to establish.

CONCLUSION

  1. A significant challenge for the Tribunal in reaching its decision in this proceeding has been that the positions and contentions of the parties have shifted since the time of the audit and objection processes. The Tribunal acknowledges that the Respondent has sought to take certain administrative approaches which would narrow the issues in dispute before the Tribunal and avoid some disadvantage to the Applicant, and the Applicant has sought to provide further information and analysis to respond to issues as they evolved. However, the Tribunal has been asked to consider alternative positions which do not neatly align and which may not be apparent from the content of the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision.
  2. This is most evident in the Tribunal being invited by the Respondent to make findings to the effect that the Meadow Heights Property might not be tenanted at all, such that it could not be described as a rental property, whilst also being asked to affirm the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision which recognised rental income of the Applicant and allowed the Applicant deductions for a portion of the rental expenses claimed by him.
  3. The Tribunal is also obliged to have regard to subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) which requires the Applicant to prove, on the balance of probabilities, certain matters. The Tribunal is not readily able to make administrative concessions or accept assumptions which are not consistent with the law as supported by evidence to this standard.
  4. The Tribunal has concluded that the correct or preferable decision is that, except for:
    (a) The Penalty Assessment issued to the Applicant in relation to the 2021 year which should be amended to reflect a finding of lack of reasonable care rather than recklessness, and

    (b) The recognition of an amount of $15,185 as a deduction for car expenses in the 2020 year (as agreed between the parties)[138],

the First Objection Decision and Second Objection Decision should otherwise stand.
