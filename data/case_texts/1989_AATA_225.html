NT88/1302-1303 and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 225 (20 October 1989)

Last Updated: 17 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income – Investigation - Default Assessments - Source and Application of Funds Statement in one year - Specific allegations of omissions in another - Burden of proof.

INCOME TAX - Burden of proof - Quantum - No allowance for outgoings made in raising assessment - Finding of fact.

INCOME TAX - Additional tax for incorrect returns - Evasion Unlawful income - generating activity - Review of 80% "culpability factor".

INCOME TAX - Practice and Procedure - Application to hear evidence in camera - Fear of reprisals - Application refused.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) - S.167, 190, 223.

AAT Decision no 5442

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos. NT88/1302-1303 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

RE: Nos. NT88/1302-1303

Applicant

AND: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Mr Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 20 October 1989

Place : Sydney

Decision : The decisions of the Commissioner under review shall be varied in that taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1985 will be reduced by $800 to $48,242 and additional tax by $5,473 to $15,000; and that for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 taxable income will be reduced by $46,170 to $85,248 and additional tax by $30,875.

(Sgn) P.M. ROACH SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos. NT88/1302-1303

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: Nos. NT88/1302-1303

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 October 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The reviews which arise upon these references relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986. Both references arise out of assessments said to have been made pursuant with the provisions of Section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act). Such assessments are commonly referred to as "default assessments". By his objections the Applicant first seeks to persuade the Tribunal that the assessments are wholly excessive and that they should be set aside. Alternatively, he claims that the assessments should be reduced. There are significant differences as to the substantive issues and the evidence presented in relation to them which distinguish the two years of income.
  2. In relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1985 the Commissioner based his assessment of taxable income upon calculations which he had made and which resulted in the preparation of a "Source and Application of Funds" statement (commonly referred to as a "T" account). As the nature and methodology of such an account has been recently explained in an unreported decision of 7 July 1989 (No. 5246), I do not pause to repeat what was then said. Suffice it to say that, having calculated that the total moneys expended during the year exceeded the sums of money known to be available, the Commissioner asserts that the shortfall - a sum necessarily derived if expenditure was accurately calculated - constituted assessable income. The Applicant has the task of persuading the Tribunal - but only upon the balance of probabilities - that the calculation so made by the Commissioner is erroneous and the assessment excessive.

3. In relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986 the Commissioner based his assessment upon records and information provided to him by a company ("Coffers") which is now in liquidation. At all material times Coffers was controlled by an individual now deceased ("the Deceased"). It is contended that, according to the records of Coffers, in a period of only a few months the Applicant purchased from Coffers cigarettes at a cost of $723,407. The purchases were made in Queensland. The contention of the Commissioner is that the Applicant then effected the transportation of those cigarettes to New South Wales and there resold them: his object in doing so being to generate income, taking advantage of the absence of any State tobacco tax in Queensland comparable to the then taxes on tobacco in NSW. By his objection the Applicant wholly denied the allegations of the Commissioner.

  1. A further issue is common to the two years. It is that the Commissioner levied an excessive amount of additional tax for incorrect returns. In doing so he remitted in part the penalties fixed upon by the Act at double the amount of tax avoided. The remissions had the effect of reducing additional tax to an amount equal to 20% per annum of tax avoided during the period of avoidance leading up to the amended assessments, plus a "culpability factor" of 80% of tax avoided.
  2. The Applicant initially claimed that throughout the relevant period he was unemployed and that he was only able to make provision for his wife, his five children and himself by reason of grants from the public purse. Upon the hearing however he acknowledged that for a short time and on a small scale he had been involved in dealings in cigarettes imported from Queensland which generated a profit of the order of $150 on each of some 12 occasions - and possibly 25 occasions according to another statement - on which he dealt in that way. He vehemently denied ever having been involved on any scale such as has been attributed to him by the Commissioner. However, he also admitted that before any dealings on his own account he had assisted a compatriot on similar trips for which he had been paid $125 per trip - income not acknowledged in his returns of income.
  3. Upon the evidence I am satisfied that the Applicant came to Australia with his wife and two children from Lebanon in 1977. He secured employment and thereby was able to make provision for his family. As a further result of his efforts he was able to purchase a modest home for his family. In 1981 he and his wife sold that home and returned to Lebanon for several months. They then returned to Australia. I accept that, at the point of leaving for Lebanon, they had available to them a sum of the order of $25,000 representing the capital gain on the sale of that first home.
  4. Having returned to Australia, the family lived in rented accommodation until about December 1984 when they moved into a newly purchased home. For most of the period following their return, and throughout the entire period to which the assessments in question relate, the Applicant was in receipt of unemployment benefits. Despite his now long period of residence in Australia I am satisfied that the Applicant has not seriously attempted to become integrated into Australian society. Even today his English remains very limited - so limited that his evidence was given throughout through an interpreter. I accept that he is barely literate in his native language. But I am not persuaded that he was incapable of becoming much more fluent in the use of the English language (as spoken in Australia) than he has chosen to be. Even on his own account he was capable of conducting his admitted dealings with Coffers in English and without assistance. Further, nothing in the evidence persuades me that he was incapable, by reason of any physical, mental or social disability, of working at at least such tasks as he had previously engaged in.
  5. By October 1984 he and his wife had contracted for the purchase of a new home at a price of $77,500. Upon its acquisition, they proceeded to undertake substantial works of renovation (including - inter alia - fitting security bars at a cost of the order of $2,000) and the installation of a swimming pool (at a cost of c. $9,000). They were assisted in doing those things by loans of $55,000 from a bank and $15,000 from a well-disposed and trusting solicitor: the latter advance being made without any substantial security. The loan from the bank was induced by a false, representation on the part of the Applicant that he was employed at a wage of $30,230 per annum. Had the representation been true, monthly mortgage payments would have absorbed 26% of his wages - a common enough standard. But the fact of the matter was that, according to the Applicant's evidence before me, the only income of the household at that time comprised unemployment benefits and Child Endowment. Were those the only sources of money on which to live, mortgage repayments would have absorbed 75% of the family revenues.

9. Another development in the affairs of the Applicant and his family had come a few months earlier when in June 1984 he became hirer pursuant to a hire purchase agreement of a new four cylinder two litre Toyota Hi-Ace van. The deposit of $3,995 was provided for by the trade-in of a previously owned vehicle. The hire purchase agreement called for 48 monthly payments of $282.85. I am satisfied that all of the payments called for under the agreement were in due course made and that some were made while the Applicant was overseas with his family in Lebanon late in 1986. The Applicant's evidence was that he was but the nominee for a group within the Lebanese community concerned to provide instruction for the children for that community; that all payments were made with funds provided by the association; and that the principal use of the vehicle was to provide transport of the children to and from class. To secure financing by hire purchase the Applicant lied - falsely representing himself to be employed at a time when he professed himself to be unemployed to secure public support. No witness was forthcoming from the group of persons concerned to present evidence in support of the assertions of the Applicant.

  1. When in 1986 the Applicant was interviewed by officers of the Commissioner, one of whom was fluent in Arabic, the Applicant denied having had any undisclosed sources of income. At the hearing he was to acknowledge that he had lied in making that assertion. That arose in the course of the hearing when he admitted that he had travelled to Queensland on a number of occasions with a third party and had there been introduced to Coffers and to the trade of dealing in imported cigarettes. Then, he said, thereafter on a few occasions - at one stage said to be about 12 but later said to have been possibly 25 - he purchased cigarettes and imported them to New South Wales and there effected sales. His claim was that on each such journey his receipts exceeded his total costs by something of the order of only $150. He acknowledged purchases of the order of $10,000 to $11,000 per trip - providing a total outlay from $120,000 to $275,000. In addition he claimed to have only used the Hi-Ace van for the purpose.
  2. In relation to the year of income ended 30 July 1986 the Commissioner did not base his determination of taxable income upon any form of "T" account. Instead he based his calculation on the records of and information provided by Coffers. In due course it will be necessary to consider the significance of that evidence in relation to the question of quantum. For the moment my concern is to assess the evidence which was presented as to the volume of dealings in cigarettes. That evidence came in two parts.

THE NEIGHBOURS

  1. Counsel for the Commissioner indicated his desire to call two witnesses who had resided in the vicinity of the Applicant's home during the relevant period. Counsel requested the Tribunal to receive their evidence in camera on the basis that they were elderly and that they were fearful that they might be the subject of some form of reprisal if it came to be known that they were to give evidence: particularly evidence preceding a result detrimental to the Applicant. It was not suggested that anything threatening had occurred, but rather only that there was a fear in the proposed witnesses borne out of their expectations as to what might happen if their testimony worked to establish the unlawful activities of the Applicant and to expose him to substantial liability in income tax.
  2. I refused the application. Justice calls for fair and open hearings in which the persons to be judged are not denied knowledge of a testimony presented against them. If that from time to time requires of witnesses that they should expose themselves to some risk - and I do not suggest that there was any risk in this instance - then it is a risk which must be run in the interests of the maintenance of a just society. If there is a basis for such fear on the part of prospective witnesses, then appropriate action needs to be taken by properly authorised and structured bodies in order to avert the type of action feared.
  3. The application for a hearing in camera having been refused. Counsel for the Commissioner elected to call only one witness. That witness had been a neighbour to the Applicant during the relevant period. He was an unsophisticated, "old Australian". He had earned his living making and marketing blinds and later in delivering ironing. He had no closer association with the Applicant as a neighbour than to exchange casual greetings. They had nothing more in common than the proximity of their homes. The relationship between the two properties was such that the bedroom of the witness lay some 3 metres from the side boundary of his property. Beyond that boundary there immediately lay a large carport on the property of the Applicant.
  4. The evidence of the neighbour was that, over a period which he could not precisely specify as to duration or time, he would be wakened in the early hours of the morning by the sound of trucks, including the sound of warning beepers as they reversed along the Applicant's drive towards his carport. On one occasion he saw a semi-trailer and on other occasions large trucks larger than vans such as the Toyota Hi-Ace. On such occasions he sometimes saw large boxes marked as containing cigarettes of specified brands being unloaded and stored. He did not always rise to look when wakened by the noise of reversing trucks. He did not count the boxes. He did not count the number of occasions, or identify or record the dates on which he heard such vehicles. He made no complaint of disturbance. In short, he minded his own business. His further evidence was that later boxes would be removed from the site by small vans similar to the Hi-Ace van.

16. When contacted early in this year by officers of the Australian Taxation Office he told what he knew of the matter and when summonsed to appear before the Tribunal he attended and gave evidence. I accept his evidence that he did not seek out the Australian Tax Office; that he would have preferred not to have become involved; that he had no reason to be more precise about dates, the number of occasions, the descriptions of the trucks or the volume of deliveries or the contents of boxes than he was; and that he bore no animus against the Applicant. I accept his evidence and find that it confirms that the scale of the Applicant's dealings was substantially greater than was acknowledged before me by the Applicant.

THE RECORDS OF COFFERS

17. At the hearing the Commissioner sought to establish the extent of the Applicant's trade with Coffers by producing evidence based on the records of Coffers. He sought to do so by calling as a witness an officer of the liquidator of that company and by having him produce records now under his control which he had taken up from the offices of Coffers. The witness had on one occasion attended with the Deceased upon the Applicant at his home. On that occasion the witness had observed difficulties in communication between the Deceased and the Applicant. It was not contended that that incident involved anything more significant than the circumstance that the Deceased and the Applicant obviously were previously known to each other - something which was not in dispute. Examination of the records of Coffers by the witness had satisfied the witness that the Applicant had dealt with Coffers in the manner recorded in the books of Coffers as dealings with an individual identified by first name only. The first name happened to be that of the Applicant. Other information supplied to the witness by the officers of Coffers had satisfied him that the entries referred to the Applicant. But that evidence does not persuade me. Documentary records are no more reliable than the persons who composed them. Mere production of the records in the form in which they came before me does not persuade me that identification of customers was maintained on such a precise and regular basis as to give credibility to the records in the absence of testimony from individuals associated with the making of, or with the supervision of the making of, the entries in those records. I am reinforced in that view by the circumstance that among the docket books produced are records maintained in names that are well familiar to the Australian community as the names of entertainers and of spies. There are other records too which, perhaps as a variant on the familiar "Tom, Dick and Harry", are presented in the form of "Tom 1", "Tom 2", "Tom 3". I am not persuaded by the records, or by the belief of the witness in the reliability of the records, that I should find trading to have taken place as contended for in the mind of the witness and the assertions of the Commissioner.

LIABILITY 1985

  1. The issue in relation to the 1985 year is simply whether the testimony of the Applicant and the other evidence has persuaded me that the calculations of the Commissioner based upon the "T" accounts have resulted in an excessive assessment. The principal error contended for is an alleged failure on the part of the Commissioner to bring to account as "funds available" a sum of the order of $25,000-$26,000 said to be held in cash (or equivalent) at 30 June 1984. That money is said to have been available to the Applicant and his wife from the time of the sale of their original home and to have been available to them, notwithstanding that it had been held in cash (or equivalent) and held unproductively and so held during a period when the family were occupying rented accommodation instead of being used to provide an equity in a new residence for them. It was alleged that the monies were available to be taken to Lebanon; were not to any extent expended, let alone exhausted, during the prolonged absence of the family there; and were brought back into Australia. It was said that the reason that the monies were not outlaid in, for example, any interest bearing investment was the moral objection on the part of the wife to the lending of money at interest. It is logically possible that it was so, but when asked to accept as a fact that it was so, and to base such a finding on nothing more than the testimony of a person whom I have found to be untruthful, I am not persuaded that it probably was so.
  2. In my view the Applicant was a wholly unreliable witness and his evidence is not to be accepted, except to the extent to which it is confirmed in some respect by other evidence. In consequence in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1985 the only allowance I would make in favour of the Applicant relates to an adjustment based upon the Commissioner's calculations in relation to the house purchase. The Commissioner brought to account the cost of the house at $80,000 and legal costs at $800. But evidence from the banking records indicates that the sum of $80,000 was sufficient to cover the purchase price of the house, stamp duty and legal expenses. Accordingly, I am persuaded that the assessment of taxable income was excessive to the extent of $800, but no more.

LIABILITY: 1986

20. As to the year of income ended 30 June 1986 the Applicant has admitted some understatements of income but he has failed to persuade me that his dealings were on so small a scale as his evidence would have it. That being so he has failed by his own testimony to persuade me that the assessment is excessive.

21. However, that does not finally determine the matter for it is appropriate to have regard to the methodology adopted by the Commissioner in fixing upon the amount of taxable income.

QUANTUM; 1986

22. The Commissioner took as the basis for his assessments the number and volume of transactions engaged in between the Applicant and Coffers as is alleged to be recorded in the records of Coffers. On that basis the Commissioner contended that gross purchases had amounted to $723,407. From that base the Commissioner alleged that gross profit on those purchases would have been $108,511: 15% of gross costs, being the tobacco tax available to be saved. To that figure the Commissioner then added $14,468, said to be a 2% rebate allowed by Coffers on purchases such as those attributed to the Applicant. The result was an assessment of taxable income at $131,418 made up as follows:

Unemployment Benefits (as returned) $ 8,439

Gross Profit 15% of $723,407 108,511

2% Rebate on $723,407 14,468

TOTAL : $131,418

23. It is to be noted that no provision was made by calculation or by estimate to allow for any expenses which might have been incurred in the generation of the alleged income. From that point I proceed to address the three questions which arise. The first is whether the Applicant has persuaded me that purchases were less than $723,407. He has failed to do so. The second is whether he has persuaded me that the gross profit on purchases was something less than 15%. He has failed to do so. The third is whether he has persuaded me that, to attribute to him the benefits of a rebate of 2% of gross purchase price has resulted in the calculation being excessive. Nothing in the evidence of the Applicant so persuades me, for there was no such evidence. However, I find that the calculation was to this extent excessive and do so on the basis of the Commissioner's own calculations. It was said by Counsel that the basis which prompted the Commissioner to bring to account the alleged rebate was a statement which had been made to an investigating officer by the Deceased. But, even if such a hearsay assertion were to be heeded, I find nothing in the materials placed before me from the records of Coffers to indicate that that rebate had not already been taken into account in fixing the gross sale price. I am not persuaded that there was any proper basis for bringing to account the rebate. To that extent the assessment is excessive.

24. The final question as to quantum is quite distinct. I am well satisfied that the Applicant incurred substantial expenses in procuring the delivery of the cigarettes and in effecting their distribution. Further, I am of opinion that, to the extent to which no attempt was made to bring such expenses to account or to establish what they were or might have been, the Commissioner acted at the very least unreasonably. Had such an exercise been undertaken and provision made on a not manifestly unreasonable basis for such expenses, the Applicant might well have failed to persuade me that the provision so made was inadequate. That would have been because the Applicant directed no evidence to establishing what those costs might have been in relation to transactions of the nature of those which I am satisfied took place. But the Commissioner's officers did not do that. Instead they caused to issue what they must have known to be an excessive claim. Not having undertaken the exercise, in my view the Commissioner is not to be now heard to complain that the Tribunal will be acting unreasonably if it makes such judgment as best it can in the circumstances and in the absence of such evidence as would have been helpful to it. I have already indicated in another decision (unreported: 7 July 1989: NT87/8291) my view of the duty of a Tribunal such as this in making findings of fact. For the reasons there expressed, it is my intention to proceed to make the best finding I can, notwithstanding the difficulties inherent in the exercise because of the paucity of evidence presented.

25. In making that finding of fact I have regard to the following considerations: first, I accept the contention of the Commissioner that there was a profit available to be had up to 15% of gross costs, plus a possible further margin for a distributor such as the Applicant. Nothing in the evidence persuades me that any provision should be made for any such possible margin as the latter. That being so I find that the 15% of purchase price had to suffice to cover the following provisions: costs of distribution; profit to consumer; profit to retailer; and profit to the Applicant. There is nothing in the evidence before me which persuades me that the retailer would have passed on any profit to consumers. But in my view it would be appropriate to find that retailers would insist upon buying from the Applicant at a price less than that at which they could have bought after local tax from local wholesalers. I find that one-fifth of the 15% available would have been appropriate for that. From the balance remaining, the Applicant would have had to bear the expenses of delivery and distribution of the cigarettes dealt with. I would allow $10,000 in that regard. If that sum is inadequate as it well may be, or excessive as it may well be, then the party disadvantaged by that mis judgment must accept the consequences of failing to address evidence as to the issue.

26. For the foregoing reasons, in relation to the year of income dated 30 June 1986 I am satisfied that the assessment of taxable income at $131,418 is excessive and that it should be reduced as follows:

Taxable Income as assessed $131,418

Less Rebate $14,468

Less Retailer's profit 21,702

Less delivery and

distribution costs 10,000 46,170

TOTAL: $ 85,248

ADDITIONAL TAX

27. I now proceed to address the issues arising upon the review of the imposition of additional tax for incorrect returns. The taxable income of the Applicant for the periods in question has been inflated to some extent by receipt of unemployment benefits to which there was no entitlement. Those monies ought be recoverable by the community, but it is not possible to determine to what extent they ought be recoverable. If in the latter year of income, cigarette trading was confined to the limited period postulated by the Commissioner - something which seems to be inherently improbable - it may be that the Applicant was entitled to receive much of the amount paid to him. But whatever might be the situation in that regard it has no relevance to the imposition of additional tax in the years in question for the simple reason that, whether obtained rightly or wrongly and whether obtained lawfully or unlawfully, the amounts actually received did constitute assesable income in the years in question and contributed to the avoidance of tax.

  1. The second matter to be noted is that, when remissions of additional tax were levied, in calculating an appropriate figure as a compensatory factor in accordance with his then current practice, the Commissioner applied an interest rate of 20% per annum: the rate which would have applied had liability to tax been correctly assessed but payment thereof deferred. Since that time the Commissioner's practices have changed (cf. Income Tax Ruling IT 2517) and the standard compensatory rate has been reduced from 20% per annum to 14.026% per annum. Counsel for the Commissioner has conceded that it is appropriate that the impositions of additional tax should be reviewed to give the Applicant the benefit of the lower compensatory rate of interest. For that reason the imposition of additional tax will be so reviewed.
  2. The matter of the "culpability factor" cannot be so easily dealt with. According to the norms applied by the Commissioner at the time the assessments were raised, the "culpability factor" was almost invariably calculated at a rate of 40% of tax avoided: rarely higher and not often lower. That practice was extensively criticised, and the present practices of the Commissioner are now expressed in Income Tax Ruling IT 2517. That Ruling contemplates a wide variety of situations in which the "culpability factor" ordinarily appropriate will often be found to fall substantially below 40% of tax avoided, even to the point where no "culpability factor" may be appropriate. However, the Ruling also recognises that there may be situations in which a culpability factor in excess of 40% of tax avoided may be appropriate. For my part I agree with that. But that is not to say that a "culpability factor" in excess of 40% of tax avoided will frequently be appropriate.

30. In the present instance the culpability factor applied by the Commissioner was quite remarkably high: 80% of tax avoided. It is a rate of imposition very rarely experienced. In considering the fitness of that penalty I consider it appropriate to take into account against the Applicant the following circumstances:

(a) that in my view the evasion in question was completely wilful and deliberate;

(b) that the Applicant was completely uncooperative during the period of investigation leading to assessment;

(c) that that non-cooperation delayed to some extent the assessment although, by reason of the levy of a compensatory factor, that circumstance is not of great significance;

(d) that the failure of the Applicant to maintain records made more difficult the task of assessment; and

(e) that during the period of investigation the Applicant sought to deceive the investigators.

  1. In my view, by reasons of those factors, a "culpability factor" exceeding 40% is appropriate. On the other hand, I do not accept that it is appropriate that the "culpability factor" should be increased by reason of the circumstance that the conduct in generating the income contravened State laws. In my view the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act should not be used to impose sanctions against criminal or anti-social conduct except as conduct liable to penalty by being breaches of duty in relation to the laws of income tax.
  2. Having regard to those considerations, I consider that a compensatory rate of 80% of tax avoided is excessive. That is so even though it is not easy to imagine a more extreme case of fiscal dishonesty. For all that, in my view, imposition of a "culpability factor" calculated at a rate of 60% of tax avoided is more appropriate in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986.
  3. Having made the foregoing findings in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986, the year in which findings revolved around allegations by the Commissioner that the evasion had arisen as a result of trading in cigarettes in breach of State law, the question which then arises is whether the same conclusion should be reached in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1985 where the assessments were based upon "T" account calculations and where no allegations were directly involved of any particular method of income generation. In my view the answer is "yes". I have already held that I reject any contention that the conduct of the Applicant was more deserving of penalty because of its unlawful nature as being in breach of the revenue laws of the State. In relation to the earlier year that question does not even arise. However, all of the considerations which I have judged to be relevant to the question of remissions are as much relevant to the 1985 year as the 1986 year. For that reason, in my view, the same principle should be applied in the calculation of remissions for both years of income.

CONCLUSIONS

34. The Order of the Tribunal will reflect the following findings:

(a) that taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1985 is excessive in the sum of $800;

(b) that taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 is excessive in the sum of $46,170;

(c) that in each year there should be a further review of remissions of additional tax for incorrect returns having regard to:

(i) reductions in taxable income;

(ii) the reduction of the compensatory rate of interest on tax avoided from 20% per annum to 14.026% per annum; and

(iii) the reduction of the culpability factor in each year from 80% of tax avoided to 60% of tax avoided.

and that amounts approximating figures so calculated be levied.

35. In consequence the Orders of the Tribunal will be that taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1985 will be reduced by $800 to $48,242 and additional tax by $5,473 to $15,000; and that for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 taxable income will be reduced by $46,170 to $85,248 and additional tax by $30,875 to $25,000.
