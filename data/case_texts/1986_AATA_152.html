Re Robert John Dakin and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 152 (10 June 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ROBERT JOHN DAKIN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A85/31

AAT No. 2701

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

A.R. Neaves J. (Presidential Member)

E. Smith (Senior Member)

Dr. D.B. Travers (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Benefit classification certificate - Test to be applied in determining whether person not likely by reason of a disclosed physical or mental condition to continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age - Whether applicant properly described as having a mental condition - Anxiety state - Whether early retirement an event which might well happen.

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16, 154

Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN No.93

Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293

Re Weeks and Commissioner for Superannuation (22 May 1986 - not yet reported)

HEARING

CANBERRA

10:6:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 12 December 1984 varying the decision made on 21 August 1980 to issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant be set aside and in lieu thereof the Tribunal decides that the benefit classification certificate dated 21 August 1980 issued in respect of the applicant be revoked.

DECISION

The applicant, Dr R.J. Dakin, has applied to the Tribunal pursuant to sub-s.29(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) for the review of a decision made by the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") on 12 December 1984 following a review, under sub-s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act"), of a decision made on 21 August 1980 that a benefit classification certificate issue in respect of the applicant under sub-s.16(4) of the Act. As a result of the review, the benefit classification certificate was varied by substituting the condition "History of Anxiety" for the condition previously specified, namely "History of Depression".

2. By virtue of sub-s.16(2) of the Act, the Commissioner may, for the purposes of s.16, require a person (with certain exceptions immaterial for present purposes) who proposes to become or becomes an eligible employee to undergo such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner determines. A report of the result of any medical examination which the person is required to undergo is to be furnished to the Commissioner (sub-s.16(3)). Sub-section 16(4) provides:

"(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report

or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of

the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason

of or for a reason connected with a physical or

mental condition or conditions referred to in the

report or reports, to continue to be an eligible

employee until the person attains his maximum

retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit

classification certificate to that effect, being a

certificate in which the relevant condition or

conditions is or are specified."

3. Sub-section 154(4) of the Act provides that, upon the receipt by the Commissioner of a request by a person affected by a reviewable decision to reconsider that decision, the Commissioner is to reconsider the decision and "may confirm or revoke the decision or vary the decision in such manner as he thinks fit". The expression "reviewable decision" clearly includes a decision to issue a benefit classification certificate under sub-s.16(4).

4. The applicant was born on 24 September 1938. He became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 23 April 1979 upon his appointment as a Principal Research Scientist with the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization.

5. In January 1979 the applicant had undergone a medical examination by an Australian Government Medical Officer (an approved medical practitioner within the meaning of that expression in s.16 of the Act) at the Australian High Commission, London in connection with his application for appointment to the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization and for the purposes of s.16 of the Act. In a statement of personal medical history completed by the applicant in connection with that examination, the applicant disclosed, amongst other things, that in 1971 he had suffered from infectious hepatitis resulting in an absence from work of 3 months and that, following the attack of hepatitis, he had, in 1971-1972, been treated for depression. The latter, however, did not involve any absence from work.

6. The examining doctor noted this information and sought advice from Dr D.L. Parker who was the applicant's general practitioner. Dr Parker replied by letter dated 6 February 1979 which contained the following information concerning the applicant:

"He has been a patient of mine since December 1971.

He first complained to me of depression in 1973 and

he said he had been depressed from time to time for

many years. I gave him a course of imipramine and

he responded well to treatment. Starting on

11.5.73 he took 75mg imipramine per day for six

weeks then continued to take 1 or 2 tablets per day

until September 1973.

In January 1974 he was depressed again after an

attack of flu but I prescribed no antidepressant.

He recovered without treatment.

In August 1974 he complained of poor sleep,

irritability and an overactive mind. He denied

feeling depressed and I gave him some Valium which

he took in a dose not exceeding 15mg per day. For

a few times a year for the next few years, usually

when under stress at work, he came and collected 30

5mg tablets of Valium which he used to help him

sleep. He has not needed to take a tablet every

night.

I have not found it necessary to refer him for

specialist psychiatric advice or treatment.

Interference of day to day activities has been

minimal and he has not missed any time off work

because of his depression. He is in good health at

present."

7. The examining medical officer commented, in relation to the depression:

"No recurrence. Depression lasted over a period of

6-8/12 - two attacks - strictly related to the

hepatitis. Not hospitalised - treated at home by

GP and was able to continue work. Was living in a

two-bedroomed house with 3 children at the time in

Harwell."

On the basis of the examination the examining officer considered that the applicant met the general medical standard. The Chief Medical Officer, Australian High Commission, London, in his capacity as Adviser to the Commissioner, concurred. The general medical standard forms part of the Handbook for Commonwealth Medical Officers and is designed to assist in the classification of new contributors to the Superannuation Fund and serves as a guide in assessing in a particular case whether to recommend the issue of a benefit classification certificate.

8. By letter dated 21 February 1979 from the Personnel Officer of the Australian High Commission, the applicant was informed as follows:

"I have received the result of your recent medical

and chest x-ray examination and am pleased to

advise that the result is entirely satisfactory and

your appointment to CSIRO is now confirmed. I have

enclosed the x-ray plate and certificate for you to

take with you when you travel to Australia."

Superannuation was not mentioned in that letter.

9. The report of the medical examination was received in the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office on 10 October 1979 and was subsequently considered by a delegate of the Commissioner pursuant to sub-s.16(4) of the Act. The delegate decided that a benefit classification certificate should issue in respect of the applicant specifying the condition "History of Depression". Notification of the issue of the certificate was given to the applicant by letter dated 21 August 1980.

10. By letter dated 28 August 1980 the applicant expressed his dissatisfaction with the decision to issue the benefit classification certificate more than 18 months after his entry medical examination and after having been informed that the result of that examination was "entirely satisfactory". He asserted that there was no basis for the conclusion that he had a history of depression and requested that the certificate be cancelled. The applicant's letter was accepted as a formal notice in writing pursuant to s.154 of the Act requesting the Commissioner to reconsider the previous decision.

11. There was a lengthy delay in dealing with the request to reconsider which has not been wholly explained. It was, however, to some extent attributable to the applicant's decision not to give appropriate authorities for the release of medical information by the doctors whom he had consulted since arriving in Australia. It is, however, unnecessary for present purposes to examine further the causes of the delay and we do not propose to do so.

12. In November 1983 a decision was taken that the applicant be examined by a specialist psychiatrist and Dr B.C. Bromhead was requested to arrange for the applicant to be examined by Dr B.J. Hughson. Dr Hughson saw the applicant on 15 December 1983 and furnished to Dr Bromhead a report dated 20 December 1983 reading -

"I saw Mr Dakin at your request on the 15th

December, 1983.

He told me that he experienced an episode of

depression in 1971 following a 3 month illness with

infectious hepatitis. He had apparently contracted

hepatitis in Papua New Guinea and continued to be

ill with this condition after his arrival in the

United Kingdom. His physical ill health lasted

approximately 3 months and depression tendered

(sic) to become more marked towards the end of this

time. He was initially treated for depression by a

General Practitioner with Amitriptyline. He did

not recollect that it continued for as long as the

medical report from Dr Parker indicates but he does

not deny the accuracy of that report. As best he

can recollect it in the light of Dr Parker's

report, this initial period of treatment with

Amitriptyline was not particularly successful and

his depression continued until he saw Dr Parker in

1973. He had not been taking drug treatment for

some time (but he can no longer remember the length

of time) and Dr Parker started him on Imipramine

(Tofranil). He recalls the feelings at the time as

being some loss of energy, a rather pervasive

gloominess and withdrawal from other people. He

said the Tofranil was helpful and his symptoms

remitted during this course of treatment and have

not recurred since.

He says that his subsequent symptoms were of a

different kind and did not contain a depressive

component. He told me that he works in a stressful

occupation and is aware of stress from time to

time, which he will feel as tiredness,

irritability, difficulty in switching off his

thoughts and occasional problems with insomnia.

From time to time he has taken occasional doses of

Valium to relieve these symptoms, and specifically

the insomnia. He said that he has not taken any

Valium in the last few years, although he still

experiences stress symptoms from time to time. He

had some persistent GIT symptoms a year or so ago

and a diagnosis of Giardia infestation was made

after a series of investigations. He occasionally

has a skin rash, the nature of which seems

uncertain.

I note that he has not taken sick leave for

depression. The family and other personal history

was non contributory.

OPINION

I consider that a distinction should be made in

respect of the condition that occurred in 1971

through to 1973 which has been called depression,

and the symptoms for which he had been prescribed

Valium in 1974 and there after. The first

condition the diagnosis of depression was made and

the symptoms he recalls now are consistent with

that diagnosis, and I therefore agree that it is

correct to state that he had a history of

depression prior to his medical examination in

1979. There is a recognised association between

certain viral infections, specifically infectious

hepatitis, infectious mononucleosis, and possibly

influenza, and depressive illness. There is an

assumption that something about the nature of these

viral infections causes the depressive illness. To

the best of my knowledge there is no firm

scientific evidence to show whether, when a

depressive illness follows such an infection, it

thereafter pursues the natural history evident in

depressive illness occurring in other circumstances

(specifically characterised by a tendency to

recur), or whether it is more commonly a single

episode. My own belief is that it is more commonly

a single episode. Any one psychiatrist will have

only seen a few such patients, because the numbers

are not large and the majority are treated by their

general medical attendants. The observation that

there has been no recurrence in nearly 10 years

would tend to support this view in his case,

although of course this support comes from a

retrospective judgement. I believe the symptoms

described by Dr Parker as occurring in 1974 and for

which Valium was used are not connected with

depression and are manifestations of trait anxiety.

Both from his history and his presentation he

impresses me as a hard working highly geared up

sort of a person who is inevitably going to

experience stress from time to time, particularly

given the nature of the work he does and level of

responsibility that he carries. No reference was

made to this as a separate entity in his original

medical examination and I assume that it is not a

matter for decision at the present time. I

therefore further assume that it is only necessary

for me to express the view that these symptoms are,

in my opinion, not connected with the condition

'History of depression'.

In respect of the specific question posed in

Paragraph 5 of the Commissioner's letter to you, it

is therefore my opinion, on the balance of

probabilities, that Mr Dakin is not likely by

reason of, or for a reason connected with "History

of depression", to cease to continue to be an

eligible employee before he attains his maximum

retiring age."

Dr Bromhead expressed himself as being in agreement with Dr Hughson's opinion.

13. In view of Dr Hughson's comments concerning the distinction he drew between Dr Dakin's episode of depression and the apparent "trait anxiety", Dr D. Jarvis was approached on 4 June 1984 for details of the applicant's recent medical history. By a report received by the Commissioner on 20 June 1984 Dr Jarvis reported:

"I have been the regular medical attendant of Dr

Dakin for the past three years. During that time

he has presented with a number of conditions that

had an anxiety component. For instance, in

September 1981, he requested a 'check-up' because

he had been feeling tired and had headaches. He

was also experiencing some chest pains on exertion.

Full physical examination was normal, as was an

electrocardiograph. In November 1982, he

complained of diarrhoea, and as this complaint

persisted on and off for some months he was

referred to Dr T. Gavaghan, a gastro-enterologist.

Dr Gavaghan's last report to me, dated 11 April

1984 says inter alia 'I feel that his symptoms are

almost certainly a consequence of anxiety and an

irritable colon syndrome ...'

I have never treated Dr Dakin for depression, and

would not now regard him as being depressed. With

reference to the prescription of Valium in 1974, I

could only add that this is not a drug used for

depression, as it often aggravates these symptoms.

It is of course normally used for the management of

mild anxiety.

I cannot of course comment on the original history

of depression, but it would seem to me that

'history of anxiety' would be a much more logical

classification."

14. Dr Hughson made a further report dated 5 October 1984 which read in part:

"You will recall that following my interview with

Dr Dakin I identified symptoms present since 1974

as anxiety symptoms and saw them as an indication

of the presence of trait anxiety. By trait anxiety

I mean a relatively fixed predisposition of the

person to develop anxiety symptoms, with the

symptoms themselves usually occurring in response

to stress. The stresses may well be minor ones.

My opinion has been further strengthened by the

medical reports you enclosed from Dr Jarvis,

identifying the presence of anxiety symptoms during

consultations over the last few years. In addition

Dr Jarvis quotes Dr Gavaghan who expressed the view

that certain bowel symptoms were almost certainly a

consequence of anxiety.

I believe the medical evidence supports the view

that Dr Dakin has a 'history of anxiety' and that

this condition has been present since 1974. He has

not been free of symptoms in the last three years.

His history increases the probability that he would

not remain an eligible employee until he reached

his maximum retiring age of 65 years."

15. Having considered the medical reports referred to above, the Commissioner, pursuant to sub-s.154(4) of the Act, varied the decision taken on 21 August 1980 to issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the condition "History of Depression" by substituting for that condition the condition "History of Anxiety". In a document dated 12 December 1984 the Commissioner recorded the reasons for the conclusion he had reached in the following terms:

"Both Dr Hughson and Dr Jarvis have pointed to a

distinction between the symptoms from which Dr

Dakin suffered in the period 1971 to 1973 and those

which have occurred since 1974 requiring treatment

with Valium. It is now apparent that the former

symptoms constituted a period of true depression

related to a viral infection (hepatitis) and in all

probability was not of a recurrent nature. However

the latter symptoms - originally identified by Dr

Parker as poor sleep, irritability and an

overactive mind and, more recently, manifesting as

related bowel symptoms - are, I conclude,

indicative of a longstanding trait predisposing Dr

Dakin to develop anxiety symptoms when under

stress.

Accordingly, although I am satisfied that Dr Dakin

had indeed at one stage suffered from the condition

'depression' and therefore could be said to have a

'History of Depression', thus justifying the

delegate's decision (certainly at the time it was

made), noting the consensus of favourable medical

opinion which discounts the significance of

'depression', I am not of the opinion that he is

not likely to remain an eligible employee until he

attains his maximum retiring age by reason of or

for a reason connected with that condition.

However, having regard to the information available

to me, including the comments by Dr Hughson and Dr

Jarvis, particularly insofar as his current health

can be related to symptoms he exhibited prior to

becoming an eligible employee on 23 Apr 1979 and

which were undoubtedly taken into account by the

delegate in deciding to issue the benefit

classification certificate, I am of the opinion

that Dr Dakin is not likely, by reason of, or for a

reason connected with the condition 'History of

Anxiety' to continue to be an eligible employee

until he attains his maximum retiring age. As a

consequence I have varied the benefit

classification certificate issued by the delegate

vide paragraph one above since this is clearly a

more accurate description of the nature of Dr

Dakin's condition."

16. By letter dated 10 March 1985 addressed to the Registrar of the Tribunal (and received by him on 12 March 1985), the applicant sought a review of the Commissioner's decision. On 29 April 1985 an order was made extending to 12 March 1985 the time for lodging the application for review.

17. On the hearing before the Tribunal the applicant conducted his case in person while the Commissioner was represented by Mr A. Anforth. The applicant gave oral evidence and tendered, without objection, certain further medical reports, being -

(a) a report dated 1 February 1985 by

Professor A.C.P. Sims of the Department

of Psychiatry, St James' University

Hospital, Leeds;

(b) a report dated 24 September 1985 by Dr

S.J. Rosenman, psychiatrist;

(c) a report dated 18 May 1986 by Dr J.F.H.

Foo.

Dr Rosenman also gave oral evidence.

18. Professor Sims' report is lengthy but should be set out in full. It reads:

"This report has been produced after perusal of the

document Reference No.252-56205 dated the 19th

December, 1984, produced by the Commissioner for

Superannuation, Australian Government Retirement

Benefits Office. As Dr Dakin's initial submission

was made whilst he was in the United Kingdom, it

was considered appropriate to report on the use

that has been made of medical information derived

from British National Health Service practice.

1. The whole matter of Dr Dakin's medical

record was provoked initially by Dr

Dakin's frankness in describing his

treatment in general practice of a

psychiatric condition. Virtually the

whole population in the United Kingdom

is registered with a general

practitioner in the National Health

Service. Within the National Health

Service general practitioners are able

to refer all patients to appropriate

specialists without financial constraint

to the patient. Whether a condition

that is wholly treated in general

practice should be regarded as a

psychiatric illness is debatable. The

total psychiatric morbidity in general

practice in the United Kingdom is 230

patients per 1,000 general population

per year; that is, nearly one quarter of

the population make complaint almost

exclusively of anxiety or depression or

both to their general practitioner each

year. This means that Dr Dakin's

initial complaint of psychological

symptoms whilst in the United Kingdom

put him in a group that included a

quarter of the whole population and,

without further referral to specialist

services, it would be implied that the

prognosis for his condition was exactly

the same as the rest of that quarter of

the population. Insurance companies in

the United Kingdom do not now weight

against such histories of psychological

disturbance.

2. The diagnosis of depression following

infective hepatitis (or perhaps

infective mononucleosis) does not fulfil

the criteria in Item 8, page 3, of the

Commissioner's report for standards not

being met. In Item 8a, depression was

not either chronic or recurrent. In

Item 8b there was virtually no sick

leave required apart from that for the

original physical illness. In Item 8c

there has been complete freedom from

depression for many years. In Item 8d

the standards are met. Item 8e is not

applicable.

3. The original diagnosis of depression was

reported by Dr Dakin, confirmed by Dr

Parker, General Practitioner, noted by

the examining AGMO, and it was

considered by the AGMO that Dr Dakin met

the general medical standard.

Subsequently the Commissioner has

excluded the diagnosis of depression as

significant for purposes of

superannuation. However,

retrospectively, the Commissioner has

imposed a diagnosis of anxiety, not only

on Dr Dakin's medical history subsequent

to his removal to Australia, but also by

implication to the time of his residence

in the United Kingdom. This diagnosis

was not made by Dr Parker nor by the

AGMO and is quite unjustifiable from the

account of treatment in the United

Kingdom. If the diagnosis of depression

which was found not to be relevant for

superannuation purposes had not been

investigated, then the subsequent

diagnosis of anxiety would never have

been made. It seems unjustifiable

retrospectively to vary the diagnosis of

another doctor in another country.

4. The current 'diagnosis' is of trait

anxiety. This diagnosis does not exist

in either of the acceptable

classifications of psychiatric illness

(International Classification of

Diseases, 9th Revision, WHO, and

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of

Mental Disorders, 3rd Edition, American

Psychiatric Association); nor does it

occur as a psychiatric diagnosis in

standard text-books within the generic

categories of either psychosis or

psycho-neurosis. The reason for this is

that 'trait anxiety' means neither more

nor less than a capacity for becoming

anxious. There is no suggestion in the

Commissioner's Report that this trait

anxiety in Dr Dakin's case is either

evidence of illness, within any

reasonable meaning of the word

'illness', nor even abnormal, using

'normal' in a statistical sense. A

label has been used therefore to

describe Dr Dakin, which is not a

diagnosis of any illness, nor even a

description of a condition that is

outside normal limits.

5. In the Commissioner's Report, Item 8,

page 3, none of a-e would imply that Dr

Dakin has failed to meet the standard.

The use of the term 'trait anxiety'

would not imply failing to meet the

standard in any of these subsections.

6. The question must be asked how could the

designation 'trait anxiety' affect Dr

Dakin's prognosis to result in premature

retirement on grounds of ill health.

From my extensive research and

publications on the topic of prognosis

in neurotic disorders, I could say

categorically that there is no evidence

that 'trait anxiety' carries a poor

prognosis tending towards either

premature mortality or severe morbidity.

Any link between so-called trait anxiety

and premature retirement on health

grounds is purely speculative and

without psychiatric evidence.

7. In the paragraph 'Results' on page 2 of

the Commissioner's Report, the phrases

'history of depression' and 'history of

anxiety' are used. Depression, although

imprecise as a term, can be accepted as

a psychiatric diagnosis; however

'anxiety' without qualification cannot

be accepted as a diagnosis and does not

exist in psychiatric classification.

Anxiety state or anxiety neurosis would

be regarded as a psychiatric diagnosis

but these terms have never been applied

to Dr Dakin. Trait anxiety, which has

been applied to him, is not a

psychiatric diagnosis.

8. OPINION: In my opinion the decision

concerning superannuation for Dr Dakin

reached by the Commissioner for

Superannuation is unjustifiable and from

the Commissioner's own Report Dr Dakin

would be regarded as psychiatrically

normal:

1. History of depression has been

removed.

2. History of anxiety is not a

psychiatric diagnosis.

3. Trait anxiety is not a

psychiatric illness and does not

place Dr Dakin outside normal

limits.

I am approved under Section 12, Sub-Section 2, of

the Mental Health Act, UK (1983). I am the author

of three books and more than 60 professional

publications in psychiatry; my book 'Neurosis in

Society' and many papers have dealt specifically

with the outcome in neurotic disorders and related

conditions. I am Sub-Dean and Chairman of the

Research Committee of the Royal College of

Psychiatrists. I am a member of the Standing

Medical Advisory Committee of the Department of

Health and Social Security and of the Medical

Committee on Accident Prevention. I am an external

assessor for the Australian Medical Research

Council."

Professor Sims was not available to give oral evidence or to be cross-examined on his report. It should be stated that Professor Sims is the applicant's brother-in-law and that relationship, coupled with his unavailability for cross-examination, requires his report to be treated with some caution.

19. Dr Rosenman's report is also lengthy but it, too, should be set out in full. It states:

"Thank you for referring Dr Dakin and for the

extensive documentation which accompanied him.

The features of the history which stood out were:

(1) The history of 'depression' following an

episode of infectious hepatitis. This

picture was dominated by lassitude and

gloominess. Dr Dakin was not able to

recall more detail about his mental

state at the time. He was treated

initially with Amitriptyline and

subsequently with Imipramine and this

treatment continued for at least 2 years

following the episode of hepatitis.

(2) From Dr Dakin's own account he has

suffered occasional disturbances of

sleep which have been treated with

Valium 2.5 mg intermittently since 1974.

He has had repeated prescriptions of

Valium over that time but since coming

to Australia in 1979 has virtually

ceased its use. He states that his

sleep disturbance has not been

associated with depression or anxiety

but is associated with work related over

stimulation.

(3) According to Dr Parker's report of July

1982, Dr Dakin received presciptions for

820 5 mg tablets between August 1974 and

April 1979. He initially used 5 mg

then 2.5 mg. Dr Dakin claims that

regular usage was rarely more than

alternate nights. From this, however,

it cannot be assumed that he was

suffering continual symptoms of anxiety.

The body develops tolerance to Valium

with regular usage and one could predict

that cessation of the Valium would have

produced sleeplessness in its own right

and not require the presence of

diagnosable anxiety.

(4) According to Dr Dakin, between 1982 and

1984 he suffered recurring symptoms of

abdominal pain and diahorrea. This

condition was initially diagnosed as

Giardiasis and the initial treatment

with Metronidazol produced improvement.

There were a number of recurrences the

last of which did not respond to

treatment with Metronidazol and was

treated symptomatically with Colofac.

The abdominal symptoms produced some

sleep disturbance. He does not know

where he may have contracted Giardiasis

as no other members of the family

experienced similar symptoms.

(5) According to Dr Jarvis' report (undated,

your reference T23/1) Dr Dakin presented

in September 1981 requesting a check-up

and at that check-up complained of

lassitude, headache and chest pain on

exertion. In between 1982 and 1984 his

abdominal symptoms were diagnosed as

'irritable colon syndrome' and were

reported to be 'almost certainly a

consequence of anxiety'.

(6) In Dr Dakin's account of his own history

preceding his hepatitis in 1971 there

was no previous history of depression.

Nor any period of disabling anxiety. He

has always been a high achiever but

despite this he denies either abnormal

anxiety preceding examinations or public

performances and no symptoms that have,

in any way, interfered with his work.

There is no definite family history of

psychiatric illness.

(7) Currently Dr Dakin's only complaint is

of occasional disturbed sleep. He

claims this occurs only intermittently

and is associated with over-stimulation

rather than anxiety from work. He

claims that his sleep is never disturbed

for more than one or two nights in

succession. He has had recent brief

recurrences of his abdominal symptoms

but has never been disabled by these.

He is a motivated and successful worker

in the CSIRO. He has continued to move

up the ladder of seniority and has

mastered the increased levels of

responsibilities that go with that

seniority.

OPINION

On a number of occasions since 1974 Dr Dakin has

sought medical assistance with symptoms which are

probably related to anxiety. In saying this I

acknowledge that, in terms of sleep disturbance,

Valium, by virtue of tolerance, can convert

transient disturbances of sleeping to an apparent

recurrent sleep disturbance which occurs whenever

the Valium is ceased. While 'irritable colon

syndrome' appears to be well related to anxiety

there appears at least a possibility of doubt of

the certainty of the diagnosis of 'irritable

colon'.

The trait of anxiety is distributed variably

through the population and, as Professor Sims

observes, possibly 25% of the population will seek

medical attention for anxiety related symptoms at

some time. On these grounds, seeking attention for

anxiety symptoms cannot be called 'abnormal' in the

sense it does not fall outside the expected

behaviour of 96% of the population (two standard

deviations from the mean). However Dr Dakin's

symptoms have been recurrent and have been

sufficient to cause him to seek medical attention

on a number of occasions.

In response to your specific question on 'trait

anxiety'; this cannot be considered a medical

condition as it represents a dimensional variation

of normal findings in the population. In my

opinion, the symptoms could be considered abnormal

if they are severe enough either to take them

beyond the bounds of statistical normality or if

they cause significant disability. On neither

ground do I consider that Dr Dakin's symptoms

constitute an illness.

However his symptoms have been recurrent. It is my

belief that the period of maximum risk of

experiencing these symptoms occurs between the ages

of 25 and 40. The propensity to experience

symptoms tends to decrease except in cases where

increasing age is associated with increasing stress

with the progression to jobs of increasing levels

of responsibility. I feel that Dr Dakin has

traversed the first period of risk and has largely

mastered his symptoms.

I cannot state unequivocally that he does meet the

Medical Standard. He has had psychoneurotic

symptoms. While they do not, in my opinion,

constitute an illness, his symptoms have been

recurrent and he has not been free of recurrence or

free of symptoms for 3 to 5 years.

Moreover paragraph 1(e) of the Medical Standard

forces me to take account of the fact that he is

likely to continue to progress into more

responsible positions with increasing demands and

stress. Therefore, potentially, he could be

exposing himself to the risk of symptom recurrence

later. Despite this, I believe that there is

almost no chance that he will be incapacitated for

work by these symptoms before the age of 65 or that

he will be disabled from work by these symptoms for

any period before the normal age of retirement."

20. Dr Foo reported:

"Dr Robert Dakin (dob 24.9.38) has been known to me

since 12th July 1985.

In March 1983, he was investigated by Dr Terry

Gavaghan for bowel symptoms. All investigations

were negative and a diagnosis of irritable bowel

syndrome was made.

On the 22.7.85 and 20.8.85 he consulted me with

symptoms of intermittent diarrhoea, lower abdominal

discomfort and lethargy. Examination was

unremarkable on both occasions and stool

examination was negative. He was also suffering

from intermittent episodes of hives. In view of

these latter symptoms, it was felt that a 'food

allergy' was probable and he was advised to go on

an 'elimination diet' (which excluded additives,

preservatives and colouring) and a milk-free diet.

At subsequent reviews, the last time being 10.4.86

he has not had any recurrence of his bowel

symptoms.

It is generally accepted that 'irritable bowel

syndrome' is not a definitive diagnosis and that

there are several causes of the condition. It is

also accepted that 'food allergy' can cause a small

percentage of these cases although some doctors

believe that this percentage is much higher.

Anxiety has been accepted as a major cause of

irritable bowel syndrome but I have not seen any

symptoms or signs of that in my year of association

with Dr Dakin. Earlier in his illness, there was

some suggestion in the history that he had a

giardia infection; irritable bowel syndrome can

follow these infections and food allergy may be

contributing factor.

In my opinion, it is probable that Dr Dakin

suffered from an 'irritable bowel syndrome' which

appears to have responded very well to dietary

manipulation."

Dr Foo was, apparently, reluctant to give oral evidence and he was not called as a witness.

21. As part of the Commissioner's case, Dr Hughson gave oral evidence as did Dr Bromhead. Dr Hughson is currently employed as the director of mental health services with the Australian Capital Territory Health Authority and has a limited right of private practice as a clinical psychiatrist. He said that in his report dated 5 October 1984 he had used the expression "history of anxiety" as synonymous with the neurotic condition "history of anxiety state". He agreed that the word "anxiety" in common usage denotes a mood or state of an individual with no medical implication at all. He also agreed that the word, standing alone, does not exist in psychiatric diagnostic classification. However, he said that he used the word in its medical usage to identify a collection of either psychological or physical symptoms - the former including subjective feelings of tension, irritability, a feeling of being overly stressed and a feeling of anxiousness or apprehensiveness and the latter including such things as tremor, palpitation, shortness of breath, diarrhoea and frequency of urination - occurring in an individual either recurrently or, if in a single episode, severely. He said that the symptoms usually had a relationship to some, perhaps only relatively minor, stressful stimulus or precipitant in the person's environment. He added that for the most part people who develop the neurotic condition have personal vulnerabilities which are characteristics of their personality. Thus, the more vulnerable the person is by virtue of his personality, the greater the range of stimuli or precipitants which will produce symptoms.

22. Dr Hughson explained that, when he first examined the applicant on 15 December 1983, he diagnosed anxiety symptoms since 1974 and a previous and unrelated depressive episode. He said that, in stating in his report of 20 December 1983 that the applicant's symptoms were "manifestations of trait anxiety", he meant to convey the observation of a relatively fixed predisposition in the applicant to develop anxiety symptoms in response to a stimulus or precipitant. He said that the symptoms manifested in the period following 1974 constituted an "anxiety state". Those symptoms were awareness of stress, tiredness, irritability, difficulty in switching off, insomnia (for which Valium was taken) and the symptoms referred to by Dr Jarvis in his report of 20 June 1984, including the symptoms for which the applicant was treated by Dr Gavaghan.

23. Asked what conclusion he would draw concerning the applicant's chances of being invalided out of the public service before age 65, Dr Hughson answered:

"Not very many. I think the risk, I believe, is

increased but I cannot say by how much the risk is

increased."

He added that before he could say that it was more probable than not that a person would be invalided out of the public service because of "anxiety state", the symptoms would have to be either very severe or moderately severe and persistent or frequently recurrent with, in all probability, an established history of absence from work.

24. Speaking of the applicant, Dr Hughson said that his level of anxiety was "at the most mild". In cross-examination he said:

"I think it can be said that the chance of a

premature retirement is increased given that there

is a history of anxiety symptoms but I do not

believe that a figure or a probability can be

assigned to it ...."

He added:

"I certainly would not think it was more probable

than not, more likely than not."

Pressed to be more specific, he expressed the view that it was "of a low order rather than a high order".

25. Dr Bromhead, who described his current position as that of Senior Medical Officer in the Department of Health, Canberra, explained that an applicant for permanent appointment to the public service is examined by a Commonwealth medical officer who advises the Public Service Board, against a set of medical criteria contained in the Commonwealth Medical Officers Handbook, whether or not the applicant is medically fit for such permanent appointment. As a result of the examination and a consideration of the applicant's disclosed medical history, the officer also makes a recommendation to the Commissioner for superannuation purposes.

26. Dr Rosenman is staff psychiatrist at the Woden Valley Hospital in Canberra and a research scholar at the Australian National University. He also has a right of private practice. He agreed that the applicant had over the years shown a propensity to manifest anxiety symptoms and in this regard he instanced intermittent disturbance of sleep, the symptoms suggestive of depression and the abdominal symptoms which were diagnosed as irritable colon. He drew attention to the intermittent nature of the symptoms and considered that in those circumstances the inscription of a cause for the onset or the remission of the symptoms was somewhat speculative. However, he considered that the applicant's level of anxiety was in the area of anxiety experienced by the more anxious one half to one third of the population so that his general level of anxiety would place him in the top 30 to 50 per cent. of the population.

27. Dr Rosenman said that in his opinion the risk that the applicant would, by reason of his propensity to manifest anxiety symptoms, not continue to be an eligible employee until his maximum retiring age, while greater than that of the 50 to 70 per cent. of the population who do not have as high a level of anxiety as the applicant, was very small - a bare possibility. He expressed the view that a significant risk would occur in that group of the population who are abnormally anxious, that is to say, the group who are significantly incapacitated by anxiety symptoms or who fall into that level of anxiety state greater than 96 per cent. of the population, that being the accepted statistical variation that takes people into the region of abnormality.

28. On the basis of the symptoms of which he had been made aware - and these were, in essence, the same as those made known to Dr Hughson - Dr Rosenman was not prepared to make a diagnosis of anxiety neurosis. He pointed, in particular, to the fact that, although the symptoms had been recurrent, the applicant had never experienced panic attacks and there were no periods of disabling anxiety. Such a diagnosis would require, he said, an intensity of symptoms and symptoms that were sustained and incapacitating. Speaking of the applicant he said:

"I do not think you have a diagnosable condition.

I think you have symptoms."

29. Having considered the material available to him, the Commissioner was of opinion that the applicant was not likely, by reason of the specified condition "History of Anxiety", to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retiring age. In our opinion, the expression "History of Anxiety", if unexplained, cannot be said to be a "physical or mental condition" within the meaning of those words in sub-s.16(4) of the Act. However, as the Commissioner clearly derived the expression from Dr Hughson's report dated 5 October 1984 and Dr Hughson's oral evidence has explained the sense in which the expression was there used, we think it is appropriate to consider the issues raised on the basis that the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate as varied by the Commissioner is "History of Anxiety State".

30. Reference has already been made to some of the provisions of s.16 of the Act and the text of sub-s.16(4) has been set out. Before proceeding further, reference should be made to some of the later sub-sections.

31. Sub-section 16(6) provides that an eligible employee in respect of whom a benefit classification certificate is in force may request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate. An employee making such a request is required to undergo such further medical examinations (if any) as the Commissioner requires. Reports of the results of such examinations are to be furnished to the Commissioner (sub-s.16(7)). Sub-section 16(8) provides:

"(8) The Commissioner shall consider the report

or reports (if any), and such other matters as he

considers relevant, and -

(a) where there is not more than 1 physical

or mental condition specified in the

benefit classification certificate and

the Commissioner is of the opinion that

the person is not likely, by reason of

or for a reason connected with that

condition, to cease to continue to be an

eligible employee before he attains his

maximum retiring age - the Commissioner

shall revoke the certificate;

(b) where there is more than 1 physical or

mental condition specified in the

benefit classification certificate -

(i) if the Commissioner is of the

opinion that the person is not

likely, by reason of or for a

reason connected with those

conditions, to cease to continue

to be an eligible employee before

the person attains his maximum

retiring age - the Commissioner

shall revoke the certificate; or

(ii) if the Commissioner is of the

opinion that the person is not

likely, by reason of or for a

reason connected with 1 or some

only of the conditions specified

in the certificate, to continue

to be an eligible employee until

he attains his maximum retiring

age - the Commissioner shall vary

the certificate by deleting the

reference in the certificate to

any other condition or conditions

so specified; and

(c) in any other case - the Commissioner

shall refuse the request to revoke or

vary the benefit classification

certificate."

32. In Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN No.93 the Tribunal, as then constituted, concluded that the Parliament, when referring in sub-ss.16(4) and 16(8) of the Act to a person who is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition, to continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age, was to be taken to be referring "to conditions which give rise to a 'real risk' and not merely to conditions which create a 'probability' or 'better than even chance' that the person will not continue until attaining maximum retiring age". The Tribunal went on (p.661):

"There is no question in our minds that where the

medical evidence establishes a probability or

'better than even chance' that a particular

condition will prove disabling before a person

attains maximum retiring age, the Commissioner is

entitled, if not obliged, to issue a certificate.

But we are unable to accept that the section limits

the Commissioner to issuing a certificate only

where such a degree of certainty is established.

The general medical standards developed for the

guidance of Commonwealth medical officers, which

were designed at establishing acceptable levels of

risk, are as we understand them, consistent with

that broader view. Failure to meet the general

medical standards may not render it 'probable' that

a person will not continue in employment until

maximum retiring age. But such failure does

suggest that, if the condition persists, early

death or retirement on grounds of invalidity is an

event which might well happen."

33. A different view was, however, taken by the Tribunal, differently constituted, in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293. At p 306 the Tribunal, referring to sub-s.16(4), said:

"The meaning of the words in question seems to us

to be clear. It is that in order to form the

relevant opinion there must be a positive

satisfaction that the person 'is not likely to

continue' etc. There must be a greater likelihood

that the person will not so continue than that he

or she will. Unless this be the case, how can it

be opined that the person is not likely to

continue? It is in our view crucial that the

phrase 'not likely' be kept intact, and be regarded

as a composite expression. The word 'not' should

not be detached and emphasis placed on the meaning

of the word 'likely'. In consequence we consider

that it is not possible to form the opinion in

question until it can be said that it is more

probable than not that the person will not continue

until attainment of maximum retiring age."

34. After referring to a number of authorities on the meaning of the word "likely", the Tribunal said, at p.311-2:

"We have to say also that we do not consider it

correct to adopt an interpretative approach which

discards the word 'not' and construes the word

'likely' standing on its own, in so doing

introducing connotations of that word that are

quite inapposite when the total phrase, in

particular the expression 'not likely', is in

question. The presence of the word 'not' makes it

very difficult to read into the word 'likely' the

connotation 'such as may well happen'. It follows

that in our opinion the requisite opinion cannot be

formed unless it is found that it is more probable

than not that the person in question will not

continue to his or her maximum retiring age."

35. The decision reached in Re Bewley and the Commissioner for Superannuation as to the meaning to be given to sub-s.16(4) of the Act was followed by the Tribunal in Re Weeks and Commissioner for Superannuation (22 May 1986 - not yet reported).

36. The nature of the opinion which the Commissioner (or the Tribunal in proceedings by way of review) must form in order that a benefit classification certificate shall issue was again argued before us. The applicant contended that, following and applying Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation, a benefit classification certificate may not issue unless the opinion is formed that, by reason of or for a reason connected with the disclosed physical or mental condition, it is more probable than not (that is to say, there is more than a 50 per cent. chance) that the eligible employee will not continue as such until attaining his maximum retiring age. In support of the contention that that interpretation of the sub-section is the correct one, the applicant submitted that it was only by giving that meaning to sub-s.16(4) that consistency could be achieved between that sub-section and sub-s.16(8). On the other hand, the Commissioner contended that the interpretation of sub-s.16(4) adopted by the Tribunal in Re Linden and the Commissioner for Superannuation was reasonably open and was to be preferred.

37. There can, of course, be no doubt that, if the opinion is formed that, by reason of or for a reason connected with the disclosed physical or mental condition, it is more probable than not that the eligible employee will not continue as such until attaining his maximum retiring age, it is proper to speak of that person as being "not likely .... to continue to be an eligible employee" until attaining that age within the meaning of sub-s.16(4). So much is clear. What is not so clear is whether it is only in those circumstances that a person may properly be so described.

38. We are unable to agree with the view expressed in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation that the language of sub-s.16(4) is clear and unambiguous. It seems to us that either of the meanings for which the parties respectively contended may be assigned to the language used. The question, then, is whether there are indicia, to be found in other provisions of the statute or in a consideration of the evident purpose and intention of the legislature discerned from the statute as whole or in a consideration of the operation which the adoption of either meaning would give to the statute, which will assist in determining which of the suggested meanings should be ascribed to the provision.

39. We think that some assistance is to be gained from a consideration of the legislative purpose in enacting s.16 of the Act. We accept that the purpose was to provide some protection for the superannuation scheme established by the Act against claims due to the premature invalidity or death of an eligible employee who at the time when he became an eligible employee was suffering from a potentially disabling or fatal physical or mental condition which, in the event, results in his early retirement or death: see Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation supra at p 660. As the Tribunal there pointed out, the protection afforded to the scheme is not absolute since a benefit classification certificate ceases to have any effect at all after the eligible employee has completed 20 years' contributory service (sub-s.16(5)) and has a diminishing effect in the event of death or retirement on the grounds of invalidity where the period of contributory service is not less than 8 years (ss.66, 81).

40. We are also of opinion that guidance as to the true meaning of the provision is to be found in a consideration of the operation which the provision would have depending upon which of the two suggested meanings is adopted. Although sub-s.16(2) refers not only to persons who become eligible employees but also to those who propose to become such, a benefit classification certificate issued pursuant to sub-s.16(4) can have no operative effect in relation to a person unless that person becomes an eligible employee. The expression "eligible employee" is defined in sub-s.3(1) of the Act so as to include those who are employed by the Commonwealth in a permanent capacity under the Public Service Act 1922 (Cth). Those included in that class of eligible employees - one may suppose the most numerous class - will, prior to being so permanently employed, have undergone a medical examination, approved by the Public Service Board, as to their health and physical fitness and will have been certified as medically fit for permanent appointment to the public service. This appears from the evidence of Dr Bromhead. According to the Commissioner's submission, it would be most unlikely that a person of whom it can be said that there is more than a 50 per cent. chance that he will not continue as an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age would be certified as fit for permanent appointment to the public service. Thus, the point was made that to give sub-s.16(4) the meaning contended for by the applicant would result in the sub-section having no capacity for effective operation in relation to that class of eligible employees and would, indeed, result in it having such a limited capacity for operation that such an intention should not, if another construction of the provision be reasonably open, be attributed to the legislature. On the contrary, it was said, to construe sub-s.16(4) in the manner contended for by the Commissioner would result in the sub-section operating so as to permit the issue of a benefit classification certificate in relation to a person falling within any one of the various classes of eligible employees, a result which would more nearly accord with the evident purpose of the provision. We think there is considerable force in this submission.

41. Taking these matters into consideration, it seems to us that the language of sub-s.16(4) is apt to include a case where the opinion is held that, by reason of or for a reason connected with the disclosed condition, death or retirement before attaining the maximum retiring age is an event which may well happen. A person in respect of whom such an opinion is held may, we think, properly be described as one who is "not likely .... to continue to be an eligible employee" for the requisite period. Although the sub-section is expressed in terms of the continuation of a state of affairs in which the person remains an eligible employee, its obvious concern is with the degree of likelihood that, by reason of or for a reason connected with the disclosed condition, that state of affairs will not continue. It is concerned with the degree of likelihood of death or retirement prior to the attainment of the maximum retiring age, either of which events would precipitate a claim for the payment of benefits under the Act. With that background, it does not seem to us to be an inappropriate use of language to say of a person who has a 49 per cent. chance of early retirement that he is a person who is "not likely .... to continue to be an eligible employee" until he attains his maximum retiring age. Yet, on the view contended for by the applicant, a benefit classification certificate could not be issued in respect of such a person. We do not think that the language of the provision compels that conclusion.

42. In the result and with due respect to those who have taken that view, we find ourselves unable to agree with the interpretation of sub-s.16(4) adopted by the Tribunal in Re Bewley and the Commissioner for Superannuation.

43. In reaching that conclusion we have not overlooked the provisions of sub-s.16(8) the text of which has already been set out. That sub-section is concerned with the revocation or variation of a benefit classification certificate consequent upon a request by an eligible employee that the certificate be revoked or varied. While the language of sub-par.(b)(ii) of sub-s.(8) follows closely the language of sub-s.16(4) in referring to the person as one who is not likely to continue to be an eligible employee, par.(a) and sub-par.(b)(i) of sub-s.(8) focus upon the likelihood of the person ceasing to continue as such. It was submitted by the applicant that sub-ss.16(4) and 16(8) were not capable of consistent operation unless sub-s.16(4) is construed in the manner for which he contended.

44. We acknowledge that the change of language to which we have referred gives rise to some difficulty but we think the difficulty remains whichever of the two suggested meanings of sub-s.16(4) is adopted. The existence of the difficulty, therefore, provides no guidance as to the meaning which should be ascribed to sub-s.16(4). It seems to us that, giving to sub-s.16(4) the meaning which we think it has, sub-s.(8) is to be construed as meaning that a benefit classification certificate is to be revoked only where the Commissioner is of opinion that there is no real risk that early death or retirement will occur. Such a construction of the provision is, we think, reasonably open.

45. Applying sub-s.16(4) according to the meaning which we think the provision properly bears, two questions arise. The first is whether the applicant at the date he became an eligible employee, had the mental condition described as "History of Anxiety State". If that question is answered in the affirmative, the further question arises whether the Tribunal is of opinion that there is a real risk that the applicant will, by reason of or for a reason connected with that condition, not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age.

46. In relation to both those questions it is necessary to turn to the medical evidence. In addition to the reports that were before the Commissioner when he made the decision under review, the Tribunal has had the reports of Professor Sims, Dr Rosenman and Dr Foo and, unlike the Commissioner, has had the great advantage of hearing the oral evidence of Dr Hughson and Dr Rosenman.

47. There is no doubt that since 1974 the applicant has from time to time manifested anxiety symptoms. Those manifested prior to the applicant becoming an eligible employee on 23 April 1979 are set out in Dr Parker's letter dated 6 February 1979. Those symptoms were described by Dr Parker as poor sleep, irritability and an overactive mind. The symptoms were not such as to require specialist psychiatric advice or treatment and they did not involve any loss of time from his employment. Since taking up his appointment with the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization there have been a number of occasions on which anxiety symptoms have been manifest. They are described by Dr Jarvis in his report of 20 June 1984 as tiredness, headaches, some chest pains on exertion and, in 1982, diarrhoea. As the latter symptom persisted he was referred to Dr Gavaghan who diagnosed "irritable colon syndrome". It may be noted that Dr Foo, who treated the applicant in 1985 for symptoms of intermittent diarrhoea, lower abdominal discomfort, lethargy and hives, symptoms which in his opinion were attributable to a food allergy, cast some doubt in his report dated 18 May 1986 on the correctness of Dr Gavaghan's diagnosis. However, as the report does not express a definitive view on the matter and as Dr Foo did not give oral evidence, we do not place any reliance on that part of his report. Dr Rosenman in his report also cast some doubt on Dr Gavaghan's diagnosis but in his oral evidence he said that there was only a bare possibility that the diagnosis was incorrect. It is also to be noted that none of the symptoms to which Dr Jarvis and Dr Foo referred resulted in any absence from the applicant's employment.

48. Dr Hughson and Dr Rosenman disagreed as to the conclusion which should be drawn from the symptoms related to anxiety brought on by stress or other stimuli manifested by the applicant over the years. Dr Hughson, while agreeing that the symptoms were mild, thought them sufficient to support a finding of "history of anxiety state". We think it plain from a consideration of the whole of his evidence that he regarded the circumstance that there had been a recurrence of the symptoms as of critical importance.

49. Dr Rosenman, while accepting that the applicant had manifested intermittent psychoneurotic symptoms and that there had been some recurrence of those symptoms over the years, considered that the symptoms were not of such a nature or sufficiently severe to warrant a finding of "history of anxiety state". He pointed particularly to the absence in the applicant's history of any significant disability arising from the onset of the symptoms.

50. In the final analysis, the question is one of degree. In so far as Dr Hughson and Dr Rosenman disagree as to the proper conclusion to be drawn from the relevant symptomatology, we prefer the evidence of Dr Rosenman. In other words, we are not satisfied on the material before us that at the time the applicant became an eligible employee he had the mental condition described in the benefit classification certificate as "History of Anxiety State".

51. We should add that, had we been of the contrary view, we would not have been of the opinion, in terms of sub-s.16(4) construed in the manner to which we have already adverted, that the applicant is a person who is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with the condition "History of Anxiety State", to continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age. We have referred earlier to the oral evidence of Dr Hughson and Dr Rosenman on this aspect of the case. In brief, Dr Hughson was not prepared to articulate the degree of risk of early retirement which he perceived and Dr Rosenman regarded early retirement as a bare possibility.

52. We think the correct conclusion to draw from the material before us is that it cannot properly be said of the applicant that the likelihood of his early retirement is such as to make it an event which might well happen or as to which there is a real risk that it will happen.

53. The applicant also submitted that there was no power in the delegate of the Commissioner to issue the benefit classification certificate dated 21 August 1980, and consequently no power in the Commissioner to vary it, because of the delay which occurred between the date when he became an eligible employee and the date when the certificate issued. In the light of the conclusions to which we have come on the other issues in the case, it is unnecessary to express any opinion on this question and we refrain from doing so.

54. In the result, the decision of the Commissioner under review is set aside and in lieu thereof the benefit classification certificate issued in respect of the applicant on 21 August 1980 is revoked.

I am in agreement with the decision in this matter and, subject to what follows, the reasons given by my colleagues for that decision.

2. However, as I was a member of the Tribunal in Re Weeks and Commissioner for Superannuation (not yet reported; decision given 22 May 1986), which followed the reasoning in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293, and as the determination of questions of law are, by virtue of s.42 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, to be decided in accordance with the opinion of the Presidential Member presiding, I express no view on the questions of law discussed in those reasons.
