ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - loss of capital to be invested in profit-making scheme - whether incurred in "carrying on or carrying out" the scheme.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 51 & 52.

Sun Newspapers Ltd. & Associated Newspapers Ltd. v. F.C.T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337.

REASONS FOR DECISION

15 January 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. On 12 July 1979 the Commissioner issued a notice of assessment against the Applicant Company (T-CO). In doing so, the Commissioner assessed the company's taxable income for the year of income ended 31 July 1978 - a date of some significance -at $44,266. T-CO objected to the assessment claiming that it was entitled to a deduction pursuant to either s.51 or s.52 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") in the sum of $33,740. It had provided a detailed statement with its income tax return in support of the claimed deduction. The Commissioner disallowed the objection and, on 26 November 1979 (sic), the Applicant requested that the decision upon its objection be referred for independent review. The Commissioner carried out his statutory duty on 12 February 1987 (sic). The matter came forward for review in December 1987.
  2. The Applicant was but one of many companies in which a particular family had an interest. It was seen as the holding company of such companies in the group as were its subsidiaries. It also held an influential position in relation to other companies in which the family has a particular interest.
  3. The group was based in a city which the witness described as being "a country town". The range of the group's interests was extensive. The companies of the group had interests in electrical retailing; in television rentals; in investments in real estate and in dealing in real estate; in investment in the share market and in dealings in shares; and in motels. In its own right T-CO at all material times held interests in real estate (over $65,000); shares in public companies (over $120,000); shares in subsidiary companies (over $160,000); and shares in other companies (over $20,000). According to its accounts to 31 July 1978, shareholders funds exceeded $500,000. One major asset not already mentioned comprised loans to related entities (nearly $1,000,000); balanced in part by borrowings from related entities (over $730,000). I am satisfied that the group of companies was active in the pursuit of profit and was willing to pursue new prospects of seeking profit when they offered.
  4. In the mid-1970's, the group was looking for a new solicitor. It thought it had found a suitable person in a local solicitor who was perceived as being a "young, solid and go-ahead" type with an "astute commercial mind". His firm became solicitors to the group. He arranged a couple of loans secured on mortgage from the group to other clients of his firm. He then proposed to the father and son - the then Directors of T-CO - that T-CO should become engaged in dealing in gold bullion.
  5. The solicitor and the son travelled to Sydney, and there had some discussions with a bullion dealer as to the prospect of an appointment as its agent to deal locally in gold bullion. The solicitor thought that a market was to be found in the ranks of clients of his firm who would be interested in small purchases of bullion. The proposal was that T-CO should be the agent. The solicitor declined involvement because he said it would be professionally improper for him to be profiting from bullion sales to purchasers chosen from the ranks of his own clients. No particular arrangements were made to secure such an appointment and T-CO did not undertake any detailed investigation as to the viability of the proposal.
  6. Early in June 1976, shortly after the Sydney visit, the solicitor came to the office of T-CO to place a fresh proposal before the Directors. The proposal was for a single purchase and an immediate, pre-arranged re-sale. In support of his proposition he produced a letter from the bullion dealers. It was probably addressed to either the Applicant or the solicitor. The terms of the letter are unknown. He also produced a copy of what he asserted was a letter from a superannuation fund expressing its wish to purchase the particular quantity of gold for a stated price. The extract was so presented as to conceal the letterhead and thereby the identity of the fund. The solicitor said that that was because, in such a small community, the fund did not wish to be identified. The proposal was that T-CO should buy bullion from dealers for $143,330 to be immediately oh-sold to the superannuation fund for $150,272. The fund was to make payment against delivery and it was therefore to be expected that a profit of $6,942 could be had within seven days of the purchase. Attracted by the prospect, T-CO drew a cheque in favour of the solicitor's trust account for the purchase price, leaving all other matters to the solicitor.
  7. The seven days came and went and T-CO started to become concerned. On 28 June 1976 it was able to obtain from the solicitor two cheques drawn on his firm's trust account for the sum of $150,272 and it promptly deposited them for collection. By reason of the 31 July date for T-CO's tax year, in this case the date of 30 June has no particular significance. By 7 July 1976, if not earlier, T-CO had learned that the cheques had been dishonoured ("payment stopped").
  8. That day the Directors of T-CO met with the solicitor and his wife and the solicitor's father to discuss the situation. The solicitor then admitted his fraudulent conduct. Promises and undertakings were given as to repayment of the $150,272. Putting its own interests to the forefront T-CO did not report the solicitor's conduct to the Law Society or to the Police. By September T-CO accepted from the solicitor a "mortgage debenture" whereby the solicitor purported to charge "all and singular his undertaking and all his assets whatsoever and wheresoever both present and future" with payment of the principal sum. The principal sum was not identified. The recitals which appeared in the standard form used - which was appropriate to the debenture of a corporation - were deleted, as were the words charging "its uncalled and called but unpaid capital for the time being". As the witness said, he was unsure as to the efficacy of any of the document as it had been prepared by the solicitor.
  9. Before the company's books were presented to its accountant so as to enable him to prepare accounts to 31 July 1976, the company, realising that it had been defrauded and anxious to not even let its accountant know what had happened, documented the advance for the purchase as a "loan" to the solicitor. It was only when the accounts were presented to the accountant to enable him to prepare the return to 31 July 1978 that the situation was at last disclosed to him. By that date the company had received what it believed to be - and what was to prove to be - the last payment to be recovered from the solicitor. It seems that by that date the solicitor had been struck off the roll of solicitors. It was also then known that other creditors were seeking recourse against him.
  10. The result was that, in the company's income tax return to 31 July 1978, it claimed a deduction in the sum of $33,740, that being the net loss suffered as a result of its dealings with the solicitor. In presenting the return a detailed explanation, in accordance with the foregoing, was provided. The explanation concluded:-

"It is stressed that Taxpayer is not claiming for a bad debt. Never at any stage were moneys lent to (the solicitor). For its own book-keeping purposes Taxpayer described the account as 'loan to (the Solicitor)'. That would not be an accurate description of the true legal situation, that is, that Taxpayer entrusted moneys to (the solicitor) as its agent for the purposes of carrying out a profit-making undertaking. The ultimate loss on the undertaking was not precisely ascertained until this 1977/78 year."

11. The claim calls for consideration of a number of possibilities. Of them, the first two depend upon the provisions of s.52 of the Act, which provides:-

"(1) Any loss incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income upon the sale of any property or from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme, the profit (if any) from which sale, undertaking or scheme would have been included in his assessable income, shall be an allowable deduction.

Provided that "

12. Clearly, the first test is not satisfied because there was no "purchase" of the bullion. Further, the second test is not satisfied either because the only profit-making scheme to be pointed to was that of profit-making by resale as is of the bullion and that scheme did not commence. The property to be risked in the scheme was stolen beforehand by the person entrusted with the task of entering into the scheme. For the Applicant, it could be contended that the tax result should be no different from that which would have arisen had the gold been bought and then stolen. It may well then have been that the loss would have been allowed - because the loss would then have arisen "in the course of" or, to use the words of s.52, in "the carrying on or carrying out of" a profit-making scheme. But, in this instance, the loss came at too soon a point of time. It happened so as to prevent the profit-making scheme commencing. The money proposed to be invested in the profit-making scheme by the purchase of bullion was misappropriated in advance of the proposed scheme.

13. Other possibilities depend upon the provisions of s.51, which provides as follows:-

"(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. One contention to be considered is that of the carrying on of a business. Associated with that is a consideration as to whether the gold would have been "trading stock": that is, stock to be used by way of purchase and resale in order to generate a profit. Another question is whether the loss was incurred "in the course of" deriving assessable income: the first limb. But however the matter is viewed, I am satisfied that nothing is allowable pursuant to s.51.
  2. Gains may be of capital or of income, for which purpose of classification, regard must be had to statutory provisions which extend the concept of income and correspondingly diminish the concept of non-taxable gains. Similarly, losses may be of capital and, to the extent not depreciable, are not allowable as deductions; or may be allowable as deductions as being losses on revenue account.
  3. In this instance, the Applicant proposed to embark on a course of action intended to produce a profit. It took a sum of money with a view to investing it in a project. The project was to be the purchase of bullion so that it might be resold. The sum to be so invested was capital. It was to provide the 'profit-making structure' [Sun Newspapers Ltd. & Associated Newspapers Ltd. v. F.C.T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337]. That capital sum was entrusted to an agent who had a commission to obtain the bullion and to sell it on behalf of the Applicant. The agent, by a criminal act and - I find - without ever intending to carry out his commission, "stole" the money entrusted to him. That act occasioned the loss suffered by the Applicant - not any act involving dealing in bullion. In my view nothing would be allowable pursuant to s.51(1).
  4. However, that points up yet a further significant dimension to the problem of the Applicant. The loss occasioned by the act of the agent was the loss of $143,330: not of only $33,740; and it was suffered during the year of income ended 31 July (sic) 1976. What happened subsequently was that some of the loss was recouped. Put another way: there was no act or event during the year of income ended 31 July 1978 which occasioned any loss to the Applicant or any outgoing for the Applicant and for that reason alone, if for no other, nothing was allowable pursuant to s.51(1) of the Act in that year.
  5. The final contention to be considered - a contention not advanced in argument - was that there was a debt due to the Applicant which was written off during the year of income ended 31 July 1978. But, as the Applicant correctly acknowledged in its return of income, there was no basis for claiming that the bad debt so written off in any way at all satisfied the requirements of s.63 of the Act. The amount written off had not been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year. Nor was the amount written off a debt "in respect of money lent in the ordinary course of the business of the lending of money".

19. The determination of the Commissioner upon the objection under review is to be affirmed.
