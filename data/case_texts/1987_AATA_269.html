REASONS FOR DECISION

Generally

Consequential upon an investigation of the affairs of Mr P (herein after referred to as P), amended assessments were on an assets betterment basis, issued by the Respondent Commissioner against him. The assessments relate to the 1980, 1981 and 1982 financial years.

The substance of the returns, assessments and alleged omitted income might be summarised as follows:

YEAR TAXABLE INCOME AS PER:

RETURN NOTICE OF NOTICE OF INCOME

ASSESSMENT AMENDED OMITTED

ASSESSMENT

  1. $17,243 $33,375 $119,504 $86,129
  2. $14,063 $14,063 $ 80,444 $66,381
  3. $16,142 $16,142 $97,156 $81,014

In due course, following discussion and further investigation, it was contended by the Respondent that for the respective years the taxable income of the taxpayer P should be adjusted by $77,054, $44,612 and $75,396. The taxpayer disputed that his income had been as alleged. He said that so far as the source of monies used to acquire various assets, generally the subject of the assets betterment calculation was concerned, that he engaged from before 1976 and up until 1984 in gambling on horse racing in a systematic manner and that the monies resulted from his winnings.

The taxpayer says that over the period of his gambling he accumulated monies totalling approximately $270,000, not all of which however, was used or applied towards the purchase of specified assets.

The issue for determination in this matter is not so much as to whether the taxpayer attended race meetings and gambled. It is not so much as to whether the taxpayer caused money to be spent on the acquisition of various assets. It is more as to whether he consistently won the monies contended by him and from this source alone, and in the fullness of time, accumulated the funds sufficient to acquire the assets whose purchase is otherwise unexplained.

The onus of proof on a balance of probabilities lies with the taxpayer. It is the taxpayer who is to prove the negative, that is, that the funds to acquire the assets did not come from undisclosed assessable income. He seeks to do this by proving that the funds came from gambling. The Commissioner in this matter puts in issue the allegation and does not seek to set up an affirmative case. That is, the Respondent did and does not seek to set up a case that the additional income upon which he assessed tax was derived from a particular source.

The Applicant's Case

P is a self-made business man carrying on his commercial and manufacturing activities by means of P Enterprises Pty limited and P Holdings Pty Limited DT Pty Limited carried on business as a service company, providing the expertise and attendances of P and his wife to P Enterprises Pty Limited for a fee.

Prior to commencing business on his own behalf, P had completed an apprenticeship and a certificate course and had worked for a number of employers, in due course being promoted to the position of Production Foreman. In 1969 he began manufacturing activities in rented premises on his own account. In 1976 P Holdings Pty Limited acquired a factory building at Emu Plains, near Sydney, P Enterprises Pty Limited then moving to such premises and trading from there to this date.

The companies have, at all relevant times, traded profitably, the customers of P Enterprises Pty Limited being, in the main, large enterprises.

In about 1962/1963 P bought, with his wife, land at St Marys on which land in 1964 a house was built. Children were born to Mr and Mrs P in 1967, 1969 and 1977. In 1978 the land at Kemps Creek was purchased, in 197 9 the property at Umina, the latter by P Holdings Pty Limited with funds lent to it by P and his wife. In 1980, 1981 and 1982 a house was built on the Kemps Creek land and improvements effected to it. Extensions were carried out on the Umina property. P Enterprises Pty Limited and a fellow race goer. An investigator at one time employed by the Respondent was also called.

The factory was built and completed in 1977.

P from an early age had been interested in racing; he came from a keen racing family. Mrs P also had an interest in the turf, her father being an "inveterate gambler" on the horses. From 1973 the father lived with P, his wife and their children.

P began attending race meetings and betting in a systematic manner sometime prior to 1976 and by about June 1976 he had accumulated winnings of between $40,000 and $50,000. It was at about this time that a safe was installed in the family home at St Marys, secreted under a loose base of a cupboard or shoe cabinet.

P had as his accountant at this time a gentleman now deceased. He mentioned to the accountant the nature of his gambling activities and of its regularity. P was advised to keep a record of wins and losses and the names of horses on which bets had been placed. Such records disclosed that over the period from July 1976 to May 1984 the nett gain to P from his gambling activities was $220,615, this to be added to the accumulated winnings at June 1976 of between $40,000 and $50,000. It was this money that was used in the acquisition of the properties and in the carrying out of the improvements to them.

Gambling Activities and the System

There was adduced evidence as to a source of monies sufficient to substantially fund the acquisition of the assets being the real estate, improvements to the dwellings and the increase in the loan accounts.

P said that he had been interested in gambling on horse racing since he was a child. His father and grandparents had had a like attachment to betting, his father more inclined to trotting and greyhound meetings than horse racing. By the time that he was eighteen or nineteen years of age he was betting "quite often" with bookmakers, although not on a regular basis. By the time he was thirty (1972) his attendances at race meetings were more frequent and by the age of thirty-four (1976) he was attending approximately forty-five Saturday race meetings in a year. This frequency persisted up until 1983/1984 when in due course and according to P on account of the pressure he felt being exerted on him by the subject investigation, he ceased going at all and has only bet on TAB since that time.

P was in the habit, he said, even back in 1972/1973 of betting on all days that he was at the races. His attendances increased as the years passed. He says he found the activity relaxing.

In about 1973 P said he began to observe a phenomena in connection with betting at the races, not seemingly apparent to others. He had already been experimenting with systems suggested by others but was seeking one of his own. He said that he observed "favourites" as noted in the Friday evening papers not necessarily being the "favourites" on the Saturday. He adopted the practice of carefully examining the two Friday evening papers and assessing his own odds in relation to two to four horses. He began to position himself at a race meeting nearby to a rails bookmaker and observed the fluctuations in the odds being offered on horses that he had himself selected as possible winners. He only watched the main bookmakers but hardly ever placed bets with them. They were the first to move, it was then too late to place bets with such main bookmakers. P noticed, so he said, that there was often a movement in prices at a time very close to the start of a race and when the horses were away from contact with persons who might influence their place, and the bookmakers. He noted the opening odds and compared them with his own preliminary assessment. If the prices shortened in the last few minutes, if the "wise money" was put on, he knew that, (so he said) the punters were betting. He would assess his odds against the bookmakers' odds. It was, he said, very much a matter of instinct or intuition. P said he believed the racing game was basically dishonest, and that jockeys can be in touch with bookmakers and others right up until just before a race starts. That is why he waited until the jockeys were out of communication range. Bookmakers could then vary prices down the line.

It was at this time that P customarily placed his own bets, not dealing regularly with the same bookmaker. His practice was to bet on only one horse, although he might have been contemplating more than one horse, in a race, and invariably in amounts of, by 1976, (be it made up of two bets of $50 each on the same horse), $100 until in the early 1980's his individual bets were increased to $200. In 1973 the bets had been in $10 or $20 amounts, later increasing to the larger figures. P seemingly only attended the four metropolitan racing tracks.

Often when the children were young, the whole family would attend race meetings and make a picnic out of the event. P's son began to place bets for P when he was about fourteen or fifteen years of age, that is in 1981/1982, the bets "normally being in amounts of $50, P himself later, and before the race started backing or "topping up" this amount with another $50 to make a round $100. By 1983 the son was betting on his. own behalf. The son now works for P Enterprises Pty Limited and is as well, employed as a part-time bookmaker's clerk.

The son himself said in his evidence that by 1981/1982 he was frequently going to the races with his father. He, the son, was in the learning process. P, the son said, would read the papers on Friday night, selecting a few horses. At the race course itself he noticed his father betting in the period just prior to the running of a race, and particularly if late money "came for a horse". His father kept an eye on the tote bookmakers and as to whether they accepted large bets - "that's where late money comes from". The son said that his father was a successful punter, only backing one horse in a race. When with him in 1981 and 1982 the times that his father lost "were rare". The son often placed bets for his father initially for $100 and in about 1982 and thereafter $200 on a horse. He was not told by P to place the bet with a particular bookmaker, but would usually go to the bookmaker closest to him. The son said that bets were placed with rails, and as well with big, bookmakers. His father would ask for the best price and "point me in the direction".

P was inclined to follow "true champions" regardless of the odds and his system. Otherwise, it depended on movement in prices as to when the bets were placed, but he also, so he said, paid-heed to the jockey. His largest win on a champion was $3500 on Kingston Town, a horse that he said he backed whenever it raced. He had bets on other champions such as Ming Dynasty and Block Buster and Artist Man that won "a lot of times"-although on the latter horse, he seemed to have lost more times than he won. Wins and losses appear in the records said to have been kept by P.

P was accustomed to place his own bets. This was not to say that he was a loner at the races. He went there with relatives and associates and occasionally asked others, no doubt whilst he was still watching the odds, to place bets for him. He generally carried about $2000 with him in $50 notes. This money he carried in his pocket. He would take out his winnings, put them away and leave the remainder.

P was accompanied to some race meetings between 1976 and 1983 by his father-in-law who would, occasionally, put on bets for P. They would stand apart. P would nominate the horse and request his father-in-law to get the best price that he could. Unlike P, the father-in-law bet on every race. He said that he is a "mad punter". The father-in-law saw P "picking out winners" at the house at St Marys. When at the races, he said that he noticed his son-in-law waiting right to the last minute before placing a bet, this in a sum in all of $100. He was a straight-out punter, only betting on one horse in a race. The father-in-law only remembered two occasions when P lost, and did remember two occasions when winnings were over $1000. The father-in-law said that P had told him of his keeping "records" although they were not sighted by him. The father-in-law did see P making notations in a race book.

P's own father also sometimes went with him to the Rosehill racecourse. Evidence was given by a truck driver, a friend of P of his accompanying P to the Rosehill racecourse on about six occasions a year. P was a "pretty astute punter" the truck driver said "very successful". He generally confirmed the system said to have been used by P. The truck driver observed what P did, he said that P "never bet foolishly, he had the capacity to control himself. His unsuccessful days were very rare".

P's wife was not as enthusiastic a punter as P, although on the evidence of she herself and P, she was privy to the "system" and assisted in counting and noting the winnings. She did not play a part in actually writing up the record sheets of winnings and losses. She said that from about 1974 P began going to the races every Saturday, sometimes taking the whole family. She referred to his successes, "a lot of success", his selecting horses, and his practice at a course. "He was always the last back after betting" she said. She referred also to P's betting in $100 bets, and of his winning amounts of $700 to $800. After the safe was installed in 1976 she said that money when it had accumulated was placed in it. The safe was hard to get to and in the meantime, the money was left in a shoe box in a drawer.

The assistant manager of P Engineering Pty Limited accompanied P, again to Rosehill racecourse about six to nine times a year.- He said that he discussed with P his "system" and the details of it. The assistant manager spoke of the constancy of P with his betting, the manner in which he regulated and controlled his selections, and of P rarely gambling more than $10 0 on a horse in a race, and then only on one horse. The assistant manager said that losses were rare for P, at least when he was with him, and that he was told of winnings of $400 to $600 on a day's events.

By 1976 P said that the nett winnings had accumulated to an amount of between $40,000 and $50,000, the same being in $50 notes and hidden in the home at St Marys. When asked why he kept such large amounts of money in the house and not in a bank account P replied that that was just the way he did things. He was told by his accountant, he said, that he was losing interest on the money, this in 1979,.and when P and his wife were about to spend part of the winnings. He left the money where it was and did not lend it to either company, even though one of the companies itself borrowed money from a bank. P said that he and his wife had agreed together that they would not touch the money derived from the winnings, it was for "the dream house".

The recording of wins and losses

P Enterprises Pty Limited, P Holdings Pty Limited and P, had, in 1976 as their accountant, M. The accountant was accustomed to visit P at the office of P Enterprises Pty Limited and at P's home. During April 1976 and in a conversation at which P, Mrs P, and M were present, P said that mention was made by him of his interest in, and success at, betting on horse races. No amount, according to P, was mentioned. P sought advice so he said, as to whether his winnings should have been included in the information set forth in his tax returns, and as to whether the same were taxable. P stated to the accountant that his winnings were becoming more regular, and according to Mrs P, that he had been quite lucky on Saturdays and that he had a system as a result of which, he had won "quite a bit". P was, on his evidence, advised that as the betting activities were a hobby and part of his way of relaxing, that he did "not have to pay tax" but that he should thenceforth keep a record "in case he ran into trouble". "If you are too successful" the accountant allegedly said, "you could be regarded as a professional, but to date it is only a hobby". Mrs P did not recollect the latter observation being made by the accountant. P said that he was advised to record the date and name of the horse, the win or loss and according to Mrs P "for the purpose of showing where the money had come from".

P commenced recording on sheets of note paper, his weekly forays, noting the names of horses, wins and losses, and the progressive total of winnings. He did not, he said, write up a the record week by week. He made notes on betting paper or a race book at the races. The latter practice was observed by the assistant manager of P Enterprises Pty Limited on occasions when he himself was with P at the races. After each race meeting P and his wife counted the money, noted the horses backed, wins and losses on a piece of paper, and placed the whole in a shoe box which was then placed in a drawer. P's son remembered "days in the late nineteen seventies" when he would see his mother and father counting money on Saturday evenings, making records and if P was successful, being taken with the other members of the family out to dinner. Most Saturdays, the son said, they went out for dinner. The record sheets themselves were, according to P, written up monthly or bi-monthly and when the money "got in the road", it being until this time placed in a drawer as above mentioned. The records were written up at P's home and on the dining room table. This was confirmed by Mrs P.

In November 1976 P caused there to be installed in the base of a bedroom cupboard at the St Marys home, a safe. Thereafter it was into this safe, it was said, that the winnings were placed once the record sheets of paper had been written up. P said that the money was kept out of the safe in a drawer, for some time, mainly because of the fact that the safe was in an inconvenient position and difficult to access. Photographs in evidence of the safe and its location confirm this to have been so.

Mrs P did not eve count the money in the safe. She saw it when P put money in or took money out. When the money was first put into the safe in 1976 there could have been, according to her, up to $50,000.

Although the accumulated winnings reached six figures, at no time were the monies placed to the credit of a bank account. They were placed in the safe until a need arose for their use. This latter practice was, it was said, on account of P and his wife wanting to keep the race winnings separate from all other funds that they or the companies might have; to keep according to P and his wife, the monies for their special purpose "dream home". They were gambling for their "dream home". Mrs P put it this way - the money was earmarked "for acreage to build a home".

Overall according to the records in evidence, a more detailed description of which will be given later in these reasons, P had a success rate of 63.7%.

Purchase of the real estate and improvements to it

P and his wife had been looking for a property in lieu of their St Marys house that they could, so they said, acquire and develop as a lasting family home. Eventually land at Kemps Creek was located and purchased in 1979 at a cost of about $40,000. Money was withdrawn by P from the safe - Mrs P said that she saw this happen - deposited into a bank account and a bank cheque used in payment for the said land. No issue on the evidence was taken by the Commissioner with this latter bank transaction actually having taken place.

In 1979 a weekender as purchased at Umina and in the name of P Holdings Pty Limited. Extensions were carried out to the same in 1980. It was said that approximately §30,000 towards the amount so required was provided by P and his wife from the accumulated winnings. The private ledger of P Holdings Pty Limited, written up from year to year, was in evidence. Current accounts therein maintained in the names of P and his wife showed that as at 30 June 1979 the amounts standing to the credit of the said current accounts were well in excess of-$30,000 and that §22,900 was advanced by them to the company "in cash" in the 1979 financial year and $5500 in "cash" in the 1980 financial year. Other credits are noted.

In 1980 in addition to the improvements to the cottage at Umina, a builder was retained and construction work was commenced on the land at Kemps Creek. In due course the cost of the building at Kemps Creek amounted to approximately $160,000, the same being in addition to the original purchase price of the land. The amount so expended on the construction was said to have been funded as to approximately $100,000 from the accumulated winnings and the balance by way of borrowing from the then Rural Bank of New South Wales. The borrowing from the Rural Bank was not on account of a shortage of cash money, but rather so it was said, because P wanted to obtain a credit rating and also because he "wanted to have money in reserve". Mrs P said that she saw money being taken from the safe and either bank cheques obtained or payments made in cash, all on account of the building works. She said that the money in the safe "came from no other source than gambling".

The Record of Race Meetings

The alleged record of his race horse bettings as maintained by P details names of horses, wins, losses and "balance" as from 3 July 1976 to 26 May 1984. The Respondent alleged that it was a fabrication.

It is said to record 1234 individual horse bettings of which 786 are said to have been successful. The record is ascribed on sheets of note paper with between 25 and 30 entries on each page. Evidence was given that P retained the record in his possession or under his control at all times and that sheets of a like kind with like information was seen by a senior employee of P Enterprises Pty limited and at the office of the company.

P said that the record stayed permanently in his brief case and always went with him to work, but not to the races. The assistant manager of P Enterprises Pty Limited said that in 1981 or 1982 he saw a record for the first time containing details of "racing activities" in a "brief case or a drawer" at the office of P Enterprises Pty Limited. The sheets of note paper were in a cardboard folder with clips. Whilst he could not positively identify the record in evidence, he said that the set out "was the same as that which he had seen".

P's son said that he saw his father write horses' names on exercise paper in a folder, as well as wins and losses. He saw the folder at Kemps Creek, but not anywhere else. He also spoke of seeing money placed in the concealed safe in the bedroom at St Marys. He did not know how much was in the safe at any one time. The son spoke of seeing P write up records at St Marys on a few occasions. He thought that the writing up took place in a bedroom and after a race meeting.

Mrs P said that she had seen her husband writing up his "betting records" many times "whenever he had time to do it". The task was usually performed on a Sunday afternoon on the dining room table with guide books, race books, papers and other documents about him. The entries were made, she said, on loose sheets of paper which he always kept with him in his bag. She identified the record that had been tendered in evidence through P as being his "betting records". The said sheets so seen were retained in a ring binder, cardboard folder.

As I said, the alleged records are in evidence as an exhibit. P gave oral evidence of a number of "significant wins". They appear on the sheets. He gave evidence of the quantum of his bets at various times. The sheets are consistent with the evidence. P said that it was not his practice to bet on every race. The sheets confirm this to have been so. The sheets are neatly written with minimal errors and alterations and although they extend over nearly an eight year period, they are in a good condition, showing minimal signs of use. But having in mind their sole purpose, little use would have been expected of them. They record nett winnings over the period of $220,615 with a noticeable decline in success from about July 1983 onwards.

The Investigation and issues arising out of it.

The investigation into the affairs of P, of P Enterprises Pty Limited, of P Holdings Pty Limited and of persons associated with P commenced in July or August 1983. P had from ten to fifteen meetings with officers of the Respondent. The meetings were usually of a half day duration. The principal investigator put "great pressure" it was alleged by P, upon himself and inferentially upon his family. The investigator visited Umina and discussed matters with neighbours. Some reports made by the investigator were incorrect. P was said to have had a tennis court at the St Marys home; there was not one.

P said that in an effort to prove his system of betting on race horses and winning with such consistency, he, following the commencement of the investigation, tried too hard to win, as a consequence of which his percentage winnings went so low as to reach 44%. He ceased gambling on horse races in 1984.

P did not mention his gambling activities to the tax investigators until August 1984 when he revealed to an investigator the records that he says he had maintained since the time of the discussion with the accountant in 1976.

In a statement of expenditure prepared by P for the investigation, presumably some time in 1984 and prior to August 1984, P omitted to make reference to an expenditure on betting. As he was, until near the end, and on his evidence, consistently winning, perhaps there was not at this time a nett expenditure of this nature. He said however, that he did not at that time regard his gambling activities to have anything to do with an investigation.

A tax investigator H, gave evidence that in 1984 he met with P and his then accountant, that he asked P a large -number of questions, including whether he gambled regularly and whether on TAB, SP and/or "on course". To each of these questions P allegedly answered "No". P himself said that the investigator H did not ask the questions referable to betting or gambling and that this subject was simply not raised in the first year of the investigation. P says that the betting wins only became important to him in the context of the investigation when the Respondent informed his accountant of the alleged deficiencies, this in about July or August 1984. Indeed, the matter might also, at about this time, have been raised by his solicitor P having, according to his evidence, left his racing records with such solicitor in May 1984. Whether it was the account or the solicitor, P was not sure.

It was however in May 1984 according to P, that he had a discussion with his solicitor and handed written racing records to him. When doing so he was advised, he said, that on the basis of what the documents contained he could be categorised as a professional and that he "should not bring that out until they were asked for". P said that he took the records to his solicitor as he "probably" thought at the time that they might be brought up in the investigation.

The officer of the Respondent, H, who conducted a significant portion of the investigation, said that he had had a number of interviews with P. The questions above mentioned referable to gambling were, H said, taken from a standard questionnaire form in addition to which he had prepared questions of his own. They were, H said, all put to P. The answers, given by P were then written by H beside the standard and prepared questions. The relevant interview H said took place in the afternoon of the 9 May 1984. By reference to the questionnaire form and the answers there written H was able to refresh his recollection he said, as to the substance of the answers given by P H said that after the interview he placed the documents in his brief case and went to his home where that evening he expanded on abbreviations that he had written down in. the afternoon. The following day he said he signed the document, put a date on it, and "filed the papers".

The printed form of questionnaire bearing the signature of H also bears a date "10-5-84", the "5" is written over another numeral which could have been an "11". If the latter be so, then either H at first misdated the document or else he did not complete the same until about six months after the interview. The latter was suggested to H by counsel for P. H did not have a recollection as to the circumstances in which the date, as to its month, was changed. He said that he thought he would have dated it on the day following the interview the "day he signed it".

By November 1984 the Respondent was well aware of P alleging his involvement in horse racing. It is not likely that the interview took place in that month and the answers then given. The amended assessments were issued under date 22 October 1984. H was involved in the investigation "probably till the first week in December 1984".

I am satisfied that the month in the dating was originally an "11". As to whether this leads to a finding that in May 1984 H made an initial mistake and then changed the numeral, or to his having signed and dated the document in November 1984, after the amended assessment issued, and in anticipation of an objection, he at some other time then having changed the date to that of the original interview, I am unable to conclude and it is not necessary for me to do so. Suffice to say that neither would H have inserted answers or P answered questions in the negative relating to gambling in November 1984. I do conclude however that in May 1984 P was still reticent about divulging his racing activities to the Respondent, particularly in the light of his having recently had the discussion as to his winnings with his solicitor, and of their being possibly liable to tax, and had handed, or was about to hand, the record of wins and losses to such solicitor.

H examined the accounting records of P Enterprises Pty Limited and P Holdings Pty Limited for the financial years 1976 to 1983. He confirmed that the customers of the manufacturing company were relatively large. Work was carried out as a result of orders being placed with the company. Manufactured goods were delivered and invoices issued. Payments were invariably made by cheque. H conducted a "test check" of payments. He was satisfied that there were not, in general, any discrepancies apart from some specific adjustments referable to entertaining, travelling and tooling that do not bear on the matter here to be resolved. His investigation was limited to the areas of his reference, that is, as to whether the increments to P had come from the company. H could not identify areas of movement of funds. He did not see any reason to doubt the validity of the records of the companies.

H went so far as to visit the St Marys home previously owned by P and his wife and noted the safe that had been installed there in November 1976. H wrote of the improvements to the home and erroneously noted the existence of a tennis court. It was put on behalf of P that H had deliberately and falsely stated the existence of the tennis court to suggest a lifestyle better than expected. I do not find this to have been so. Mistake, faulty or an absence of recollection there might have been, but not on the part of H a calculated attempt to mislead. Likewise with a misdescription by H of the nature of the roof cladding on the cottage at Umina, there more likely to have been a mistake.

Credibility of P

The Applicant's case substantially depends upon the authenticity of the race record. Yet little cross examination extended to the exhibit itself. It was and is the credit of P that is the paramount consideration.

One avenue of fallibility explored in cross examination related to the constancy of the recorded attendances at race meetings. There was in the period rarely a Saturday race meeting missed. In the calendar years 1981, 1982 and 1983 P was said to have attended 43, 41 and 43 race meetings respectively. He was asked questions about his taking holidays at Christmas time and at other times of a year. He was asked as to his having taken a business-come-holiday with his family to Queensland. P referred to an occasion when he did go to Queensland with the family for three or four days, they to holiday, he to work. P's son said that the family did spend some time in Queensland on holiday prior to 1984. He was indefinite about times but thought that there was such an occasion when he was about ten years of age (1977) and in 1983. The son could not recollect going to Queensland for three or four days only, his mother could. The assistant manager of P Enterprises Pty Limited could not recollect one way or the other.

The frequency of the family visiting Umina at weekends and the extent of Summer holidays was canvassed. P said that at Christmas time he usually stayed away from the race meetings for about three weeks when on holiday, but on the occasion of weekends at Umina he would drive to the racecourse and return to Umina after the event. The record confirms the former and does not deny the latter. P's son said that his father did leave Umina of weekends to go to the races and occasionally, the son said, at Christmas time. I do not consider that any finding adverse to P emerges from the above.

In his Notice of Objection against the amended assessment in respect of the year ended 30 June 1980, signed by P and dated 21 December 1984, it was stated,

"10. Further, and in the alternative and without prejudice to any of the preceding grounds set forth herein, the statement of my assets and liabilities as at 1 July 1979, prepared by your officers is incorrect in that an amount of $140,000 or thereabouts being an amount of cash on hand as at 1 July 1979 has not been included therein."

P was pressed, in cross examination, as to the basis on which this figure of $140,000 was derived. He said that he relied heavily on his accountant, as one might well imagine to have been the case, and when asked for information gave it to him. He said that he gave the accountant a cash on hand figure as at 30 June 1976 and otherwise relied on his record. Assuming an amount of $50,000 to have been on hand as at June 1976 and winnings according to the record of P in evidence, from 3 July 1976 to 30 June 1979 to have been $88,130, then the total cash said to have been on hand at 1 July 1979 was in excess of the alleged winnings by $1870 without regard being had to any money by then spent on the purchase of the Kemps Creek land. P said that he did not think that he would have checked as to the accuracy of the amount after initially giving the figures to his accountant. I do not find any aspect of this suggestion adverse to P.

In a document described as an "approx. and complete statement of assets and liabilities" signed by P and dated 14-3-84 P sets forth his "cash on hand" in various relevant years as,

  1. $50,000
  2. $16,447
  3. $52,101
  4. $ 2,228

In such document he did not make mention of the Umina cottage, no doubt as it was owned by P Holdings Pty Limited, and he underestimated the cost at that time of the improvements to the Kemps Creek home. Indeed, round figures only were given for the latter, the same approximating the amount borrowed on mortgage from the Rural Bank and regardless of the monies withdrawn from the safe, it was said, for the building works. H said that the figures above mentioned appearing on the document were not given by him to his then accountant; he signed the document relying on its accuracy. He said that the document was substantially compiled by the accountant. He did given the accountant the cash on hand figure as at 30 June 1976.

Certainly some part of the information on the document could have emanated only from the private ledger or the final accounts of the companies P Enterprises Pty Limited and P Holdings Pty Limited. Balances on "shareholder loan accounts" and depreciated values of plant and equipment fall into this category.

It seems to me that the information contained in the document is consistent again with the reticence then being displayed by P to disclose his racing activities to the Respondent. It was shortly after the date on the statement that P had the discussion with his solicitor and then according to him handed to the solicitor the racing record. I consider that the information contained in the document is such as would more likely than not be derived by the accountant from the accounting records with some limited information only being provided by P. I do not see anything adverse to P, other than an unwillingness to disclose his gambling activities from his having signed the document.

Submissions of counsel remaining to be considered

Mr McGovern of counsel appearing for P submitted that the Tribunal should accept the evidence given by P, that he had discharged the onus resting upon him and had not only given an acceptable explanation as to the source of the monies, but had gone further and demonstrated that there was not another source from which funds might have been derived sufficient for P and Mrs P to acquire their various assets.

It was specifically submitted on behalf of the taxpayer that the Tribunal should accept that,

gambling was in "the blood of P" as well as on the wife's side of the family - P gambled frequently and consistently, this as a mode of relaxation and release from business life. Equally so, his gambling was consistent with the expressed purpose of P and his wife to acquire their "dream home". It was conceded by counsel that the Applicant's case rested squarely on his credit. Reference was made to the frequency of P's attendances at race meetings, even when holidaying at Umina, other than at Christmas time.

the nature of the business carried on by P Enterprises Pty Limited was that of manufacturing and the customers of the company were large enterprises. There were minimal cash sales of the company, and the records of P Enterprises Pty Limited were not at issue;

whilst it might seem strange to many that P and Mrs P would keep such relatively large sums of cash at their home, the explanation given whereby they wanted to keep the money separate from the business enterprises and expressly for their acreage or home, should be accepted. The explanation also given as to why the money was not placed in a bank account was acceptable;

the methodology adopted by P in relation to his ambling recognised a skill, intuition, and instinct. The method applied was described in detail by P and confirmed by others who had observed him in his racing movements at racecourses. His method had been analysed and his purpose demonstrated;

the record of race meetings in evidence" was consistent with the testimony of P. It had been seen by Mrs P and documents of a like nature have been seen by the son, and by the assistant manager of P Enterprises Pty Limited. The latter indeed had seen documents the like of those in evidence in 1981 or 1982 at the office of P Enterprises Pty Limited. He gave a description of the nature of the documents then seen by him and the fact that they were contained in a folder with a ring clip.

I interpose here by noting that on behalf of the Respondent it was submitted that there was an inconsistency between P and the assistant manager in that P had said that he carried the documents with him in his brief case, whereas the assistant manager said that he had seen the documents in a case or in a drawer. It was said that the assistant manager did not identify the documents tendered, only as to a similarity.

Having in mind that the alleged sighting took place some five years ago and that if true, little significance would have been attached to the sighting at the time, I do not consider that this discrepancy in recollection effects adversely the evidence of P in this regard.

the existence of monies in 1976 and in 1979 did not only depend upon the evidence given by P. The son gave evidence of his having sighted the money, it being counted from time to time and of its being placed in the safe. Mrs P evidenced cash being on hand at 1976 as deposed by P and the gambling money being used in relation to the Umina and Kemps Creek properties;

the advice given to P in 1976 by his then accountant, who has since died, whether it be as recounted by P or as similarly recounted by Mrs P, should be read together, the point of moment being, that records should be maintained and that they should be significant in their contents.

On behalf of the Respondent, Mr Burnes of counsel contended that whilst the Commissioner cannot point to some other source of funding, P stands or falls on the account given by him, that is, that the monies utilised in the acquisition, construction and improvement to the real estate and to some extent movement in the loan accounts, solely emanated from gambling. It was not necessary it was submitted, for the Commissioner to establish that all of the evidence of P and his witnesses should be rejected. It was open to the Tribunal to reject P's claim but accept the evidence of some of the witnesses that, for example, that P regularly attended race meetings but then not accept the evidence as to the quantum of his winnings.

It was submitted that the "scheme of deception" might arise at any stage.

Specifically it was put,

P's case rests on the accuracy of the "records produced by him and the account that he gives of their coming into existence. It was pointed out that the records disclosed P's having attended" some three hundred meetings on only five of which he lost at the end of the day. His success rate was 63.7% of bettings having resulted in a win. The Tribunal might exercise some scepticism in its analysis.

the record of betting transactions was said by P to have been transcribed from race books, guide books, or notes placed with money in a drawer pending deposit in the safe. Mrs P had made mention of the money being temporarily stored away with a running total document attached to it, all enveloped in an elastic band. She said that when she observed P preparing the record sheets, that he took the information from guide and race books.

She was not herself aware of the total winnings from time to time. The son saw his father prepare the documents, the like of the racing records, he said in the bedroom.

Whilst there is here a degree of inconsistency between the recollections of father, mother and son, as submitted by counsel for the Respondent, the same seems to me to be consistent with the passage of time and the transcribing being an exercise largely performed by P, Mrs P and the son being occasional observers. The counting of race winnings weekend by weekend and the steps then taken were described by the three above mentioned participants with some particularity. This might well have been expected.

some criticism was made by counsel of the evidence given by P as to his betting in $100 and later $200 bets. Mention was made of the son and others making bets on behalf of P in the sum of $50 and this then being "topped up" by P to the sum of $100. There is not any evidence in the racing records of P having placed a bet of $50 on any horse. I do not see the evidence as being significantly inconsistent with what was said in this regard by P;

P had said that he ceased his activities at the race track because of the tax investigation. He said that he "tried too hard to win", did not maintain his percentage, and in due course, in 1984, ceased his activities, this some months prior to his revealing to the tax investigator his gambling winnings. The evidence given in this regard by P was criticised, the explanation given by him said to be contradictory. It was suggested that P had not expressed concern about, and had not associated the investigation with, his gambling until some time in 19 84, yet the record of gambling showed a discernible trend away from previous success in 1983. It was suggested that the trend away from success was not associated with a concern referable to the investigation. This submission does not seem to me to be supported by the evidence. The investigation began in 1983. He did not disclose his gambling activities to the Respondent until about August 1984. He did evidence ten to fifteen meetings with tax investigators from the commencement of the investigation in 1983 he did instance the attendance by the investigators at his home, his weekender, and his previous home at St Marys and the investigators discussing the matter with neighbours and others. What P was saying in his evidence was that the pressure that he felt as a consequence of the investigation led him to deviate from the system that he had used at least over the prior seven or eight years with a consequential reduction in the percentage of his wins. He still continued attending race meetings for some twelve months after the investigation commenced. Whilst a matter to be taken into account in the overall assessment of the credibility of P, I do not consider that the evidence of P in this regard is such as to be disregarded;

the taxpayer said that he on one occasion, spent three to four days in Queensland. Mrs P had a like recollection. The son was not able to recollect such a time and made mention of two holidays in Queensland that he said took place, the one in about 1977 the other in 1983. There is an inconsistency here between the evidence of the parents and the evidence of the son. Having in mind the age of the son in 1977 and the various other events that took place in 1983, including the prospective purchase of a unit at Surfers Paradise, I do not consider that the evidence of the son in the form it was given should be accepted in preference to that given by the taxpayer and his wife;

it was submitted that there was an inconsistency between the evidence given by the inspector H and that of P referable to the interview conducted by H in May 1984. I have already referred to this matter and have indicated the view that I have formed in relation to it.

finally, it was submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that the keeping by P of his money in a safe in lieu of his placing it in a bank account should raise a grave suspicion as to its source and thereby render the explanation given by P, suspect. It was submitted that the Tribunal should not accept P as being ill-informed as to the possibility of the money attracting interest and the explanation of P and his wife wishing to keep the money separate and for their "dream home" should be rejected.

The Relevant Principles, Conclusions and Findings

I have elsewhere in these reasons referred to the onus as it lies upon the taxpayer and that it is for him to show that the assessments and amended assessments are excessive.

The nature of the responsibility that rests upon a taxpayer in an asset betterment reference was expounded in Crew -v- Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR 324, 326 -

" It was said [in the course of submissions by counsel] that where there is a surplus of assets over those that would be explained by returned income, there is no presumption that that surplus is income, or is assessable income. In a sense that is true (see Elsey -v- Commissioner of Taxation (C'th) [1969] HCA 48; (1969) 43 ALJR 415, 422). But in my opinion that is of no assistance in deciding the present case. It was argued also that the basic question (described as a question of law) was whether in the circumstances the surplus was income or was capital and that there was no presumption that it was income. In my opinion this misconceives the problem which was before the Board of Review and the problem that is now before this court. The case is not one in which nothing more is known than that there was a surplus. It is a case in which it appears that in every year over a long period, the appellant's position improved in a way for which his disclosed income cannot account......

In each year the Respondent treated the betterment for that year as being income. He did not prove how it had been earned. He could not provide evidence showing affirmatively that it was assessable income. If the onus had rested upon the Respondent he would have failed. But when the Respondent made assessments which included the betterment funds as assessable income and disallowed the appellant's objections, the matters were taken to the Board of Review and the onus was then on the appellant to show that the assessments were wrong. He gave evidence of his ordinary business activities and of his gambling activities and of the way in which these were inter-related. If his account of the matter had been accepted in full, it would have been shown that the disputed accruals to his wealth were not assessable income and that the assessments were wrong......

But the explanations of the appellant were not accepted and that meant that he had not discharged the onus of showing that his assessments were wrong."

At 328 the reasons continued,

" If the evidence of the appellant had been considered to be completely truthful and reliable, it would have been sufficient, I think, to look generally at the whole period covered by the assessments and to reach a general conclusion without examining closely any particular year and without troubling about the absence of specific evidence to explain in respect of a year the discrepancy shown in the betterment statement"

In case R124 84 ATC 791, 806 Mr PM Roach sitting as a Member of a Board of Review having noted that assessment of taxable income by the betterment method "is little understood" expounded upon the objective of assessment by this method and the burden of proof resting upon a taxpayer. He, rightly in my view, stated that the burden cannot be discharged simply by establishing that the Commissioner erred in some respect. "The taxpayer must go further and prove 'what is right'" He continued,

11. It follows that there are ordinarily only four ways of demonstrating an overall error in assessment by the "betterment method". They are to prove -

(a) that the cost of opening nett assets has been understated;

(b) that the cost of closing nett assets has been overstated;

(c) that the amount of non-deductable expenditure incurred in any, or all, of the years is less than that alleged by the Commissioner; or

(d) that receipts of a capital nature in any or all of the years in question are greater than the Commissioner acknowledges.

  1. Subject to one exception, unless the taxpayer can prove one or more errors in those categories, the under statement of taxable income in aggregate will remain unchanged.
  2. There is a fifth course open which may result in a finding in favour of the taxpayer without any particular error in the Commissioner's calculations having been proved. L'Strange -v- FC of T 78 ATC 4744 illustrates the point. His Honour (McGarvie J) had to resolve a conflict between the "betterment" calculation and a calculation based on the accounting records of the taxpayer. Both calculations could not be correct, and it followed that if the taxpayer's records were complete and accurate, the "betterment" calculation had to be in error. As the evidence did not indicate error in the calculation, His Honour found as a fact that the calculation based on the taxpayer's records was more likely to be correct."

It is these principles that are applicable to the issues that are to be determined in this reference.

The Respondent fairly put in issue the matters that have earlier been referred to in these reasons. I shall not again repeat them. They raise the truthfulness of the record and the motivation of P in alleging it to have been a contemporaneous document with the race winnings and not one created in an endeavour to explain the source of the funds that were used in the various asset acquisitions.

There are some aspect of P's evidence that one might find difficult to accept. His conduct in 1983 and 1984 it might be said was not the conduct of a man with a clear conscious. This might have been so, P being well aware of the possibility of his race winnings being challenged at some time by the Respondent. The reason given for the money being placed in the safe and not in a bank account, might also be suspect, however again the concern and the anticipation of P as to a possible investigation in relation to his racing activities could provide the explanation; but this is conjecture and not in accord with the evidence. I do not however discern on the whole of the evidence any reason why I should not accept Mrs P as being a truthful witness nor the assistant manger of P Enterprises Pty Limited. They both depose as to having seen the racing record as it then was or documents to a like effect well prior to 1983.

The winnings shown by the betting transactions of P and as disclosed on the record sheets of paper in evidence is, as P conceded, inconsistent with that of other race goers known to him. This however, does not negate the events having taken place. On the basis of the evidence before me and the conclusions reached as already detailed, I do not consider that the matters raised on behalf of the Commissioner and not resolved in favour of P are such as to preclude the Tribunal from making a finding favourable to the Applicant.

P's position did improve year by year "in a way for which his disclosed income cannot account" (Crew -v- Commissioner of Taxation, supra). P offered an explanation that was not accepted by the Respondent, to the effect that cash was derived from successful gambling which continued over the relevant years, the funds thus generated being applied to the purchase of and improvements to the real estate. On the basis of the evidence before me in this matter, I accept this to have been so. The taxpayer has shown and proved "what is right", (Case R124 supra). He has demonstrated an overall error in the assessments by the "betterment method" by proving receipts of non-taxable money in the relevant period greater than was acknowledged by the Respondent.

Accordingly I consider that the taxpayer has discharged the onus resting upon him and I am of the opinion that the objection the subject of this review, should be sustained as it relates to the matters that have been the subject of these reasons.

Decision

I would uphold the objection of the taxpayer as it relates to that part of the amended assessments the subject of these reasons.
