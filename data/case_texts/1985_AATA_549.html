Bashar and Australian Taxation Office [1985] AATA 549 (20 December 1985)

Last Updated: 19 February 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Compensation - aggravation of disease - whether employment a contributing factor - whether continuing total incapacity -applicant suffering from degenerative disease of the spine -"aggravation" in s29 applies both to disease and its symptomatology - sufficient connection between employment and aggravation demonstrated - applicant unable to carry out specially devised light duties - no suitable employment available.

Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971, Ss5, 29, 45.

Commonwealth of Australia v Beattie [1981] FCA 88; 35 ALR 369

Re Roder and Department of Veterans Affairs 5 ALD 103

Re Welsford and Commonwealth Banking Corporation 5 ALN N365

Johnston v Commonwealth of Australia [1980] FCA 93; 31 ALR 445

Re Stevanovic and Australian Telecommunications Commission 6 ALD 359.

  1. Paragraphs (a) of clauses 4 and 5 of the determination under review are set aside. The matter is remitted to the Commissioner with the direction that the applicant is totally incapacitated for work by reason of the aggravation of a pre-existing back condition to which his employment by the respondent was a contributing factor.
  2. The respondent is ordered to pay the applicant's costs in accordance with the appropriate scale in the Compensation Court of New South Hales as agreed, to be taxed by the Registrar or a Deputy Registrar of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in the absence of agreement.
  3. Liberty to apply on the matter of costs is reserved.

Re : AHMED BASHAR

Applicant

And: AUSTRALIAN TAXATION OFFICE

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant was employed by the respondent from 1958 until he was compulsorily retired on 4 December 1984. That retirement came about following a determination by a delegate of the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation dated 16 December 1982. That determination is the subject of the present review. The substance of it was a finding that the effects of an admitted injury ceased to exist no later than the date of that determination. Because of the continued failure by the applicant to attend to his duties thereafter, he was given a formal notice and was subsequently deemed to have resigned from the public service. His case in the present application is that the determination is incorrect in that he continues to be totally incapacitated for work as a result of aggravation of a disease to which his employment contributed, disease being used in the same sense as in Sections 5 and 29 of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act ("the Act").

The applicant has had a long history of back illness. The evidence of his own doctor indicates that degeneration of his spine probably set in early in life. At all events by 1962, this degeneration, coupled with some heavy strains at home involved in lifting gates, brought on such pain that he determined to undergo the operation of laminectomy. Although this gave him some relief at the time, the evidence showed that there were still some after effects. Certainly whatever disabilities he had continued to focus on his back. His sickness record shows that between 1958 and 1978 there were at least 28 incidents for which he had time off as a result of a stiff neck, a bad shoulder, or a bad back. During this time he was carrying out heavy work involving bending, lifting and working with his arms above the level of his head. After 1971, when he was promoted to clerical assistant grade 3, the physical demands of his employment were somewhat diminished. Nevertheless there was still a certain degree of activity which, as the evidence showed, had an effect on his back.

On 3 March 1978 there was an incident at work described by the applicant in these words:

"If I could take you back to 3 March 1978, did something happen at work relating to your back?---I was unloading a basket, out of the basket bundles of returns - not one bundle, maybe five, six , or seven, and I had to bend into the basket and then, say, at the end I felt some pain.

Did this pain become apparent immediately or did it get worse?---No, what I remember, it was very little in the morning before 12 and in the afternoon I felt more pain and then I put a claim form in and I do not know - I think the next day or after some days I went to a doctor, which I cannot remember very well.

If I can take your memory back to 1978, since the onset of this pain you have just described, what do you say about your pains?---Very nearly I had every day. If I did not have it at bedtime the pain woke me up about two, three, four, five, or 6 o'clock in the morning, in my back or in my stomach.

So in one shape or another you had pain since 1978?---Since 1978."

An application for compensation was made, liability was accepted and a determination was issued on 5 July 1979 which contained a finding that "The said Ahmed Bashar sustained an aggravation of a pre-existing injury namely back injury arising out of or in the course of his employment on 3 March 1978." Liability however was denied for any incapacity beyond 11 March when the applicant was said to have suffered further pains at home while opening a refrigerator door. The respondent took what was, in our opinion, a curious view of this incident. Having accepted that an incapacitating trauma had taken place 8 days earlier, it was then prepared to assume that the pains from that incident disappeared and were replaced by new pains induced from the minimal effort of opening a refrigerator door and taking an orange from the front of a shelf. Neither common sense nor the preponderance of evidence could support such a finding. Nevertheless the applicant at that stage did not appeal.

In 1980 there was a further incident described by the applicant in these words:

"Then in 1980 I think there was another event at work?---That is correct.

Would you describe what that event was?---There were some

documents on the floor, a lot of them, and a supervisor picked them up and put them on the table for grade ones to do the job, and as the doctor, the government medical officer and my own doctor, told me not to lift I did not; and then the officer in charge of my place came and said, "Why did not you help the other supervisor?" I said that the doctor told me not to lift anything or bend down and he said, "Does not matter: help him". From that moment I started doing, say, lifting and bending again.

And you carried out that duty?---Yes.

Did you feel anything after you carried out this duty?---I felt pain."

He has continued to experience incapacitating pain ever since. There is today no doubt that he continues to be incapacitated. The issues in this application are firstly whether that incapacity is caused by an aggravation to which his employment contributed and secondly whether his incapacity is total or partial. Liability for total disability for certain days and periods was accepted by a determination issued on 6 May 1981. Between that time and 1983 he returned to work intermittently but was absent for many periods. In an attempt to accommodate his capacities, the respondent went to considerable lengths to devise light work. His duties consisted of opening the mail, reading cheques and notices, sorting documents and placing them in appropriate baskets. However, he found himself unable to carry out even these tasks because of the pain which he says was caused by the sitting, bending and general activity.

"You were told to return back to duties, as you indicated, in 1982. At that time you had been told that the job that you were going to be given, you were allowed to sit and stand as you pleased?---That is right.

And you are not to do any lifting or any bending and I think驶you were initially - it was a desk job?---A desk job and I was told I could stand up whenever I liked.

Could you indicate what kind of duties you had to undertake?---Working on the cheques, checking the cheques, and finding some addresses from the telephone book and ringing up the taxpayer outside, trying to get some information; that was all. I can say it was not a hard job.

Even though you were able to sit and stand as you pleased, could you indicate, how did you feel?---As the days passed, the first day I had some pain and I took some tablets, tried to put up with it. Then the second day it got more pain and the third day or fourth day, I cannot remember exactly, it was very, very bad and I had to lay down. I could not do it any more. I think I remember I went to Sister and she - I am not sure of this after all these years, but I remember going to Sister in sick bay once or twice and after a while she sent me home and I went to my family doctor and got a certificate and sent it to the Taxation Department.

Each time you were told to go back to work you went back to work?---Yes.

Do you remember what was the shortest time that you stayed there after you returned to work and what was the

longest time---1 remember, I think two, three or four

days and the last time towards the end of 1984 I stayed five working days - I am not sure - five, even six, I am not sure and then I was in such pain I went to solicitor's office and I could not sit down or stand up and I laid down on the carpet where the - I went to the back and laid down about 15 minutes or half an hour."

He now claims to be totally incapacitated and describes his present pains in these words:

"Would you please describe to the Tribunal what pains you are feeling now that you say are related to your back pain?---Now, if I sit down, stand up, or lay down, say - and it depends where I sit. For example, seats on the buses and seats outside the courts, as soon as I sit down, after about half an hour, an hour, the pain starts; and if I sit longer hours, it gets more and more. Then, if I stand up, say, I get a bit of relief; but if I stand up or sit down days after days, a time comes that I cannot sit down or stand any more. I have to lay down; and nowadays, what I do, say, the pain wakes me up in my back or my stomach at 2,3, or 4 o'clock in the morning. I wake up. Then I try to go back to sleep, if I can; then, I am all day at home. About 11 o'clock or 12, or sometimes 2,3, or 4, I go to bed an hour or two and I feel a lot better.

Now, apart from your backache, are you feeling any other pains in your limbs - in your arms or your legs – or anywhere else?---When I sit down long time or stand up, especially when I sit down, the pain goes to my buttock, to my hip; then to the back of my left leg. It goes down to my heel and to my toes. And sometimes - this happens very seldom - I have no feeling. It happens once a year, maybe. If I stand up long time, say, left leg goes numb; but that happens very, very seldom."

We will deal first with the aggravation point. There is a surprising amount of unanimity in the evidence. Firstly, the respondent accepted liability for aggravation on at least two former occasions. There can be no doubt, therefore, that his early symptoms were accepted as being work related. The only possible area of dispute in the aggravation question is whether his present symptoms derive directly from that aggravation or (as Dr Ehrlich would put it) whether his present disease is so derived.

Dr Taylor described the basis of the applicant's problems as a prolapsed intervertebral disc. A C.T. scan of the lower spine organized by Dr C. Browne showed arachnoiditis and spinal stenosis. There was some bulging of the L4/5 disc. Facet joints were hypertrophied, causing the spinal canal to be narrowed. Dr Taylor was of the view that this condition was worsened (or aggravated) by "his long period of work and the heavy lifting." Such a condition is consistent with a considerable amount of pain.

Dr Hall, who examined the applicant on behalf of the respondent, considered that the employment may have been a contributing factor to the creation of his present condition. He seemed to be in no doubt that at any event there was a subsequent aggravation of the underlying condition caused by the employment, even though there is also an element of natural progression of his disease.

Dr Ehrlich, who also examined the applicant on behalf of the respondent, reported:

"It might well be argued in this case that a previously essentially asymptomatic degenerating spine has been rendered symptomatic by the trauma in question. This might have occurred perhaps by irreversibly stretching or rupturing some tight joint capsules and ligaments. This might lead later to healing with scar formation which in turn has less resilience and therefore may be damaged by lesser provocation on subsequent occasions with resulting further pain and repetition of the cycle".

He recognised the severity of the applicant's back pain and acknowledged that it could be brought on by circumstances that might to others appear innocuous.

"You do not have to do any lifting. It is a mistaken belief that one has to do heavy lifting in order to develop back pain. Back pain can develop from any ordinary act."

He was however, in our view, in error in making a distinction between aggravation of symptomatology and aggravation of the disease. The distinction may be relevant from a medical point of view, but in the structure of liability attribution to be found in the Act incapacity, however medically arising, is the touchstone. The symptoms, not the cause, are what immediately debilitates the employee. A worsening of those symptoms is encompassed within the concept of aggravation as used in Section 29 of the Act. For example in Commonwealth v. Beattie [1981] FCA 88; 35 ALR 369, the Federal Court emphasised that pain brought on by work activity may constitute an aggravation of a pre-existing injury even though no pathological change takes place. Whether it does or not will usually be a question of fact to be decided by the Tribunal or prescribed court whose decisions on questions of fact are not the subject of appeal.

In Re Roder and Department of Veterans Affairs 5 ALD 103 this Tribunal said at 112:

"The distinction between aggravation of the underlying pathology as opposed to aggravation of symptomatology is probably of no great relevance where aggravation in either sense contributes to a period of incapacity for work during the course of a person's employment."

It is the symptoms (in this case considerable pain) which incapacitate the applicant from doing even the lightest of work. That these current symptoms have been contributed to by the exigencies of his employment has in our view been amply demonstrated. The degree of contribution to be demonstrated by the applicant was described in Re Welsford and Commonwealth Banking Corporation 5 ALN N365 in these words:

"It is sufficient that the employment contributes to the contraction, aggravation, acceleration or recurrence of the disease. The contributing factor need do no more than contribute in a material way. The factor is not required to be the real, proximate or effective cause of the disease or of its development. In a case where a number of separate factors contribute to the contraction of a disease or its acceleration, aggravation or recurrence, all that is required is that such factor exhibits the necessary connection with the worker's employment."

In our view a sufficient connection has been demonstrated between the physical stresses of the applicant's employment and his present symptoms. Although the applicant from time to time was selective in his memories and although evidence was given of some of his extra-curricular activities, (which nevertheless led to more pain) we are satisfied with the applicant's account by and large, as it is substantially corroborated by all the medical evidence. Although the degeneration of the applicant's spine is an "autogenous disease" (Johnston v Commonwealth [1980] FCA 93; 31 ALR 445 at 466) the pain attributable to an acceleration of that disease has been shown to be connected with the applicant's duties to the necessary extent.

The remaining question then is whether the applicant's present incapacity is partial or total. Whatever the answer to this question, it is clear that his condition is not going to improve. He is a man aged 63. All doctors on both sides of the record agree that having regard to his history and age, the condition of his back will get worse and not better. If one were to adopt the test suggested by this Tribunal in Re Stevanovic and Australian Telecommunications Commission 6 ALD 359 there would be no doubt that the applicant would be considered totally incapacitated. At page 379 of its decision the Tribunal said:

"It follows that if an employee is a person who is not, as it were, crippled by work related injury, but is capable only of light work with a sympathetic employer more interested in rehabilitation of an injured worker than in obtaining a fit and active worker for his workforce, and is a person who has lost the ability to attract an employer except on those terms, then the full force of the authorities referred to is attracted. He is to be found to be totally incapacitated for work unless the employer can find or provide suitable employment."

In the present case of course, the respondent went to some trouble to provide employment that it regarded as suitable. The fact that the applicant was unable to do this work (and we accept his evidence for reasons outlined above) is, in our view, probably conclusive evidence both that the employment was not suitable and that he is therefore totally incapacitated for work. The job that was created for him is not a real job to be found in the market place. It is not "work" as that is understood in Section 45 of the Act. Work means regular employment for a full five day week in a recognised category of a job that is within the capabilities of the applicant. In our view the evidence indicates that there is no employment which the applicant is capable of securing and holding down and accordingly in our view he is totally incapacitated for work.

The applicant also suffers from abdominal pain which appears to be unrelated to his spinal condition. It also appears to be unrelated to his employment. No reliance was placed on it during the hearing of this application except to illustrate the limitations on the medication that the applicant could take. Some absences from work were due to the abdominal pain and some medical expenses were incurred in diagnosing and treating that condition. They were referred to in the determination under review and liability for that condition was disclaimed. This has not been questioned on the hearing of this application.

Accordingly only paragraphs (a) of clauses 4 and 5 of the determination under review are set aside. The matter is remitted to the Commissioner with the direction that the applicant is totally incapacitated for work by reason of the aggravation of a pre-existing back condition to which his employment by the respondent was a contributing factor. The respondent is ordered to pay the applicant's costs.
