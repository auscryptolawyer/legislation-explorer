Re Vivian H Kingston and Secretary To the Department of Social Security [1985] AATA 298 (31 October 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: VIVIAN H. KINGSTON

And: SECRETARY TO THE DEPARTMENT OF SOCIAL SECURITY

No. T85/19

Social Security

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.C. Jennings Q.C. (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Social Security - age pension - whether "a de facto spouse"

Social Security Act 1947 s.6, 28 (1A) (a)

Lynham v Director-General of Social Security 52 ALR 128

Wilson v Secretary to the Department of Social Security 6 August 1985.

HEARING

CANBERRA

31:10:1985

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent with a direction that the applicant be granted an age pension pursuant to s.28 (1A) (a) of the Social Security Act 1947 as from the date of his application, being 24 October 1984. Liberty to apply is reserved.

DECISION

The applicant is 80 years of age. He is the tenant of a State Housing Department unit at Chigwell which he shares with a Mrs. Nankivell who is 75. Both receive an age pension but since July 1971 the respondent has treated them as if they were legally married to each other. In order to test the situation the applicant lodged a fresh application for a full pension on 24 October 1984. It was rejected and following appeal to the Social Security Appeals Tribunal that rejection was confirmed on 1 May 1985. The applicant now seeks review of the decision to reject his claim.

It being common ground that the applicant and Mrs. Nankivell have lived together on a bona fide domestic basis at all relevant times the only question for decision is whether he is living with her as her husband. If he is not he is entitled to pension as an "unmarried person" pursuant to s.28(1A)(a) of the Social Security Act 1947.

The only evidence adduced at the hearing was that of the applicant himself who conducted his own case very competently. I found him to be an honest witness although time has clearly affected his recollection of events which occurred 15 or more years ago. He confessed to that weakness, particularly so far as dates were concerned but I accept without hesitation his evidence as to the essential facts upon which the Tribunal's decision depends.

The T documents were received in evidence and although inadvertently not marked as an exhibit a very comprehensive report dated 10 October 1985 by a social worker, Mr. David Langworthy, was referred to in evidence and treated as such without objection by either party.

According to a statement (T5) signed by Mrs. Nankivell in 1970 she had lived with the applicant "as man and wife" since 1965. The applicant also signed a statement (T6) several weeks later which said:

"Mrs. Nankivell is not bound to me in any way

legally but it is correct that we have

lived together for about six years."

The applicant's evidence before the Tribunal as to when they first lived

together was confused and unsatisfactory but it is not material to determine precisely when that event occurred.

It is the background to that event and the reasons for it that are more important because they throw light on the nature of the relationship between the parties.

The applicant had known Mrs. Nankivell's husband since 1926 and worked with him in 1933. He also worked with their son and was aware that he had an alcohol problem. When told by the son that Mr. Nankivell had to go to hospital and that he and his mother had nowhere to live the applicant suggested that they share the house he was then renting at Main Road, Glenorchy. The applicant had been divorced for some years at that time and wanted a housekeeper. It was arranged that he should pay the rent and other outgoings and Mrs. Nankivell would provide and cook the food as well as perform other housekeeping duties. She occupied her own bedroom and has always done so since.

Mr. Nankivell died on 15 May 1974 although this was another matter in respect of which Mr. Kingston's recollection was quite erroneous. In July 1974 the applicant secured the lease of a unit at Chigwell where he has lived with Mrs. Nankivell ever since. Although the son also moved into the new home he left sometime during the following year.

Financial arrangements between the applicant and Mrs. Nankivell never varied until the applicant's pension was reduced by reason of increases in the War Widows' pension which Mrs. Nankivell was granted after her husband's death. Originally telephone and power bills were met by the applicant but Mrs. Nankivell has now assumed these responsibilities because of his inability to meet them.

During recent years Mrs. Nankivell's health has deteriorated because she has cancer. She cannot leave the house on foot and accordingly the applicant now does the shopping and hangs the laundry on the line. Mrs. Nankivell still performs limited household chores and prepares the meals.

It is obvious in view of the time that has elapsed since the original arrangement that the relationship has firm foundations and is likely to continue whatever vicissitudes increasing years may bring for either of them. But the applicant was quite adamant, and always has been, that he has never had sexual relations with Mrs. Nankivell. Neither of the social workers who has seen the parties together has expressed any doubts about this. Mr. Langworthy said that although Mrs. Nankivell is presently in very poor health she expressed her agreement with the applicant's definition of their relationship (i.e. "housekeeper"). He obviously accepted that statement as true.

It was clearly not possible for Mrs. Nankivell to give evidence and accordingly I draw no adverse inference from her absence. It was never suggested by counsel for the respondent that the applicant was falsely denying a sexual relationship.

It was conceded by counsel for the respondent that there was no evidence that the parties "held themselves out as married". He relied however on material in the T documents to suggest that both parties held the subjective belief that the relationship was like one of man and wife.

He relied on T5 and T6 which were the statements to which I have already referred and which were made by both to the Department in 1970. He also relied on the fact that apparently no objection was taken by the applicant (or Mrs. Nankivell) to letters from the respondent in December 1975 (T23) which warned that "if you and your spouse receive income in excess of $. . . . . . notification is required . . ."

Whilst the 1970 statements were understandably treated by the respondent on their face value at the time, they do not now have the same force as the applicant's unequivocal sworn evidence before the Tribunal. Nor can I draw any adverse inference from the apparent failure to comment on the letters of 1975.

It was also urged that the applicant's 1969 taxation return provided evidence sufficient to justify the inference that he was then, and therefore stillis, living with Mrs. Nankivell as her husband. Document T7 establishes that in his return of that year he claimed a deduction for Mrs. Nankivell as a dependant spouse. The applicant says that he had previously claimed for her as a housekeeper but apparently in that particular year the return did not contain provision for such a deduction, or if it did he mistakenly claimed for her as a spouse. The applicant asserted, and no one denied, that the same deduction was at about that time available for a spouse as a housekeeper. Even if that explanation were rejected I would not regard the claim as concluding the nature of the relationship. At worst it was an attempt to deceive the Taxation Department. The essential question is whether the claim reflected the true relationship of the parties. I am not satisfied that it did.

It was further suggested that the applicant and Mrs. Nankivell had some joint social life together by reason of a mutual interest in darts but on closer examination the weight of evidence was very much to the contrary. They have never enjoyed any significant "outside" social life.

The respondent was really left to rely on the undoubted quality of permanence which is now a notable feature of the relationship in question. Moreover it was said that the willingness of each to physically care for the other in sickness and old age was indicative of the kind of emotional tie that is frequently present in an actual marriage situation.

There have been many decisions of this Tribunal which have considered the provisions of the Act now in question. The applicant referred most aptly to the following passage in the Federal Court decision of Fitzgerald J in Lynam v Director-General of Social Security 52 ALR 128 at p.131:

"Financial arrangements cannot be taken in isolation

and considered of particular importance in determining

the nature of a relationship. Their materiality, like

each of the other elements of the relationship, stems

from the impact which they have as part of an overall

situation. Each element of a relationship draws its

colour and its significance from the other elements,

some of which may point in one direction and some in

the other. What must be looked at is the composite

picture. Any attempt to isolate individual factors

and to attribute to them relative degrees of

materiality or importance involves a denial of common

experience and will almost inevitably be productive of

error. The endless scope for differences in human

attitudes and activities means that there will be an

almost infinite variety of combinations of

circumstances which may fall for consideration. In

any particular case, it will be a question of fact and

degree, a jury question, whether a relationship

between two unrelated persons of the opposite sex

meets the statutory test."

That same extract was recently cited by Deputy President Layton in Wilson (6

August 1985). The facts of that case were not unlike the present including in particular the "lack of romantic and sexual contact".

The Deputy President said:

"Their relationship is one born of financial economy so

that two individual incomes (when the applicant has

one) can be used to the greatest advantage and provide

security for each of them for the future. They have

not, made a commitment to live together for its own

sake, nor are they continuing to live together because

of their personal relationship, rather they have a

commitment to a joint financial venture and their

friendliness and companionship merely accompanies that

venture. Each views the commitment as being one to

himself or herself for the individual and mutual future

security of both."

Whilst there may well be many "housekeeping" contracts or arrangements which

develop into a man and wife relationship it certainly does not follow that all such arrangements acquire that quality merely by reason of their comparative permanence or the strength of the friendships developed. Before such a relationship is converted there must be, as the Tribunal recently observed, some kind of "spark to ignite the tinder of cohabitation into the fire of a quasi marriage relationship" (Smith - 22 May 1985).

In the present case an arrangement which began as one of financial convenience has now developed into an arrangement for mutual support to the best of the physical and financial abilities of each party, but there is no evidence to justify a conclusion that there is a quasi marriage relationship.

The decision under review will be set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent with a direction that the applicant be granted an age pension pursuant to s.28(1A)(a) as from the date of his application, being 24 October 1984.
