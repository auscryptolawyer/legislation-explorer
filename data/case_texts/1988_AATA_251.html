Re Claudia Anne Rowlands and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 251 (12 August 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CLAUDIA ANNE ROWLANDS

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A87/30

AAT No. 4555

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

Dr D.B. Travers (Member)

N.J. Attwood (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - whether failure to furnish information required to be furnished at entry medical examination - whether full disclosure would have resulted in issue of benefit classification certificate - whether retirement resulted from condition that would have appeared on benefit classification certificate

Superannuation - administrative practice relating to issue of benefit classification certificates

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16, 66(2), 154(4)

Public Service Act 1922

Re Kelly and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No.4319, 6 May 1988)

Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N129

Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 2983

Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229

Margaret Muir French v Phipson Nominees Pty Limited (Decision No. SC169 of 1984, 9 June 1987)

Phipson Nominees Pty Ltd v French (No. ACT G45 of 1987, 19 July 1988)

HEARING

CANBERRA

12:8:1988

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the benefit entitlement of the applicant under the Superannuation Act 1976 upon her retirement on the ground of invalidity with effect from 18 October 1983 is to be determined in accordance with the provisions of s.66(1) of the Act.

DECISION

This is the review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, pursuant to sub-section 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") -

(i) confirming the decision taken by a delegate on 31

January 1985 to issue pursuant to sub-section

16(11) of the Act in respect of the applicant a

benefit classification certificate ("BCC")

specifying the conditions "Dental Caries" and

"Tenosynovitis (R) Hand"; and

(ii) confirming the decision taken by another delegate

on 31 January 1985 to be of the opinion under

paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act that the incapacity

which was the ground for the applicant's

retirement was caused, or was substantially

contributed to, by a condition or conditions

specified in the BCC, or by a condition or

conditions connected with such condition or

conditions.

2. The basic facts are, as is usual with the abovenamed respondent, very clearly and fully set out in the statement lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act"). We set out in paragraphs 3 to 9 below the following portion of that statement, embodying some relatively minor alterations of our own, as our findings. Thereafter the findings are our own.

Entry to the Superannuation Scheme

3.(a) The applicant, who was born on 29 October 1944, became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 21 May 1979 by virtue of her appointment as a typist with the then Department of Administrative Services. She was previously employed by the Department as a typist in a part-time capacity. She underwent a medical examination for the purposes of s.16 of the Act on 24 September 1979. The examination was conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO"), Dr. D. Winterbotham.

(b) In providing details of her personal medical history in connection with the medical examination, the applicant answered "NO" in response to the specific questions:

"Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered

from any of the following disabilities -

...

5(a) Lumbago, paralysis or disorder of the limbs

or spine

...

5(c) Arthritis, rheumatism, gout or fixed joints

...

12(b) Have you had any complaints, illness or

injury not mentioned above?

...

13(b) Are you aware of any circumstances regarding

your health which will interfere with the

satisfactory discharge of the duties of the

position for which you are now applying?"

She answered "Yes" in response to the question

"12(d) Are you in good health now?"

4.(a) On the basis of the information revealed by the applicant and his own examination, Dr. Winterbotham considered that the applicant did not meet the general medical standard ("GMS"), used as a guide in the classification of entrants into the Superannuation Scheme, due to the condition "Dental Caries". Dr. V. Boyce, Senior Medical Officer ("SMO") and Adviser to the Commissioner, concurred, and recommended that the applicant be medically reviewed in eleven months.

(b) The applicant however was not re-examined, and the report of the medical examination dated 24 September 1979 was furnished to the Commissioner. A delegate of the Commissioner, having regard to the report of the examination and such other matters as were considered relevant, issued on 22 February 1980 a BCC in respect of the applicant specifying the condition "Dental Caries".

Events leading to the applicant's retirement

5.(a) On 23 June 1983 the applicant was examined by CMO Dr. R.J. McArthur for the purpose of assessing her fitness for continued employment. Dr. McArthur recommended in a report dated 7 July 1983 that she was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the ground of invalidity due to the effects of the conditions:

"Tenosynovitis Right Forearm 60%

Osteoarthritis Right First

Metacarpus Phalangeal Joint 40%"

and commented:

"Pain in right forearm aggravated by typing since

1978. Has persisted despite redeployment to work

not involving typing or repetitive work ... Dr.

Brook (rheumatologist) has diagnosed tenosynovitis

and osteoarthritis."

(b) SMO Dr. B.C. Bromhead concurred with Dr. McArthur's opinion, and the applicant was retired on the ground of invalidity with effect from 18 October 1983.

Delegate's decision under s.16(11) of the Act

6. As the applicant's contributory service at the time of

her invalidity retirement was less than 20 years and she had not attained her maximum retiring age, a delegate of the Commissioner, having regard inter alia to the comment of Dr. McArthur that the applicant had suffered pain in her right forearm since 1978, addressed his mind to the possibility that the applicant may have failed to furnish information required to be furnished by her, or may have furnished false information at the time of her entry medical examination on 21 May 1979.

7.(a) For the purposes of the investigation, a copy of Dr. Brook's report, which was referred to by CMO Dr. McArthur in the report of the applicant's retirement medical examination, was obtained, as well as a report from her General Practitioner Dr. M.J. Kneebone.

(b) In his report dated 22 April 1983 Dr. Brook stated inter alia:

"...

I first saw this woman on the 24th March 1983 when

she told me the main problem was pain around the

base of the right thumb etc.

She first noticed her symptoms in 1978 using a

manual typewriter. At this time she had returned

to work some four months before after many years

off work, raising her family. At this time her

pain was particularly troublesome when she had to

use a twisting movement to roll heavy cards into

the typewriter. In 1980 she changed to an

electric typewriter which she found helpful.

However early in 1982 her symptoms became severe

and she was off work for over two months. She was

then put on clerical work flipping cards in a

ledger and using a calculating machine with some

writing. There was no improvement by Christmas

and in January she was put in a Registry splitting

open envelopes and filing. She could not cope

with this at all and had been put off work by the

CMO.

Apart from troubles with working she also has other

difficulties since May of last year because of the

pain she has had to give up squash, ten pin bowling

and tennis. She feels she has missed out on

promotion and is depressed about her condition.

She saw Dr. Gytis Danta the neurologist in June of

1982 who investigated her for Carpal Tunnel

Syndrome without finding any evidence of this.

On examination she had a mild osteoarthrosis of the

DIP joints with moderate changes in the first

carpo-metacarpal joint which is symptomatic.

This is the main source of symptoms in the thumb

but in addition there is some tenderness over the

right thumb flexor. The elbow pain is due to a

medical epicondylitis. I was not able to reproduce

the shoulder pain and I thought the thoracic outlet

and cervical spine functioned normally.

She had had a good deal of physiotherapy at Calvary

Hospital which was not helpful. In my opinion the

first carpo-metacarpal joint was the dominant

problem with secondary involvement of the flexor

tendon and medial epicondyle (so called golfer's

elbow).

..."

(c) Dr. Kneebone in his report of 25 July 1984 commented inter alia:

"This patient consulted me only twice before May

1979.

...

6.2.1979 - pain in right middle finger and thumb

since starting work as a typist. She

was tender over the thenar eminence. I

felt this to be a tenosynovitis and

ordered Butazolidin and rest, and

suggested I review her in two weeks.

She did not return to discuss this

problem."

8.(a) Pursuant to paragraph 16(11)(c) of the Act a delegate was not of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by the condition "Dental Caries" specified in the BCC issued on 22 February 1980, or by a condition or conditions connected with "Dental Caries".

(b) However, having regard to the medical reports of Dr. Brook and Dr. Kneebone, the delegate was satisfied that in connection with her entry medical examination on 24 September 1979 the applicant had failed to furnish information required to be furnished by her or had furnished false information, and that had that not been the case, there would have been in force in respect of her, immediately before her retirement, a BCC specifying the conditions "Dental Caries" and "Tenosynovitis (R) Hand", which latter condition caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement.

(c) Being so satisfied, the delegate issued on 31 January 1985 a BCC in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "Dental Caries" and "Tenosynovitis (R) Hand". Under s.16(11A) of the Act, the latter certificate was deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant immediately before her retirement, and the BCC issued on 22 February 1980 under s.16(4) of the Act was thus deemed to have been revoked.

Delegate's decision to apply s.66(2) of the Act

9. On 31 January 1985 pursuant to s.66(2) of the Act, another delegate formed the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, namely the effects of "Tenosynovitis Right Forearm" and "Osteoarthrosis Right First Metacarpus Phalagist (scil. Phalangeal) Joint" was caused or was substantially contributed to by the conditions specified in the BCC issued under s.16(11) of the Act.

The Legislation

10. The provisions of s.16(11) of the Act were amended by

Act No.151 of 1986, but at the time of the delegate's decision, s.16(11) was in the following terms:

"(11) Where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee

by reason of death or retirement on the

ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less

than 20 years and, on the day on which he

ceases to be an eligible employee, he has

not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) a benefit classification certificate is not

in force in respect of the person

immediately before his death or retirement

or, if a benefit classification certificate

is in force in respect of him at that time,

the Commissioner is not of the opinion that

the death or incapacity which was the ground

for his retirement was caused, or

substantially contributed to, by a physical

or mental condition or conditions of the

person specified in the certificate, or by a

physical or mental condition or conditions

connected with such a condition or such

conditions; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied -

(i) that, at or in connexion with a

medical examination which the

person was required to undergo

under this section, the person

failed to furnish any information

required to be furnished by him or

furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed

to furnish that information or had

not furnished that false

information, there would have been

in force in respect of the person,

immediately before his death or

retirement, a benefit

classification certificate in

which there would have been

specified the physical or mental

condition or conditions which

caused, or substantially

contributed to, the death or

retirement, or a physical or

mental condition or conditions

connected with such a condition or

such conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of

the person a benefit classification

certificate in which there is or are

specified the physical or mental condition

or conditions of the person which, in the

opinion of the Commissioner -

(e) in a case where a benefit classification

certificate is in force in respect of

the person at the time he dies or

retires - would have been the physical

or mental condition or conditions of the

person specified in the certificate at

that time; or

...

if the person had not failed to furnish that

information or had not furnished that false

information and, for the purposes of this

Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have

been in force in respect of the person

immediately before his death or retirement."

11. For s.16(11) to be applicable in the applicant's case

the decision-maker needed to be satisfied of the following matters:

(i) that the applicant was an eligible employee

immediately before her retirement on 18

October 1983 (s.16(11)(a) of the Act);

(ii) that the applicant ceased to be an eligible

employee by reason of invalidity retirement

(s.16(11)(a));

(iii) that the applicant's period of contributory

service was less than 20 years

(s.16(11(b));

(iv) that on retirement the applicant had not

attained the age of 65 years (s.16(11)(b));

(v) that either a BCC was not in force in

respect of the applicant immediately before

her retirement or, if one was in force, it

did not specify a condition or conditions

related to her retirement incapacity

(s.16(11)(c));

(vi) that at or in connection with the entry

medical examination held on 24 September

1979, the applicant had failed to furnish

information which she was required to

furnish, particularly with reference to the

medical questionnaire and questions put to

her by the examining CMO;

(vii) that if that information had been furnished

as required then a BCC would have been in

force in respect of the applicant

immediately before her retirement;

(viii) that the condition(s) specified on such BCC

either -

. caused the applicant's incapacity on

retirement; or

. substantially contributed to her

incapacity on retirement; or

. was/were connected with another

condition(s) which in turn caused her

incapacity on retirement; or

. was/were connected with another

condition(s) which in turn

substantially contributed to her

incapacity on retirement.

12. The provisions of s.16(4) were amended retrospectively

with effect from 1 July 1976 (see s.2(1) of Act No.80 of 1986) are as follows:

"(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the

opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or

mental condition or conditions referred to in the report

or reports, will not continue to be an eligible employee

until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the

Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification

certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which

the relevant condition or conditions is or are

specified."

13. There is a problem about the retrospective amendment of

s.16(4). It was submitted that the amended version applies to these proceedings. The Tribunal has referred to this problem in Re Kelly and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No.4319, 6 May 1988). The question under s.16(11) being whether a BCC "would have been in force", a question of fact, we do not see how an amendment made retrospectively could be held to apply to a prior factual situation that had occurred some 9 years previously. But as was said in Re Kelly the point is of no moment, for in Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N129 the Tribunal, in which the presiding member was a judicial presidential member, declined to follow Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 2983, holding that the words of the legislation do not require that it be more probable than not that premature retirement occur but only that it be something "that may well happen". This test is in our opinion indistinguishable from the "real risk" test adopted in the amending legislation. It follows that it is effectively the "real risk" test that is to be applied in any event.

14. In his written submission, which was as well and fairly

composed by Mr. Eadie, who appeared for the Commissioner, as was his conduct of the case during the hearing, three fundamental issues were identified as necessary to be decided by the Tribunal:

(i) Was there non-disclosure at the applicant's entry

medical examination on 24 September 1979?

(ii) If full disclosure had been made, would a BCC

have been issued in which the condition

tenosynovitis right hand, or some such wording,

have been specified?

(iii) Was the applicant's retirement incapacity caused

or substantially contributed to by the condition

that would have been specified in the BCC (the

"non-disclosed condition") or by a condition

connected with that condition?

15. The Tribunal has not had any significant difficulty with

either question (i) or question (iii) as set out above, and in consequence we think it appropriate to state our conclusions in respect thereof in more summary form than has usually been the case in matters of this kind in the past. Having regard to the evidence and other material before us, namely the documents marked as exhibits and the oral evidence which is contained in the transcript, we make the following findings as to these 2 points:

As to (i)

(a) In Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229,

at 237 the Federal Court summed up the position as follows:

"There must be a requirement made of the employee to

furnish information before any obligation arises. The

extent of that obligation depends upon the questions

asked of him and the actual state of his knowledge in

relation to them."

(b) With these principles in mind we consider that it is enough

to say that we agree with the following submission that was

in substance put by Mr. Eadie as follows:

On the basis of the evidence, the applicant should have

revealed details of the symptoms affecting her hand in

answer to question 1(j) and more particularly, question

5(a) or, failing that, question 12(b) in the entry

medical report form. She should also have answered

"yes" to questions 12(c) and 13(b) in the form.

Question 1(j) is admittedly rather less clear cut

than the others, but it should nonetheless have

been answered in the affirmative, particularly in

view of the fact that the applicant did not demur

when it was put to her that she had experienced

"tingling" since starting work in 1978 (see Tr.27).

But Question 5(a) clearly must apply. The hand is

part of a limb and there was a disorder in the

sense that there had been something wrong with it.

Further, on Dr Kneebone's evidence this had been so

for some time before he was consulted by the

applicant only a few months before the entry

medical examination.

Question 12(b) applies if the previous two

questions (1(j) or 5(a)) do not. The applicant

knew what had led her to consult Dr Kneebone in

February of the same year, and even Dr Corry was in

no doubt that this amounted to at least a

"complaint" (see Tr.57). In this instance there

would surely have been no real distinction between

an intelligent lay person's and a doctor's

perception of what is understood by the word

"complaint".

Mr. Eadie emphasised that consultation had occurred

only months before the entry medical examination.

Question 12(c): In cross-examination, the applicant

could give no proper explanation at all for failing

to answer "yes" to question 12(c), even though she

accepted that it was a question that was fairly

easy to understand (see Tr.30-31).

Question 13(b): In view of her medical history with

regard to the condition in question the applicant

should also have answered "yes" to question 13(b).

We would however here add our own comment that, not

for the first time, much of the trouble arising in

respect of this aspect of the case would have been

averted had the form to be filled in by the

applicant have been better designed. We need not

develop the point. The form has subsequently been

replaced.

(c) The applicant did not meet her obligation to furnish the

information required of her by referring to a "check up" in

response to question 13(a). Such information was of no

real value without being related to a particular context.

Early in her evidence, at a point when she seemed to convey

that she had written the words "check up" herself, the

applicant said (Tr.4) that the words meant "Only that I just

went to the doctor in 1979". In answer to a question from

the Tribunal (Tr.32) the applicant agreed that a "check up"

meant, to her, "a complete medical", and that Dr Kneebone

did not give her a complete medical. It is of no

consequence who actually wrote the words in the Form: the

applicant was plainly content to adopt them as her own

(Tr.4), although we accept her sworn evidence that those

words were not written by her.

As to (iii)

We likewise agree with the Commissioner's submission in

relation to this question. It is contained in Exhibit 8,

paragraph 5 and it is not necessary to refer to it in

detail. It is enough to say that, if we were to agree with

the Commissioner's submission under (ii) above that, if full

disclosure had been made, a BCC would have issued specifying

the condition "tenosynovitis right hand", it would follow as

the night the day that we would find that the applicant's

retirement incapacity would have been caused or, the better

view in our opinion, substantially contributed to by, the

condition that would have been specified in the BCC or by a

condition connected with that condition. In our opinion

the evidence indicates that the BCC condition was

essentially the same as the basic retirement condition

namely "tenosynovitis right forearm". We agree with Dr.

Brooks' opinion that the two conditions are for all

practical purposes the same, noting that he pointed out that

both the flexor and the extensor muscles and the tendons

cross from the forearm into the hand, and that accordingly

tenosynovitis in the one area will affect the other. In

saying this we mean no disrespect to Dr. Corry's carefully

expressed views in the helpful evidence which he gave to the

Tribunal. We simply prefer Dr. Brooks' opinion for the

reasons stated in paragraphs 5.15, 5.16 and 5.17 of Exhibit

8.

Would a BCC have issued?

16. We now turn to the troublesome question (ii). Looking back with the aid of that most valuable tool, hindsight, it would be easy enough to jump to a conclusion that a BCC would have issued once it was known that a keyboard operator had a history of tenosynovitis. The events of the 1980's in relation to "RSI" have been a fertile source, and a mixture, of positive debate, rumour, conjecture, scorn and even scurrilous abuse. Some, even medical people, have denied its existence, a view denied by the judgment of Davies J. in Margaret Muir French v Phipson Nominees Pty Limited (Decision No. SC.169 of 1984, 9 June 1987). That denial is not in our respectful opinion gainsaid by the terms of the judgments delivered in the course of the setting aside of that judgment by the Federal Court on appeal (Phipson Nominees Pty Ltd v French No. ACT G45 of 1987, 19 July 1988). It is however necessary to make clear here for present purposes that the "RSI" debate is not relevant here. As will appear, the medical evidence is that on the balance of probabilities the applicant would not in September 1979 have been diagnosed as having "tenosynovitis". She was however later retired on the basis of incapacity founded on the diagnosed conditions "tenosynovitis" and "osteo-arthritis", and "tenosynovitis" is the condition that the respondent has sought to include in a BCC. "Tenosynovitis" is a well-recognised condition. An authority placed before us indicates that it was observed in France as early as 1818 by Velpeau, and was later described by him in his "Anatomie Chirurgicale", published in 1825. In fact the present applicant suffered the kind of unjustified contumely that seems not uncommonly to have been visited on employees with very real complaints. An example is to be found in Ex.B, a document which should never have emanated from any officer, let alone from a senior officer, of the Public Service. It referred to the obtaining of two (moderately) updated typewriters for the applicant and for a colleague who was a fellow sufferer. Presumably before an Assistant Secretary signed it, a reference to the condition complained of was "whited out", and a very offensive substitute was inserted in careful handwriting. It should not have been done, and the letter should not have been signed. If of course the letter was altered after it was signed, the person who signed it would be exculpated but the person who altered it would be even more fit to be condemned. Mr. Eadie appropriately spoke of people "who should have known better". The whole incident was symptomatic of the treatment by her employer of which the applicant complained. The applicant's claim was essentially that her troubles arose out of the management of her problems after she was accepted for permanent employment. It is however not a claim with which we have to deal, and we say no more about it.

17. The question whether a BCC would have issued, had

adequate disclosure been made, specifying "tenosynovitis right hand", has two aspects. One relates to the medical evidence, the other to the then state of administrative practice. While they are interlinked, it is appropriate to deal with them separately.

The medical evidence

18. Dr. Kneebone's evidence on this point was -

(i) That the condition as seen in February 1979 fitted the

diagnosis of tenosynovitis

(ii) Asked to comment on Dr. Corry's report that the

incident of February 1979 was over in September of

that year and that the applicant was at no more risk

than an average worker of developing further symptoms

relating to over-use of her upper limbs, he said:

"None, except that I did ask her to come back and see

me in a fortnight and she did not return ... So I

assumed that the treatment had been successful ... And

then I saw her one, two, three or four times before

this sort of problem was seen again - was mentioned

again, rather".

(iii) Asked if it was reasonable behaviour on the

applicant's part, if the complaint had not recurred,

to believe that it was an isolated incident that was

over, Dr. Kneebone replied "Yes".

19. Dr. Corry is a consultant in rehabilitation medicine.

His evidence on this point was -

(i) He understood the history of the February 1979

incident to have been of a "relatively short duration

episode of pain in the hand, particularly around the

thumb and one of the fingers, which settled down after

a period of ... two weeks off work ... It settled,

from the history she gave me, completely, and she was

able to move from a part-time to a full time workload

afterwards ... The actual episode of injury was

relatively brief".

(ii) As far as tenosynovitis was concerned, in September

1979 "she had no symptoms, her workload was increasing

so that her capacity to use her upper limbs was

increasing over that time ... (I)f that were the case

I would regard a person as being healthy ... (S)he

would have, in all probabilities, been able to carry

through a normal working life".

(iii) In "1979 the incidence of these problems was

relatively low, and although I think everyone was

aware of the development of these problems and the

risks involved, they were not a major disability -

causing injury in Australia at that time. I would

have thought - if the history is as I understand it, a

relatively short onset at a time when a person was

getting herself back into the workforce, a relatively

short duration of symptoms, and then a period of time

where she was producing fairly high levels of work

without symptoms, then I would not have thought that

people would have taken too much notice of that

incident".

20. The medical evidence called for the respondent was that

of Dr. Brook, a specialist rheumatologist. The significant parts of his evidence on this point were if anything even stronger from the point of view of the applicant's case-

(i) He said that when he saw the applicant in 1983 (when

her condition, we would observe, was much worse then

it had been in 1979) he "did not think that she had a

disability from which she would be retired. I mean I

thought it was a relatively ... minor problem". Dr.

Brook said that in forming that view he almost

certainly had at the time been wrong, but the point is

that even in 1983 this had been the conclusion to

which this specialist practitioner had come.

(ii) In April 1983 he thought that the applicant had a

localised musculo-skeletal problem.

(iii) When he had seen the applicant in April 1983 she did

not describe the broader symptoms of RSI and she

developed them after going back to work in late 1983.

(iv) Asked again, in relation to the February 1979

incident, as to whether it should have been perceived

as evoking a real risk that the applicant would not

continue to maximum retiring age, Dr. Brooks said that

he thought that "given that there would be no clinical

findings at all when examined that is strongly in

favour of regarding it as an episode that is

finished".

21. The medical evidence on this point thus seems to be all

one way. We are unable to discern in it a foundation for a finding that it should have been concluded, had the facts in relation to the February 1979 incident been known, that a BCC would have been issued. Such should not, of course, have been issued unless there were valid grounds for concluding that there was a real risk that the applicant would not, by reason of the condition "tenosynovitis right hand", continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age.

22. It should be mentioned that the applicant gave evidence that she saw Dr. Kneebone in October 1979 over a problem with "sore hands", identified by her as swelling of the fingers in her right hand. It was honest of her to say so, for if her problems of February 1979 had recurred in October 1979 and been known, that could have given rise to difficulties. There are however problems about this story. First, Dr. Kneebone, whom the applicant consulted about medical problems as her general practitioner and whose notes were in evidence, made no record of such a consultation. Secondly, the applicant's leave records indicate no absence from work. Thirdly, in her evidence she ran the October 1979 story together (see Tr.16) with an account of the rejection of a compensation claim and the writing of the scandalous minute referred to in paragraph 16 above, a minute dated 28 May 1980. Taking everything into consideration we consider that we should disregard the suggestion that the applicant had a relapse of her condition in October 1979.

The then state of Administrative Practice

23. The medical evidence leading to the conclusion expressed in paragraph 21 above does not stand alone. It is also necessary to consider the administrative practice which obtained in 1979. Evidence on this point was given by Dr. D.M. Zonta, who is Director of the CMO office in Canberra and Manager of the A.C.T. office of the Department of Community Services and Health. She has former experience as a senior medical officer ("SMO") and then a CMO in Sydney. Dr. Zonta's evidence was clearly given, but she commenced as a CMO in 1981 and could not speak from her own knowledge of what would have happened in 1979. She did suppose that, in her opinion, a deferment would in the applicant's circumstances have been likely, and we shall return to this topic below. The other comment to be made about her evidence is that, naturally enough for a medical practitioner, her interest tended to be in answering the question whether tenosynovitis could have been confidently diagnosed and thus specified in a BCC. It is no criticism of Dr. Zonta to say however that this leaves out a step in the process.

24. The evidence of Dr. Valentino Boyce was of more assistance to the respondent. Dr. Boyce is an SMO with the Department of Community Services and Health in Sydney. In September 1979 he was relieving in Canberra as an SMO, and one of his functions was to oversee entry medical reports on candidates for entry into the Australian Public Service. He had overseen Dr. Winterbotham's report on the applicant and it was he who had written on the report the words "Review in 11 months". He said that had the applicant provided the information as to the February 1979 incident, he would not have regarded her as meeting the GMS. His concern, he said, treading on firm ground, would have been "whether there was a real risk of a recurrence or a progression of this condition". He said that he would "consider that there was a risk of possible early retirement as a result of this condition or something related to this condition". Yet he said that he would have regarded the applicant as fit for appointment under the Public Service Act 1922. This meant that Dr. Boyce accepted that, the applicant being free of symptoms when she was examined, she could be expected to serve for three years. When the Presiding Member put to Dr. Boyce the fact, explained below, that there had at the relevant time been at most one case in which a BCC had been issued specifying tenosynovitis in relation to a keyboard operator, he said that the only answer he could give was that "perhaps it was not as evident ... or people did not have it - I do not know why". But he said that tenosynovitis was not a rare condition medically.

25. We now refer to the evidence of Mrs. S.M. Biffin, who is the Director of the Superannuation Classifications Section of the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office ("AGRBO"). She stated that the "real risk" test was the test applied. Yet it has to be noted that her evidence was, when asked what would be the situation if an entry medical report identifies or refers to some relatively uncommon medical condition, that a delegate who was unfamiliar with the condition would probably consult a medical text or contact the CMO Service about the condition. If however, she said, the CMO has identified a condition she would, in most circumstances, issue a BCC for such a condition. This evidence however evokes a comment which we made above in relation to the evidence of Dr. Boyce, namely that it is not enough to have a condition diagnosed. The condition must be identified as one that gives rise to a real risk of not attaining retiring age, and we would wish to assume that that is what Mrs. Biffin meant. We return to this topic in paragraph 34 below.

26. In the course of Mrs. Biffin's evidence it became apparent to the Tribunal that it needed to know what was in fact the state of administrative practice in relation to the issue of BCCs for tenosynovitis at the time when the present applicant's examination took place. AGRBO deals with some 30,000 to 36,000 cases a year, and in the world before computers a file search for such cases would have imposed an unreasonable if not an intolerable burden. When however the Tribunal pressed the point, Mrs. Biffin helpfully volunteered that a computer search could be made which would identify what BCCs had been issued specifying the condition tenosynovitis in the financial year 1979-80 and before. The search was to be a file search of a "word search" kind. It would not of course identify files in which tenosynovitis had been diagnosed but in respect of which no BCC had been issued, but that would not matter. The question was whether the delegates were then issuing such BCCs. The hearing was then adjourned for several weeks for the search to be conducted.

27. The Tribunal wishes to express its thanks to Mr. Eadie, and particularly to Mrs. Biffin, for the generous spirit in which it was agreed to undertake the search, and to her and the staff involved for the efficient way in which it was conducted. We should emphasise that the mode of the search was such as to reveal nothing that would jeopardise the privacy of the persons whose files were being searched. The Tribunal itself does not know their names.

28. In private duty bound, the Tribunal does also wish to draw attention to its own role in the matter. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal does not have the resources to be a fully inquisitorial body, but it has a duty to seek out information which it regards as necessary for the decision of the case in hand, and when it identifies this need it will seek that information through the parties or, having obtained it aliter or through its own expertise, will expose it to the parties for comment. Any suggestion that the Tribunal simply remains content with the information proffered to it by the parties is quite wrong.

29. What then did the evidence show?

30. Only two typist/keyboard operators appeared amongst the cases given. If we are to hypothesise that a BCC would have issued in the present case the hypothesis would have to proceed on this basis: Giving the delegate a notional 12 months in which to come to a conclusion about the matter, the hypothetical BCC would have been the first or at the most the second BCC to be issued specifying tenosynovitis in respect of a typist/keyboard operator. That seems to us to involve a quantum leap in the world of supposition. If the medical evidence pointed strongly to a conclusion having been likely to have been drawn, in September 1979 with all facts being known, that the applicant had a history of a diagnosed condition such as would enable it to be predicated of her that there was a real risk that she would not serve to retiring age, it may be that we would have concluded that we should take that leap. But we believe that we have demonstrated that such is not the state of the evidence. We consider it quite unsafe on the evidence to conclude that a BCC would have issued within the kind of time frame indicated by the speed at which BCCs were being issued after entry medical examination in the cases set out in Exhibit 3 and listed above.

31. The question of the time frame raises the point that, had the facts been known, the position may well have been, as Dr. Boyce suggested, that a BCC would not have been issued there and then but that there would rather have been a deferment. But such deferment could not have been for long, and we have seen how in this very case the deferment for 12 months because of the condition "Dental caries" was overlooked or sidestepped. Had this case been deferred on the evidence of the issue of BCCs as set out above we would not be disposed to alter our conclusion. We refer again to the evidence of Dr. Brook in particular that he had not seen the applicant's condition as a real risk in the relevant sense even in 1983.

32. In our view therefore the decision to issue the BCC must be set aside.

33. There is one final comment that we would make as a general observation, based on this and other cases that have come before the Tribunal. In the years prior to 1976, when the new Superannuation Scheme came into force, it was customary for CMOs to identify lesser conditions such as "dental caries", "hernia", "underweight" and "overweight" etc. as conditions justifying exclusion from the Superannuation Fund, but not necessarily precluding acceptance for permanent appointment with Provident Account benefits. Generally the CMO in these cases recommended medical review in 6 to 11 months to allow the entrant to have the identified condition treated, with the intention that he/she should have the opportunity to qualify for the Superannuation Fund. In the applicant's case the CMO followed this pre-1976 practice of having identified "dental caries" alone, and recommended "review in 11 months". As previously mentioned, that review never took place, and evidence from Dr. Zonta (Tr.104) suggests that this "review" practice was recognised as a hangover from the earlier scheme, and was, post 1976, largely ignored.

34. The effect however in this, and in other cases, appears to have been the identification of relatively minor conditions which have in turn led the delegate of the Commissioner to raise a BCC if considered justified in terms of s.16(4) of the Act. The issue of a BCC for a condition such as "dental caries" alone, as happened in this case, brings into question the standard of judgment being applied by delegates of the Commissioner when they address the test(s) set out in s.16(4) of the Act. We have already emphasised the fact that diagnosis of a condition is not the sole criterion for issue of a BCC. Yet the evidence which appears at pp.166-167 of the transcript suggests that that is virtually the case. The evidence in question was part of an extensive answer given in relation to the extraction of the information obtained as a result of the computer search described above. Reference was made to the fact that the search had revealed that tenosynovitis was known (and had been assigned the code number 727) and that delegates were issuing BCCs during the relevant period. The evidence continued:

"The fact that there were only a small number of

cases - maybe I could put an even bigger

perspective or better perspective on this in that

in a general sense we would say each year we would

get around 30,000-36,000 new contributors to the

scheme and we issue probably between 11,000-13,000

benefit classification certificates, so your target

group for any given year is around sort of say

13,000 but we have such a large number of

conditions that are identified and a large number

of codes that we use you could select any

particular condition and you may not find that

using the specific words of whatever the condition

is that you are looking at you might not find a

large number of cases but you would within the code

group you might find quite a significant number.

DR TRAVERS: Can you just clarify what you just

said?---Yes, certainly.

About a third - of what you just said, of all

applicants coming in would have BCCs?---That is - I

would say that is accurate. That fluctuates a

little bit.

Yes. I know it is going to fluctuate a little bit

but based on the figures that you have just

given?---But yes, around - yes. And that has been

a fairly consistent pattern from 1976 to current

days."

In the light of this evidence, the Great Debate which took place as to the difference between the "more probable than not" test (enunciated in Re Bewley) and the "real risk" test (effectively the test stated in Re Dakin) would appear to have been irrelevant in relation to the respondent's practice. That practice appears to have been to issue a BCC where an intending entrant was identified as having, or as having had in the past, a diagnosed medical condition of known aetiology.

35. The answer does seem to be that mere diagnosis of a condition, or identification of a history of a condition, has been treated as enough justification for the issue of a BCC. When one takes into account that in this case dental caries, a condition that would surely not, save in an extreme case, be regarded as a threat to employment to retiring age was seen as warranting issue of a BCC, the case for an expression by the Tribunal of its concern is made manifest.
