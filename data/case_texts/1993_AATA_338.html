Re Taxation Appeals [1993] AATA 338; (1993) 27 ATR 1108 (12 November 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT92/293-VT92/296

AAT No. 9122

Number of pages - 5

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 338; (1993) 27 ATR 1108

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.H. PASCOE (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Amended assessments resulting from audit - unexplained understatements of income - burden of proof on taxpayer.

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936

Taxation Administration Act, 1953

Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 C.L.R 614

Commissioner of Taxation v Grbich and Shen 93 A.T.C. 4564

Omran v Australian Postal Commission [1991] FCA 406; (1991) 14 AAR 51

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 5 October 1993

12:11:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Bassal

Solicitors for the Applicant: Bunnett and Bassal

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Sest

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government

Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

B.H. PASCOE This application is for the review of the decision of the Commissioner of Taxation made on 22 July 1992 to disallow objection against amended assessments of income tax for the years of income ended 30 June 1986 to 30 June 1989, inclusive.

2. The amended assessments arose from an audit conducted by an officer of the respondent in which a 'Source and Application of Funds Statement' was prepared in respect of each of the relevant four years. The statement resulted in unexplained understatements of income as follows:-

Year ended 30 June 1986 $64,922

Year ended 30 June 1987 $82,717

Year ended 30 June 1988 $177,124

Year ended 30 June 1989 $120,974

3. On 31 August 1990 the applicant signed a statement agreeing to adjustments to his income of $50,000 in each of the four years. Subsequently, after placing his affairs in the hands of another tax agent, the applicant withdrew his consent to such adjustments. The respondent Commissioner then issued amended assessments including the additional income originally calculated from the Source and Application of Funds Statement together with additional tax based on a 30% culpability component and 14.026% per annum late payment component.

4. The applicant objected against the amended assessments and the decision to disallow those objections is the subject of these applications.

5. In the income tax returns lodged by the applicant in respect of each of the relevant years he described his occupation as "panel beater". From the statements lodged by the parties it would appear that he commenced business as a panel beater in 1980 and sold the business in May, 1989.

6. The objection lodged on behalf of the taxpayer by his tax agent contained these two specific grounds:-

"Withdrawals from bank accounts that the taxpayer did not

operate were incorrectly included as unexplained withdrawals

in the calculation of the understatement. Deposits and

withdrawals were made by other members of his family".

"Amounts listed as unexplained withdrawals were identified to

the Commissioner as the purchase of damaged cars, which were

repaired and sold. It also included purchase of materials

and mechanical repairs. Had these purchases and expenses

been accounted for the understatement would be reduced

significantly."

7. The hearing of this matter commenced on 3 September 1993 and the Applicant had been given notice of that date on 17 May 1993. In his opening address to the Tribunal, the representative of the applicant, Mr Bassal, submitted that the applicant, in addition to his normal panel beating business, had regularly purchased damaged cars which he repaired and, on many occasions, re-registered and then sold. No records were maintained for these transactions. It was said that invoices for the purchase of many of these cars could be produced and that it was reasonable to allow estimates for repairs, roadworthy certificates and re-registration costs. It was then claimed that, notwithstanding the lack of documentary evidence, "logic, common sense, the nature of the business and the law of the land all support his claim" and that "he did not come to be faced with technicalities that deny him his rights for natural justice".

8. After completion of his short address, Mr Bassal was asked by the Tribunal what evidence he would be leading in the hearing. Mr Bassal then stated that applicant was not available to give evidence as he was currently in Lebanon and he had no idea as to the likely date on which he might return. It was said that he had separated from his wife and had to travel to Lebanon to resolve the matter of custody of his son. No other witnesses were planned to give evidence to the Tribunal.

9. On application by Mr Bassal the hearing was adjourned for one week to enable inquiries to be made as to whether it would be possible to nominate a specific date on which the applicant would be available. On 10 September, 1993 a letter from the applicant's brother was produced which stated that the applicant "intends to return to Australia before December 25 of 1993, at the completion of the negotiations with the separation of his wife and the custody arrangements for their son". Application was made for adjournment of the hearing until after 25 December 1993. This application was opposed by the `respondent on the grounds that the applicant had been given adequate notice of the hearing, no notice of any adjournment application had been given until after commencement of the hearing, there was no undertaking that the applicant would be available at any subsequent date and information obtained by the respondent indicated that the applicant would not be returning to Australia. The income tax payable pursuant to the amended assessments had not been paid and the respondent sought early resolution to the dispute.

10. The adjournment application on the basis sought was refused on the grounds that no definite assurance could be given that the applicant would return to Australia to provide evidence to the Tribunal within a reasonable time and, notwithstanding the fact that more than three months notice of the date of hearing had been given no application for adjournment had been made until the Tribunal made it clear, after the commencement of the hearing, that the applicants case required some evidence to be provided. The hearing was re-listed to resume on 5 October 1993.

11. At the resumed hearing Mr Bassal sought a further adjournment until 4pm on 23, 24 or 25 November 1993 and it was proposed that the applicant should attend the Australian Embassy in Beirut to give evidence by telephone. This application was opposed by the respondent.

12. Given the lack of documentary evidence, it was clear that the view which the Tribunal would take on the credit of the applicant would be a significant factor in reaching its decision. Telephone evidence would not allow the Tribunal to note the demeanour of the witness, would severely limit the ability of the witness to testify as to any documents and would greatly limit the ability of counsel for the respondent to cross examine the witness. The expected length of time which would be required for examination, cross examination and re-examination and the eight hour time difference, when coupled with a doubt on the likely reliability of a long continuous telephone line to Lebanon, were all against this method of providing evidence. On questions being raised as to whether there existed an Australian Embassy in Beirut, Mr Bassal agreed that there may not be but, perhaps, some other location could be arranged.

13. Whilst recognising that, pursuant to Section 34A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal may allow a person to participate by telephone, it was no considered that it was appropriate for such a medium to be used for the evidence of the applicant in this case.

The Tribunal was referred to the decision in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Grbich and Shen, 93 ATC 4564 when Beaumont, J dismissed an application by the Commissioner for judicial review of a decision by Senior Member Grbich of this Tribunal to permit evidence by a video conference facility. His Honour quoted with approval a decision in Omran v Australian Postal Commission [1991] FCA 406; (1991) 14 AAR 51 when, in discussing the taking of evidence by telephone of a medical witness, Morling J paid (at p.54) "it may well be a sufficient compliance with the rules of natural justice for the Tribunal to make an arrangement whereby questions may be put to the expert by telephone without requiring his personal attendance. Such an arrangement would obviously be unsatisfactory in a case where the credit of a witness is concerned but this would rarely, if ever, be the ease with a medical expert". Beaumont J then pointed out (at p. 4570). "there is, of course, a difference between a cross examination conducted by telephone and what is proposed here".

14. Pursuant to Section 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 the burden of proving that the assessments were excessive lies upon the taxpayer. This provision was repealed by Act No. 216 of 1991 and replaced by Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 applicable to objections where the assessments to which the objections relate were notified after 1 March 1992. In substance the two sections require the same burden of proof and nothing turns on which section applies to this matter.

15. In this case the representative of the applicant provided to the Tribunal a brief, impassioned and possibly plausible explanation of why the "unexplained understatements" of income in the relevant years may not represent taxable income. However, no formal evidence or sworn testimony was produced. No comment was made in relation to the first of the two specific grounds of objection noted in 6 above. In relation to the second ground, whilst Mr Bassal stated that he could produce invoices for the purchase of cars, no evidence was to be led to prove that the applicant actually purchased the cars for income producing purposes or subsequently sold such cars. The absence of any sworn testimony of the applicant or any other witnesses meant that the burden of proving that the relevant assessments were excessive was not even attempted.

16. The Tribunal was referred by the respondent to the decision of the High Court in Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco (1990)168 CLR 614. Given the complete lack of any sworn evidence in this matter it is unnecessary to canvass in detail the views of the judgements in that case. A few extracts from the judgement of Brennan J will suffice. His Honour said (at p. 621):

"It would be inappropriate for a court determining an appeal to

make an order altering the tax liability assessed (s.199) unless

the court were satisfied that the amount to which it proposed to

alter the assessment represented the true tax liability of the

taxpayer. Although the grounds of objection limit the grounds of

appeal, the ultimate question for the court hearing the appeal is

not whether the grounds have been made out but whether the amount

assessed as taxable income is wrong. The burden which rests on a

taxpayer is to prove that the assessment is excessive and that

burden is not necessarily discharged by showing an error by the

Commissioner in forming a judgment as to the amount of the

assessment".

Later he went on to say (at p.623)

"The ground of objection on which the taxpayer here relies is

error in the formation of a judgment as to the amount on which

tax ought to be levied. But mere error in the formation of that

judgment by the Commissioner does not warrant the setting aside

of the amount assessed. Given the validity of the exercise of

the power to make an assessment under s.167(b), the ultimate

question is whether the amount of the assessment is excessive.

The amount of the assessment might not be excessive in fact,

though the reasons which led to the assessment were erroneous. In

George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1952) [1952] HCA 21; 86 CLR 183 at

p.201) the Full Court said:

"the law has always been taken to be that in an appeal from an

assessment the burden lies upon the taxpayer of establishing

affirmatively that the amount of taxable income for which he has

been assessed exceeds the actual taxable income which he has

derived during the year of income"".

And again (at p.624)

"The amounts assessed represent the Commissioner's bona fide

judgment as to the amount of the taxpayer's taxable income and

the power to make the assessment was validly exercised. The

assessments being valid, the burden was on the taxpayer to prove

that the amounts assessed were excessive.

The manner in which a taxpayer can discharge that burden varies

with the circumstances. If the Commissioner and a taxpayer agree

to confine an appeal to a specific point of law or fact on which

the amount of the assessment depends, it will suffice for the

taxpayer to show that he is entitled to succeed on that point.

Absent such a confining of the issues for determination, the

Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in proof of

the excessiveness of the amount assessed to uphold the

assessment, though the taxpayer is limited to the grounds of his

objection. In Gauci v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1975)

[1951] HCA 1; 82 CLR 177 at p. 186), Mason J said:

"The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that

the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory

requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported

by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be

inconsistent with s.190(b) for it is a consequence of that

provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the

assessment is incorrect, it will prevail".

"That view, expressed in a dissenting judgment, now prevails:

Macmine Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation ((1979) 53 ALJR 362 at

pp. 366, 371, 381); McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

((1979 143 CLR at pp. 303, 306, 323)."

17. In this case, the applicant did not discharge the requisite burden of proof and, consequently, the decision under review must be affirmed.
