Last Updated: 28 March 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Additional tax for incorrect return -Under-statement of separate net income of spouse - Whether taxpayer misled by spouse - Remissions of additional tax - Relevance of Income Tax Ruling 2517.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 S.223, S.227 Case U36 87 A.T.C. 266

Applicant

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT88/1445-1446

AAT Decision No 5110

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

26 May 1989

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL)

) No. NT88/1445-1446

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 26 May 1989

Place : Sydney

Decision : The Tribunal directs that the determination of the Commissioner under review be varied and that the additional tax imposed be reduced from $263 to $466 in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 1985 respectively.

(Sgn) PETER M. ROACH

Senior Member

REASONS FOR DECISION

26 May, 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. Ignorance such as the Applicant professed may be bliss, but exposure of ignorance in circumstances such as these is often attended by unpleasant consequences by way of income tax penalties. That was the experience of the Applicant, a retired man who had claimed and been allowed rebates of tax in sums of $483 and $717 in the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 1985. The rebates had been claimed in relation to his wife. In those returns of income he had stated her "separate nett income" at $830 and $734 respectively. His evidence in chief was that he had provided information to her as to the amount of her equal share of their joint earnings but that, in all other respects he had no knowledge of her financial circumstances. In consequence he was dependent upon her to advise him as to the amount of her "separate nett income". He said that she had done so and that he used the figures provided by her in presenting his returns: figures which in the event proved to substantially understate her "separate nett income". His further contention was that the extent of his ignorance as to her affairs was such that he was not at all surprised to find that the figures she supplied to him for her "separate nett income" were in each year substantially less than the figures he had supplied to her as being her share of their jointly derived interest income.
  2. When the understatements were discovered by the Commissioner, the Commissioner issued amended assessments which wholly set aside the rebates of $483 and $717 previously allowed. At the same time the Commissioner imposed additional tax for incorrect returns. Instead of imposing penalties of $966 and $1,434 (200% of tax avoided as provided for by Section 223 of the Act), the Commissioner carried out his duty to consider remissions and remitted the penalties to $333 and $545. The remissions so far as they related to rebates over-claimed were fixed upon as a result of the following calculations:

  1. The Applicant objected to the assessments only in so far as they rendered him liable to penalty. Although the objections related only to an amount of $742, upon the objections being wholly disallowed the Applicant sought an independent review of the Commissioner's decision. Such was his sense of outrage at the injustice he perceived himself to have suffered that he paid the high price of $480 for the "privilege" of making such a challenge. It was to result in a hearing in which he was paying at the rate of $160 per hour for the time of this Tribunal devoted to the hearing.
  2. To understand the objections it is necessary to consider them in the context of the circumstances of both the Applicant and his wife: involving also a consideration of their assessments to income tax for the year ended 30 June 1983. During his working career the Applicant had been a senior lecturer at a university. His specialty was statistics. He retired at age 60 in September 1982. Thereafter his sources of income were a superannuation pension; occasional lecture fees; dividends and royalties; and interest on investments, for the most part shared with his wife. His wife of many years - they had married in 1960 had been employed in secretarial duties and related matters. She retired from February 1983 and thereafter the couple travelled overseas.
  3. The Applicant claimed to have no detailed knowledge of her financial affairs and professed almost total ignorance of them. I accept that he was not aware of the detail, but I do find that he knew that she had been employed; and that he understood the rates of remuneration payable for her services were substantially less than those payable for his. I also find that he knew she had been in a superannuation scheme at the time of their marriage in 1960. He asserted that he did not know whether she had continued her membership of the scheme and whether she was entitled to superannuation benefits upon her retirement. I shall defer assessment of that evidence. I accept that ordinarily the spouses prepared their own tax returns.
  4. When the Commissioner came to investigate the tax affairs of the couple I accept that there were difficulties and confusions in two respects. In so far as questions are raised as to the amount of income derived, I accept that there was confusion in dealing with a bank and one building society, but I make no finding as to whether those difficulties were caused by the institutions or by the individual. I also accept that there were confusions and difficulties in dealings with the Commissioner. The Commissioner's staff necessarily declined to discuss with the taxpayer the tax affairs of his spouse but that confused and angered the Applicant because, he contended, his difficulties with the Commissioner were occasioned by the tax affairs of his spouse, and not by the action of his.
  5. It seems the end result of the investigation was the issue of amended assessments against the husband in relation to three years and, possibly, original assessments against the wife in relation to three years. The Applicant can be thankful that only he has had a need to object, and then only in relation to two years. Had there been a need for six objections (three for each spouse) the issues might have been as readily and shortly heard and determined but the price of justice would have been $1,440.
  6. As the total position in relation to the wife was not clearly presented in the evidence I have some reservations about the accuracy of the Table to follow. It sets out as accurately as the evidence permits what had been disclosed in the returns of income lodged by the spouses. The figures are based upon the returns of income which were placed in evidence but they are insufficient to fully account for the "separate nett income" attributed to the wife by the Commissioner in the assessment of the husband.

* The figures alleged by the Commissioner to have constituted the taxable income of the spouse of the Applicant in assessing the Applicant was not demonstrated to have been sound. Nor was it explained.

  1. The taxable income of the Applicant was increased by $1,851 in the year of income ended 30 June 1983. Presumably penalties were imposed but no objection was taken to them. Before me the Applicant's assertion was that he still contended as he always had contended, that he derived no such interest income from his bank in that year of income. It is clear to me that that has always been his contention, just as it is is clear to me that he attributed several errors to incompetence on the part of bank officers. However, I make no findings as to the merits of those matters. The significance of the evidence in relation to the 1983 returns of the spouses relies in what was thereby disclosed as to what was known to the Applicant as to his wife's financial affairs. There is no issue as to the calculation of taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1984. However, as to the year of income ended 30 June 1985 the Applicant acknowledges that, despite his professional background and despite having correctly brought to account in his working papers all interest jointly derived, he then demonstrated his humanity and his capacity for error something he was quick to criticise in others. He incorrectly divided by a factor of 2. He thereby understated his share of interest for that year on his own calculations by $1,000. However for reasons not explained, the assessment of taxable income of the Applicant was not increased by that figure. By setting one item of interest off against another, the Commissioner brought to account only $808.
  2. Due to the circumstance that the couple were overseas in the latter part of the calendar year 1983 they obtained deferments of the due date for lodgment of their returns. Being under some pressure to complete that duty upon their return they broke from tradition and for that year the returns for income for both were prepared by the husband. The principal relevant features of the husband's return were the record that it provided of a transfer from salaried employment to a superannuation pension, carrying with it the right to a substantial deduction ($1,655 for the part year) in relation to the "undeducted purchase price" of that annuity. The substantial calculations presented with the return in support of the claim for the undeducted purchase price indicated close attention to such matters. It is not without significance that in the closing three months of his employment $14,898 was contributed to the superannuation scheme.
  3. What was most significant about the return of income prepared by the Applicant for his wife for that year was that, although it recorded salary to February 1983 and payments on termination, it did not record any pension entitlement. Further, it was not attended by any claim for a deduction in relation to undeducted purchase price of any pension. In short, it discloses no pension entitlement. In so far as the Applicant gave evidence that he did not know whether his wife had a pension entitlement and that he had assumed that her claims to deductions, like his own, might have been substantial and included large claims in relation to undeducted purchase price, I completely reject his evidence.
  4. When the time came for the presentation of the 1984 returns the couple reverted to their traditional practice: each prepared his own. I accept that the Applicant accurately calculated the interest derived by them jointly and that he correctly disclosed the derivation of one-half of that interest in his own return. I find that as a result of that he knew that his wife had derived assessable income of $2,866. I also find that, by reason at least of his experiences the previous year, he knew that his wife had not derived any pension and had no entitlement to any substantial deduction in relation to 'undeducted purchase price'. I find that he knew that she had some earnings, but I accept that he had no precise information as to those matters. I reject his evidence that his wife had told him that her "separate nett income" for that year was $830 and I find that he had no belief, and no reasonable basis for believing, that her separate nett income for that year was only $830.
  5. The same findings are appropriate in relation to the 1985 year, although it is appropriate to note that due to a mathematical error the income by way of interest returned by the Applicant at $3,768 should have been returned at $4,768. Once again I find that the Applicant did not believe, and did not have any reasonable basis for believing, that his wife's separate nett income was only the sum of $734 disclosed by him in his return of income.
  6. When he was assessed, the Applicant was notified that the Commissioner had determined the separate nett income of the Applicant's spouse to be $6,250 and $4,280 in the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 1985. The Applicant complains that, even now, he does not know whether those assertions are correct. The evidence presented before me does not enable me to say whether they are correct or not. For the Applicant, I am satisfied that, had he wanted to do so, he could have found out very simply. He could have asked his wife. Indeed it was open to him in framing his objection to contend that her "separate net income" had not been so high as had been determined by the Commissioner. There might have been a trial in which those issues were addressed and it might have been one resolved in his favour. One can imagine such course being appropriate where there is a complete estrangement between a couple: the sort of situation described for one taxpayer in Case U36 87 A.T.C. 266: a case in which the only way the taxpayer husband could have ascertained what his former wife's separate nett income had been, and how it had been constituted, would have been by such a process. But in my judgment no such difficulty faced this Applicant.
    1. It is clear that one factor which substantially contributed to an under-assessment of the wife's taxable income was the fact that her claim to a half-share in interest was entirely omitted from her returns. I make no findings as to why it was omitted. It may be that she was not aware of her interest in the joint accounts. It may be that she was aware of her entitlement to claim upon those accounts, but not aware of any significance in the joint derivation of interest income. It may be that her omission to disclose the derivation of income in her returns was due to confusion, misunderstanding or oversight. They are all considerations which would be relevant to a review of any imposition of penalty against her. But such considerations are not relevant to review of the penalties imposed against her husband.
  7. He complains that this places all those who would seek to claim a spouse rebate in a difficult situation. He argues that, if a taxpayer is in a situation of not knowing the amount of the separate nett income of his or her spouse, the taxpayer either has to forego making the claim thereby possibly losing out on an entitlement to rebate; or to ask the Commissioner to allow whatever rebate might be appropriate, with the result that the taxpayer will never know with certainty whether or not his due entitlement has been allowed; or to state the spouse's separate nett income as honestly as he can and claim the rebate so seen to be appropriate. He claims to have followed the latter course as the only one calculated to produce a just result. Such an analysis can be appropriate, but in my view it is not relevant to the present situation. I am satisfied that in this instance the representations as to separate nett income were irresponsibly made with reckless indifference as to whether the statements made were accurate. Having regard to all of those circumstances I am well satisfied that in fixing upon a culpability factor of 20% the Commissioner was not acting at all unreasonably - if anything in the circumstances disclosed by the evidence the remissions were liberal.
  8. One question remains. As I indicated in my reasons for decision in Case U36 (ante), bearing in mind that at the material times a taxpayer who had delayed paying tax correctly assessed would have liable to interest on the unpaid amount at 20% per annum, it seems to be not unreasonable that, if a taxpayer achieved the same result by having the benefit of a delayed assessment induced by an incorrect return, use of the same rate of interest would be appropriate. However, recently the Commissioner has issued a new Income Tax Ruling, No 2517, in which he has proposed that the interest rate to be used to calculate a compensatory factor is to be 14.026% per annum. In my view if that is to be the normative practice to be used throughout Australia in calculating what is to be remitted, that circumstance should be recognised by this Tribunal and the benefits of that approach extended to all who appear before it, regardless of merit in their claims or the lack of it. In consequence, for that reason, I will review the calculations of remissions by substituting the lower interest rate in the calculation of the compensatory factor. The result will be that I will direct that the determination of the Commissioner under review be varied and that the additional tax imposed at $333 and $545 will be reduced to $263 and to $466 in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 1985 respectively.
  9. For the Applicant that will prove to be a most fortunate circumstance. He will not forfeit the $480 he was called on to pay for the exercise of what most would have considered to be a precious right in a just society: the right to have an independent review of decisions against individuals made by public officials acting in the name of the community.
