Re the Commissioner of the Australian Federal Police and Trevor Arthur Davies and the Commission of the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation of Commonwealth Employees [1991] AATA 238 (23 October 1991)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF THE AUSTRALIAN FEDERAL POLICE

And: TREVOR ARTHUR DAVIES and THE COMMISSION FOR THE SAFETY,

REHABILITATION

AND COMPENSATION OF COMMONWEALTH EMPLOYEES

No. N90/216

AAT No. 7494

Compensation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.N. Watterson(1) (Senior Member), Mrs J.H. McClintock, A.M.(1) (Member) and Ms J. Kalowski(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Compensation - federal police officer - investigation by officer of alleged social security fraud - interview in course of investigation - interviewee alleges officer demanded and obtained a large sum of money from him by threat - police officer denies - police officer arrested and charged under new South Wales' law by New South Wales police - police officer investigated by internal investigations unit of Federal police in connection with allegations - charges dismissed in committal proceedings - internal investigations discontinued - police officer claims stress and anxiety condition arising out of or in the course of employment by reason of arrest, charge and internal investigation - finding on the balance of probabilities having regard to the seriousness of the allegations that allegations are true - finding that action of police officer constituted serious and wilful misconduct - finding that police officer's condition not result in serious and permanent impairment - compensation not payable in the circumstances.

Commonwealth Employees' Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988 Sub-Section 14(3)

HEARING

SYDNEY

23:10:1991

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and for that decision is substituted the decision of this Tribunal, namely, that compensation is not payable in respect of the injury claimed by the Respondent, being an injury which did not result in serious and permanent impairment, and that any such injury was caused by the serious and wilful misconduct of the Respondent.

DECISION

This is an application by the Commissioner of the Australian Federal Police (hereafter the Applicant) for review of a decision made by The Commissioner for the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation of Commonwealth Employees (Comcare, the Second Respondent) in relation to Trevor Arthur Davies (the First Respondent, hereafter the Respondent).

2. On 5 January 1990 a delegate of Comcare determined that the anxiety state with secondary depression from which the respondent allegedly suffered arose out of his employment and decided that Comcare was liable to pay compensation in respect of such condition pursuant to the Commonwealth Employees' Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988 ("the Act"). The Respondent first sought treatment for his condition on 29 December 1988 and first claimed incapacity for work from 29 December 1988, and accordingly, the delegate found the date of injury for the purposes of the Act to be 29 December 1988. The Applicant now seeks review of the delegate's decision.

3. At the hearing of this matter, Mr P. Roberts appeared for the Applicant. Mr T. Boyle appeared for the Respondent. Miss Pownall for Comcare made a submitting appearance only.

4. At the hearing, oral evidence on behalf of the Applicant was given by Inspector John Edward Zimmer of the New South Wales Police; Mr Frank Zsifko, accountant at the Waverley Branch of the Commonwealth Bank; Mr Vernon Walter Turnbull, pensioner; and Detective Sergeant Valerie May Hoskins of the Australian Federal Police. The Tribunal also heard from the Respondent, Trevor Arthur Davies and on his behalf, Mr Garry Anthony Ward, retired Detective Superintendent of the Australian Federal Police.

6. The issues before the Tribunal may be stated summarily as follows:

(i) Did the Respondent suffer a "mental injury" (within the meaning

of that expression in Section 4 of the Act.)

(ii) If so, did such injury "arise out of, or in the course of" his

employment, in which case compensation is payable to him in

respect of incapacity for work resulting from such an injury

(according to Sections 4 and 14 of the Act).

(iii) If such injury did arise out of, or in the course of the

Respondent's employment, could such injury be said to have been

suffered as a result of reasonable disciplinary action taken

against him in which case such an injury is not compensable

(according to Section 4 of the Act).

(iv) Could such injury be said to have been caused by the serious and

wilful misconduct of the Respondent in which case compensation is

not payable unless such injury resulted in serious and permanent

impairment to the Respondent (according to sub-section 14(3) of

the Act).

Background

7. The Respondent was employed as a policeman by the Australian Federal Police in June 1978 and remained so employed until he retired with the status of Detective Senior Constable at the age of 36 on 20 June 1990. On 23 January, 1989 he lodged a claim for compensation for stress allegedly caused by his arrest by officers of the New South Wales Police Force (See "T3" - Claim for compensation). The Respondent was arrested on 7 March, 1988 as a consequence of certain allegations made by Mr Vernon Walter Turnbull.

8. Turnbull alleged that in February 1988 the Respondent extorted $10,000 from him and stole the sum of $400 and a number of video tapes from his premises.

9. The Respondent was arrested by officers of the New South Wales police on 7 March 1988 and charged under New South Wales' law with demanding money with menace and stealing.

10. According to the Respondent's evidence he was suspended from duty the day after this arrest.

11. The Respondent was discharged by a Magistrate at the conclusion of the committal proceedings in respect of these charges in December, 1988. The Magistrate concluded on the evidence before him that a jury would not be likely to convict.

12. According to the Respondent he returned to duty some time in January 1989 as a uniformed police officer stationed at Parramatta Family Court. He was stationed there until his retirement in June 1990.

13. Further, according to the Respondent's evidence some time after his return to duty he was approached by officers of the Internal Investigation Division of the Australian Federal Police on four or five occasions.

14. The Respondent's evidence on these internal investigations was far from satisfactory: it was vague and uncertain. However, the gist of his evidence on this matter is that officers of the Internal Investigation Division interviewed him on at least one occasion about Turnbull's allegations before he was charged by New South Wales police. On at least one occasion before the committal proceedings, officers of the Internal Investigation Division interviewed him in relation to Turnbull's allegations. He apparently refused to answer these questions on the grounds that he was facing criminal charges in respect of them. On at least one and perhaps more occasions after the committal proceedings and after he returned to duty he was again interviewed in relation to these allegations. At first he refused to answer. Finally he answered the questions put to him. Subsequently disciplinary charges were laid against him according to the Respondent "for refusing to answer questions the first time around" but these charges were not proceeded with.

15. Further, according to the Respondent on one other occasion an officer of the Internal Investigation Division had telephoned him at the Parramatta Family Court where he was stationed about a matter unrelated to the Turnbull allegations that had occurred years before at a time he could not remember but thought may have been 1984. An officer or officers of the Internal Investigation Division had subsequently interviewed him about this matter.

16. The Respondent gave evidence that he first started to obtain medical treatment related to a stress condition after he was charged by the New South Wales police and that he sought further treatment after the committal proceedings in which the charges against him were dismissed. He said that the internal investigations after the committal proceedings gave rise to massive pains in his neck and back and eyes.

Events Prior to Saturday, 27 February 1988

17. At the time of his arrest the Respondent was a Detective Senior Constable attached to the Fraud and General Crimes Branch, Eastern Region of the Australian Federal Police. On 2 February 1988 he became the case officer in charge of the investigation of one Ralph Walsh. Ralph Walsh was also known by the names of George Francis Henley and Hans Fischer. The investigation, carried out under the supervision of Detective Sergeant Valerie May Hoskins, was into the suspected commission by Walsh of various alleged offences under the Commonwealth Crimes Act 1914 of defrauding the Commonwealth. The alleged frauds related to social security claims.

18. Hoskins and the Respondent executed a search of Walsh's premises at 112 Doncaster Avenue, Kensington, and arrested him on Friday, 12 February, 1988. Thereafter the Respondent, as the Walsh case officer, had the responsibility of preparing the brief of evidence in the Walsh matter, including the task of obtaining statements from witnesses.

19. According to the Respondent the search and arrest yielded a large number of documents which indicated that Walsh had been drawing social security benefits in a number of names other than his own. One such name was that of a Mr Ray Smith. Further, according to the Respondent, a number of pornographic videotapes or movies, movie equipment and photographs of Walsh and some elderly male people dressed in women's clothing having sexual intercourse were also found during the search of Walsh's premises on 12 February 1988. He said that he had also seen some children's bikes at Walsh's premises and had been told by another person (by the name of Witherspoon) that Walsh had been seen with another elderly person and some children in the house.

20. The Respondent also gave evidence that a telephone answering service tape found at Walsh's premises on the day of the search contained a recording of a person saying "I have got another one to come in $50,000 or $5,000".

21. The Respondent's evidence was that on the basis of these pieces of information he formed the view that Walsh may possibly have been involved in child pornography: either importing it or making pornographic movies. The Respondent admitted however that he did not tell any of his colleagues or superiors that he was investigating pornography in connection with the Walsh investigation but he said that he had told some of his colleagues that something concerning pornography could be going to come out of the Walsh inquiry.

22. According to the Respondent the name V.W. Turnbull was also included among the documents seized from Walsh on 12 February 1988.

23. The Respondent said that on Thursday 25 February 1988, he visited Ray Smith at his residence, an old people's home, in order to obtain a statement. A statement of Ray Smith taken and witnessed by the Respondent on Tuesday, 1 March 1988 is in evidence as Exhibit A9. According to the Respondent, Smith was a potential witness in the social security fraud charges against Walsh, known to Smith by the name Hans Fisher.

24. The Respondent's evidence at the hearing was that at the conclusion of his interview with Smith, he would have followed his normal practice of asking Smith if anyone else might be able to assist him with this particular inquiry. He said that Smith gave him a few names including those of Turnbull and Goldfinch. He could not recall exactly what was said but he believed that Smith had suggested to him that "Turnbull" and "Goldsmith" might be able to help him with his inquiry into various aliases which he (the Respondent) had put to Smith.

25. In a letter of 19 September 1989 the Respondent says "Smith gave me the names of other people who knew Henley (aka Walsh). Some of these names I already had, one of these names was that of Vernon Turnbull of Henrietta Street, Waverley".

26. In his record of interview on 7 March 1988 (Exhibit A2, pp 3-4), the Respondent said that Smith had informed him on the day that he interviewed him that Turnbull might be involved in pornography. However, the Respondent told the Tribunal (TR227) that he could not recall whether Turnbull's name had been given to him in relation to social security fraud or child pornography.

27. Under cross-examination the Respondent said that after taking Smith's statement and as he was leaving, Smith had asked him about the Walsh matter generally. He told Smith that he had found movie equipment and pornographic videotapes at Walsh's premises. Smith then said "Child pornography. You should be speaking to Bathely. Do you know that Bathely is making inquiries relating to threatening letters that I am receiving and pornography?". The Respondent said this chance remark by Smith put a bit more credence on the connection between Walsh and pornography that had been running through his mind as a result of the search of Walsh's premises. The Respondent gave evidence that he contacted a Detective Sergeant Bathely of the New South Wales Police after speaking to Raymond Smith and notified Bathely of the seizure of videotapes at Walsh's premises.

28. The Respondent gave evidence that he obtained the address of Vernon Turnbull by doing a computer licence or registration check. He said that he called on Vernon Turnbull for the first time on Saturday, 27 February 1988 primarily in connection with his investigation into social security fraud but with the possibility in mind that Turnbull may have been in some way connected with child pornography.

The Respondent's Theory Concerning Turnbull's Allegations

78. During these proceedings, the Respondent advanced a theory to account for Turnbull's allegations against him. He referred to a conversation which he said took place between Walsh and him in Sergeant Val Hoskins' presence in a police car on the way back from one of Walsh's bail applications. On this occasion, which he was sure was the one occasion on which he had given evidence against Walsh's bail, the Respondent said that Walsh had asked him to arrange bail for Walsh in return for supplying information about senior police officers and politicians. The Respondent said he had declined Walsh's request and Walsh had then threatened to "fix" him. The Respondent said that at a later bail application Walsh (aka Henley) had objected to him (the Respondent) giving evidence against bail because he was then suspended from the Police Force because of Turnbull's allegations against him. The objection was overruled, he gave evidence against Henley and Henley absconded at the completion of his evidence.

79. The Respondent's theory as advanced at the hearing was as follows: he (the Respondent) believed that Walsh (aka Henley) and Turnbull were lovers and that they had got together some time after Walsh's arrest and before Saturday, 27 February 1988 to set him up. They had conspired together to have him charged with demanding money with menaces from Turnbull because he was the police officer who had arrested Walsh. Alternatively, the conspiratorial purpose of Walsh and Turnbull's ploy was to discredit him and have him suspended from the police force to get him out of the way so that Walsh could make a successful bail application. The Respondent said that Walsh had been denied bail while he was involved in the matter, but that when Turnbull's allegations were made against him the Respondent and he was suspended from the force, Walsh obtained bail. In the Respondent's view his suspension meant that his credibility was shot down in flames and he believed that "that was why bail was probably agreed to." He agreed, however, that Walsh had been caught red-handed with a number of bank books and other documents in his possession, and that the evidence against Walsh was comprised mainly of these documents and Walsh's own admissions. He further agreed that if he had been unavailable to give evidence in Walsh's bail case then Sergeant Hoskins would have been able to give that evidence.

80. Sergeant Hoskins gave evidence that Walsh was in custody after his arrest and that the first occasion that he appeared in court was on 13 February 1988. He was on this occasion granted bail but could not raise the bail. The police had opposed bail on this occasion but had given no evidence.

81. She said that Walsh remained in custody until approximately 10 March 1988 when he was granted bail by a Supreme Court judge. Walsh's committal proceedings commenced on 17 May 1988 and continued until the lunch time adjournment on 19 May when Walsh absconded.

82. Under cross-examination Sergeant Hoskins stated that on 22 February 1988 she and the Respondent went together to St James court for a bail application by Henley. The Standard of Proof Applicable

92. Both Counsel agreed that the Tribunal must be satisfied on the balance of probabilities as to the facts in this matter and the inferences to be drawn from these facts. They agreed that this was the appropriate standard of proof to apply and not the standard of beyond reasonable doubt, applicable in criminal proceedings.

93. The Applicant's objection to the Respondent's claim for compensation benefits requires the Tribunal to determine the probable truth or falsity of serious allegations made against him. These allegations have been adjudicated upon in committal proceedings. We have had regard to such evidence in those proceedings as has been drawn to our attention by the parties in these proceedings. But, of course, as each of the parties agrees, the decision of this Tribunal must rest ultimately on the whole of the evidence before us and not the evidence before or the decision of the Magistrate in those proceedings. We have given careful and detailed consideration to the allegations now before us having regard to their seriousness.

Conclusion

94. The Respondent and Turnbull agree that the Respondent first saw Turnbull on the morning of Saturday, 27 February 1988 at Turnbull's flat. They also agree that the Respondent returned to the block of flats in which Turnbull resided on the following Monday morning and that together they drove in the Respondent's car to the Waverley Branch of the Commonwealth Bank.

95. The evidence is that Turnbull withdrew $10,000 in cash from that branch while the Respondent waited outside in his car.

96. But the Respondent and Turnbull give two different versions of these events. According to Turnbull he withdrew the money and handed it over to the Respondent because the Respondent demanded the money and told him he would be in trouble with the police for involvement with pornographic materials if he did not do so.

97. According to the Respondent his visit to Turnbull on the Saturday was in connection with his investigation of a social security fraud allegedly perpetrated by one Walsh. According to the Respondent he was following a lead to Turnbull as an associate and former lover of Walsh, provided by Smith, whom he had interviewed in connection with the investigation of Walsh. His return on Monday was to interview Goldfinch, another associate of Walsh who lived in the same block of flats as Turnbull. According to the Respondent he was taking Turnbull down to police headquarters on the Monday at Turnbull's suggestion to obtain a statement from him concerning Walsh. In the course of this journey and also at Turnbull's suggestion he stopped at the bank so that Turnbull could conduct some business there. The Respondent denies Turnbull's version of events and adds that Turnbull's allegations are part of a plot hatched by Turnbull to set him up.

98. In determining this matter the Tribunal must decide whether to believe the Respondent's version of the events or Turnbull's.

99. If Turnbull's version of events is accepted then the Respondent's conduct in this matter clearly amounts to "serious and wilful misconduct" within sub-section 14(3) of the Act.

100. We have concluded that it is more probable than not that the Respondent demanded $10,000 from Turnbull, arranged to take Turnbull to his bank on the Monday to obtain the $10,000 and obtained that sum in cash from Turnbull outside his bank on the Monday. Turnbull handed over the money to the Respondent because the Respondent had informed him that he was a police officer and told him he was in a lot of trouble in connection with possession of pornographic material.

101. In arriving at this conclusion we have not overlooked the many different versions of the events that have been provided by Turnbull. However these variations concern peripheral matters: we are persuaded as to the truth of Turnbull's version of the central matters. On these central matters Turnbull's evidence is consistent. In our view the inconsistencies in Turnbull's version of events arise not because he is lying but are explicable having regard to his age and the lapse of time since the events and his testimony before this Tribunal.

102. This was the central allegation relied on by the Applicant in this matter. Having regard to the manner in which the case was presented to us and to our conclusion on this central allegation we have not found it necessary to make a finding in respect of the allegations that the Respondent took $400 in cash and three videos from Turnbull's premises on the Saturday.

103. Turnbull impressed us as a credible witness on crucial facts. Furthermore, while admitting to matters which in themselves might not receive community approval, he was candid as to his own involvement and did not appear to be deceptive. We did not form the impression from his presentation in the witness box or from any other evidence that he was a vindictive person who would or did fabricate a story against the Respondent.

104. Furthermore on the evidence before us Turnbull had no good reason and even less opportunity to fabricate a story against the Respondent.

105. According to the Respondent, Turnbull set him up by arranging for their joint visit to the bank on Monday so that he could withdraw cash from the bank and incriminate him. A motive for this set up as suggested by the Respondent was that if he were thus incriminated and charged he would not be available to give evidence at Walsh's bail application and this would improve Walsh's prospects of obtaining bail. But the connection between the alleged set up and its purpose is so remote and the likelihood that such a remote prospect would have occurred to Turnbull so fanciful that it must be disregarded. Furthermore, on the Respondent's own admission, even if he had been unavailable to give evidence in the Walsh case, Sergeant Hoskins would have been so available.

106. According to the Respondent, the set up involved Turnbull arranging for their joint visit to Turnbull's bank on the Monday. On the evidence before us, Turnbull had no way of knowing and did not know that the Respondent was to visit him until he did so on the Saturday morning. Furthermore, such a set up depended on the Respondent returning to visit Turnbull on the Monday. But the possibility of the Respondent's return on the Monday to visit the nearby Goldfinch was raised not by Turnbull but by the Respondent himself on the Saturday. Accordingly the Respondent would have had to conceive and execute such a set up in the short time frame between the Saturday and the Monday in circumstances where its successful execution depended on a step suggested not by Turnbull but by the Respondent himself.

107. We reject the proposition that Turnbull engineered the visit to the bank on Monday in the company of the Respondent as part of a plan to set up the Respondent and have him accused of or charged with extortion.

108. We have found as a fact that the Respondent and Turnbull travelled together to Turnbull's bank on the Monday and that Turnbull withdrew $10,000 in cash from his account. We have concluded that Turnbull did so in order to hand that money over to the Respondent and that he did hand that money over in consequence of threats made to him by the Respondent.

109. The Respondent went alone to see Turnbull on the Saturday. He believed at the time of his visit that Turnbull may have had a homosexual relationship with Walsh, the person he was investigating. From his investigations of Walsh for alleged social security fraud he believed that Walsh may have been somehow illegally involved in the production of pornographic material. He found Turnbull, an elderly male homosexual, alone, viewing a pornographic videotape. Turnbull no doubt presented to the Respondent as he presented in the witness box: a timid and vulnerable person. In our view the Respondent was provided with an opportunity to prevail upon Turnbull and his apparent fears about having pornographic material in his possession. In our view he took that opportunity.

110. Clearly the Respondent's conduct constitutes serious and wilful misconduct.

111. The medical opinions before us as to the nature of the Respondent's mental condition, its cause and connection with his employment were formed on the basis of the Respondent's version of events.

112. In the light of our findings it is not possible to rely on these opinions so formed as to the nature and causes of the applicant's condition. We do not have before us medical opinion formed by reference to the facts as we have found them.

113. Accordingly, and in the light of our basic findings of facts we have not thought it necessary or appropriate to make findings as to the nature and cause of the applicant's condition.

114. Nor has it been necessary to decide whether that part of the applicant's condition said to have been brought about by investigations carried out by the Internal Affairs Division of the Federal Police could be said to have been suffered as a result of reasonable disciplinary action taken against him. Such a decision is unnecessary in view of our decision regarding misconduct.

115. Assuming for the moment that the Respondent suffered a severe anxiety state with secondary depression from March to the month after his court case and that he experienced several further episodes of relapse following upon various investigations by the Internal Affairs Division as reported by Dr Maguire.

116. It is clear that the cause of the onset of any such anxiety state and any such relapses in the light of our findings was the Respondent's own actions in demanding money from Turnbull in the circumstances we have found. It was this serious and wilful misconduct by the Respondent which brought about the charges laid by the New South Wales Police and the investigations conducted by the Internal Affairs Unit of the Federal Police.

117. Any stress or anxiety experienced by the Respondent and associated in the Respondent's mind with his arrest and subsequent internal investigation is stress and anxiety brought about by his own serious and wilful misconduct. As such in this case any such injury is not compensable unless such injury results in "serious and permanent impairment" (see sub-section 14(3)).

118. However, it is clear from Dr Maguire's report that the Respondent's anxiety state was not permanent. At the time of that report Dr Maguire said that the Respondent had suffered from an acute anxiety state with several further episodes of relapse but was at that time psychologically well and fit for work.

119. It is clear from this report that any anxiety state from which the Respondent suffered was not regarded as permanent by Dr Maguire. Furthermore, when read carefully, Dr Maguire notes that

"If there are further episodes of investigation he

will probably develop further periods of relapse of

symptoms. Furthermore, if he continues to perceive

himself as harassed, he might go on to develop a

chronic form of the disorder as a result of

anticipation of further harassment and investigation."

This cannot be regarded as a diagnosis of a permanent condition existing in the Respondent at that time.

120. It follows that any anxiety state from which the Respondent suffered could not be regarded as a serious and permanent impairment. It follows therefore that compensation is not payable in respect of his claimed condition of anxiety state according to sub-section 14(3) of the Act.

121. The decision under review is therefore set aside and for that decision is substituted the decision of the Tribunal, namely, that at all relevant times compensation is not payable to the Applicant because the claimed injury was caused by the Respondent's serious and wilful misconduct.
