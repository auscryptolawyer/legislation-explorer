NT92/117-122 and Commissioner of Taxation [1992] AATA 846; (1992) 25 ATR 1057; 93 ATC 167 (3 December 1992)

Last Updated: 26 May 2009

Administrative Appeals Tribunal

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - application for review - extension of time - four years delay - alleged fault of agent - applicant does not speak English – principles for discretion to extend period

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s188(2), s188B

Chambairux v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1986) 74 ALR 480

Duff v Freiiah [1982] FCA 159; (1982) 43 ALR 479

Stergis v Boucher (1989) 86 ALR 174

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No:NT92/117-122

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ) NT92/251

Re:

Applicant

And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent

Tribunal: Senior Member YFR Grbich

Date: 3 December 1992

Place: Sydney

REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. This is an application for extension of time for the lodging of an application for review of a decision under s188(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. It involves a default assessment for $370,000, raised for the years 1981 to 1987. The Commissioner found against the taxpayer's objection on 9 May 1988 but the request for referral was not lodged until 1 May 1992. The 60 day period has been extended to almost four years.
  2. The application concerns the income years ended 30 June 1981 to 1987. The applicant failed to lodge returns for the 1982 year to the 1985 year. The applicant is an unemployed welder who claims he has been on sickness benefit since he received a neck injury in a car accident some four years ago. The Commissioner undertook a T account audit and issued a default assessment under s167. Under this procedure, the Commissioner matches incoming and outgoing cash flows for the relevant years, makes educated assumptions about the cash likely to be expended on living (taken from Bureau of Statistics average weekly household expenditure figures) and works back to an estimate of taxable income. Assessments were issued on 19 January 1988. An objection was lodged by the applicant. The Commissioner reached a decision on the objection and issued formal notices of disallowance on 9 May 1988. The request for a referral to this Tribunal was not lodged until 1 May 1992, almost four years later.
  3. The taxpayer's case turns, essentially, on the proposition that the delay is due largely to the faults of his first tax agent. The taxpayer claims that, since he does not read English and is unfamiliar with income tax procedures, he did not know what was going on. The applicant said that he gave all the papers to his first tax agent, F, and assumed that F was looking after his affairs. But, even assuming in the taxpayer's favour, as I am prepared to for present purposes, that the client is protected from the defaults of his first tax agent and there was no communication of the position to the taxpayer personally, it is still necessary to explain fully 14 months delay after the intervention of his solicitor and appointment of his second tax agent. Even assuming in his favour that the taxpayer was not informed of the delay by the first agent and that his lack of enquiry can be justified, when he changed agents he would have been put on enquiry and he must explain the delays after this date.
  4. The gist of the respondent Commissioner's case is that there is no acceptable explanation for the long delay, that the delay in resolution of the matter would prejudice the respondent and that there was little substantive merit in the claim of the applicant
  5. Let us turn first to the relevant statutory provisions. The normal time limit for referring an objection to the Tribunal is 60 days. It is common ground that the provision granting powers to extend this period is contained in s188(2) and this Tribunal is empowered to review the Commissioner's decision to disallow such extension under s188B. Both provisions were repealed in 1991 and replaced by provisions in Part IVC of the Tax Administration Act 1953. The provisions at the relevant time read as follows:

s188(2) Where the period for the lodgement by the taxpayer of a request under section 187 has ended, the taxpayer may, notwithstanding that the period has ended, send the request to the Commissioner together with an application in writing asking that the request be treated as having been duty lodged.

s188B(1) Where the Commissioner receives an application under subsection 188(2), the Commissioner shall, as soon as practicable-

(a) if the application relates to a request to refer a decision to the Tribunal - send the application to the Tribunal; or

(b) if the application relates to a request to refer a decision to the Federal Court - send the application to that Court.

(2) The sending of an application to the Tribunal under paragraph (1)(a) shall, for the purposes of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, be deemed to constitute the making of an application to the Tribunal to extend the time within which the request may be lodged with the Commissioner.

(3) The sending of an application to the Federal Court under paragraph (1)(b) constitutes the making by the taxpayer concerned of an application to that Court to extend the time within which the request may be lodged with the Commissioner.

(4) The Tribunal or the Federal Court, as the case may be, may grant or refuse the application.

(5) Where an application under subsection 188(2) has been granted, the taxpayer shall, for the purposes of this Part, be treated as having duty lodged the request to which the application relates.
  1. The discretion in the provisions is extremely wide. Under s188B(4), the Tribunal "may grant or refuse the application" for extension of time for lodging a request for a reference. Essentially, the task for making these rules is delegated to the Tribunal or to the Courts.
  2. The Tribunal was not referred to any authorities on the application of the relevant provisions. But the Tribunal, ably assisted by Mr Young for the respondent, was referred to authorities on analogous provisions. In Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs (1984) 58 ALR 305 Wilcox J considered an application for extension of time under s11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. In that case he distilled the principles on the exercise of the discretion from a number of authorities. The principles have been applied frequently under the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. They read as follows (at 310):

"(a) Although this section does not, in terms, place any onus of proof upon an applicant for extension an application has to be made. Special circumstances need not be shown but the court will not grant the application unless positively satisfied that it is proper to do so. The "prescribed period" of 28 days is not to be ignored (Ralkon v Aboriginal Development Commission [1982] FCA 153; (1982) 43 ALR 535 at 550). Indeed it is the prima facie rule that proceedings commenced outside that period will not be entertained (Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 at 416). It is a precondition to the exercise of discretion in his favour that the applicant for extension show an "acceptable explanation of the delay" and that it is "fair and equitable in the circumstances" to extend time (Duff v Freijah [1982] FCA 159; (1982) 43 ALR 479, at 485; Chapman v Reilly. Neaves J, 9 December 1983, not reported, at p7).

(b) Action taken by the applicant, other than by making an application for review under the Act, is relevant to the consideration of the question whether an acceptable explanation for the delay has been furnished. A distinction is to be made between the case of a person who, by non-curial means, has continued to make the decision-maker aware that he contests the finality of the decision (who has not "rested on his rights": per Fisher J in Doyle v Chief of General Staff [1982] FCA 124; (1982) 42 ALR 283 at 287) and a case where the decision-maker was allowed to believe that the matter was finally concluded. Compare Doyle. Chapman. Ralkon and Douglas v Allen (Morling J, 3 April 1984, not reported, at p18 of the transcript) with Lucic at pp414-15 and Hickey v Australian Telecommunications Commission (1983) 48 ALR 517 at 519. The reasons for this distinction are not only the "need for finality in disputes" (see Lucic at p410) but also the "fading from memory" problem referred to in Wedesweiller v Cole. [1983] FCA 94; (1983) 47 ALR 528.

(c) Any prejudice to the respondent including any prejudice in defending the proceedings occasioned by the delay, is a material factor militating against the grant of an extension: see Doyle at p287; Duff at pp484-5; Hickey at pp525-7 and Wedesweiller at pp533-4.

(d) However, the mere absence of prejudice is not enough to justify the grant of an extension: Douglas at p18; Lucic at p416; Hickey at p523. In this context, public considerations often intrude (Lucic. Hickey). A delay which may result, if the application is successful, in the unsettling of other people (Ralkon p550, Becerra v Fowell (unreported, Moiling J, 18 February 1983) at pp12-13 of the transcript) or of established practices (Douglas p19) is likely to prove fatal to the application.

(e) The merits of the substantial application are properly to be taken into account in considering whether an extension of time should be granted: Lucic p417; Chapman p6.

(f) Considerations of fairness as between the applicants and other persons otherwise in a like position are relevant to the manner of exercise of the court's discretion: Wedesweiller at pp534-5.

In considering the authorities it is, I believe, important to bear in mind the point made by Sheppard J in Wedesweiller (at p531) relating to the diversity of decisions of which review may be sought under the Act:"... there will be some cases which may be decided upon considerations which affect only the immediate parties. It will be appropriate to consider whether the delay which has taken place has been satisfactorily explained, the prejudice which may be caused to an applicant by the refusal of an application, the prejudice which may be suffered by the government or a particular department if the application is granted and, generally, what the justice of the case requires. In other cases wider considerations will be involved."

8. A number of factors are reviewed by Wilcox J in this case. First, the idea that an applicant should not give the respondent the impression that he has rested on his rights. Second, an examination of the extent to which the respondent's or third party rights are prejudiced by the delay. While a relevant consideration, the mere absence of prejudice is not, of course, determinative. Third, the merits of the substantive claim. That this last criterion can become unruly, was demonstrated at the hearing of this extension of time where the parties took fully two court days to argue this preliminary procedural point, with much of that time spent on rehearsing the substantive issues. Northrop J in Duff (post) thought it irrelevant.

  1. All that said, the emphasis in the test must be firmly on the steps actually taken and whether there was an acceptable explanation for the delay. Since the Tribunal will only grant the application if it is positively satisfied that it is proper to do so, this requires the assertion of factors sufficient to over-ride the prima facie position that the statutory period applies. While this does not in terms put an onus of proof on the applicant, Lockhart J makes it clear in Hickey v Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 176; (1983) 47 ALR 517 that it was tantamount to this. The application of the prima facie rule was emphasised by Fitzgerald J in Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411, 416. He gave reasons which, with respect, are not always given adequate weight by Australian courts: the need for finality in disputes, the appropriate allocation and expenditure of public funds and the imperative need for orderly administration. Tribunals and courts which have the luxury of full viva voce hearings and a lavish use of public resources for the resolution of a tiny minority of 10 million plus tax assessments can often lose sight of the imperatives of mass decision making. Care must be taken to ensure that excessive concentration on giving every last opportunity for fairness for those privileged few who can afford to get before a court or tribunal is not bought at the cost of effective, timely and affordable justice for the great majority who can not
  2. In Chumbairux v Minister For Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1986) 74 ALR 480. 490 Burchett J, citing earlier authorities, granted an effective five month s11 extension on the basis that the delay had arisen because of the applicant's emotional marriage dissolution at about the time of an adverse deportation order. He stressed the need to decide each case according to its own particular circumstances. Section 11 is also considered by Northrop J in Duff v Freijah [1982] FCA 159; (1982) 43 ALR 479. In that case a four month extension was disallowed because (at 485) the applicants had not explained in any satisfactory manner the delay in lodging applications. While he accepted earlier dicta, to the effect that if there was no prejudice to the respondent and the time of delay was small that a court would not find it difficult to make an extension, the entire lack of explanation was fatal.

11. Significantly, Northrop J in Duff found (at 485):

'It is well established that delays by a solicitor are visited upon the client when those delays are relevant to limitation periods or matters involving want of prosecution."

That said, there are authorities which hold that a taxpayer should not be penalised for the errors or neglect of his agent. In Case U175 87 ATC 1007 the Tribunal granted an extension where the delay in requesting the reference was the fault of the taxpayer's accountant. Mr Roach found (at 1018) that the delay "was occasioned by nothing more than an excusable oversight" by the applicant's accountant in mislaying a file while the accountant's office was being reorganised. It should be noted that Mr Roach did not present reliance on the professional advisor as a separate ground, bracketing excusable oversights by applicants and professional advisors. There is, of course, some problem in principle in a general rule allowing taxpayers to shield behind the defaults of their professional advisors. Dr Gerber in Case W23 89 ATC 253,256 quotes Taxation Ruling IT 2455. seemingly with approval. This ruling outlines a number of considerations to be taken into account. The ruling seeks to balance the responsibility of the taxpayer and the responsibility of the agent. Mere retention of professional advisor does not exonerate a taxpayer, although prompt instruction of an agent would be a factor supporting the granting of the application. The ruling concludes that, "it cannot be said that in every case where the delay is not the taxpayer's fault the application should be granted."

In Case X75 90 ATC 558, 560 Mr Hallowes said "one of the principles established by the decided cases," citing only U175. "is that neglect by an agent does not automatically entitle an applicant to additional time." It seems to me that the proposition should be put the other way round. Normally an agent or professional advisor acts on behalf of the taxpayer. The delays and defaults of the agent are imputed to the taxpayer. The taxpayer's proper recourse for professional defaults is against the agent. It is only where there are special circumstances that the taxpayer will gain any special insulation from the acts of the agent. Such insulation will not normally extend to taxpayers who do not make normal and timely enquiries of their agents.

  1. This case addresses the application of the provisions dealing with the extension of time for lodging requests for a reference to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The decision of Sweeny J in Fardon v FC of T 92 ATC 4339, 4347 applied Hunter Valley. the analogous tax provisions dealing with the time limit for lodging an objection in st88(1) and s188A. In that case, the decision of the Tribunal to disallow an extension was upheld. The taxpayer argued that it was some time before he became aware of a ground for objection and that he was putting himself in the position to pay the whole tax before he turned his mind to the objection. It was held that this did not justify the delay. The lodging of the objection would not have interfered with this other activity. The fact that he was advised by competent accountants was considered relevant. Sweeny J accepted the argument that the Commissioner would be prejudiced in proceedings to recover the balance of the tax.
  2. The Tribunal approached the case before it with some care because it was anxious to ensure that a migrant applicant, unfamiliar with the English language and the intimidating procedures in the tax legislation, was not prejudiced. But, even given the most generous interpretation of the facts, it is hard to justify the extraordinary delays in this case.
  3. The applicant gave evidence that his original tax agent, F, had been entrusted with his entire tax affairs. F remained his agent until March 1991 and, although during the relevant period he heard nothing from F, he assumed F was dealing effectively with his tax affairs and did not realise that any problems had arisen. During the relevant period, after the objections were disallowed, there was evidence that F was ill and the applicant had trouble reaching him. F was treated as the culprit and the applicant maintained he personally had no knowledge of any assessment F was not called to give evidence. The Tribunal draws no wider inference from that fact beyond noting it.
  4. One problem with this version of the facts is the evidence of personal contact with the Tax Office by the applicant in this period. In her affidavit of 7 August 1992 Ms B, an employee in the debt collection section of the Tax Office, swears that a Supreme Court Statement of Liquidated Damages in the debt recovery action was served on the applicant on 12 September 1989.
  5. Soon after 12 March 1991, on learning of an action to recover tax for the relevant years from a Tax Office letter, the applicant went to his solicitor, who advised him to go to a new tax agent, M. From that date onwards it is clear that the applicant was personally aware of the delay and could have been expected to monitor the progress of his professional advisors. At that stage, M wrote to the Tax Office to get a copy of the applicant's tax papers. Apparently M could not get in touch with F on the telephone number given by the applicant. It was put to him that F was practicing normally as a tax agent and could have been reached on a telephone number in the telephone directory. The applicant's case was that after M and the solicitor were apprised of the matter, further delay was caused because of their attempts to gain information, including a Freedom of Information request of the respondent. But there was fully 14 months delay to explain and there can be no basis, from this point of time, to deny that the taxpayer shared or should have shared knowledge of the delay with his professional advisors.
  6. Let us focus on that 14 months from March 1991 until the lodgment of this application. As Or Gerber says in Case W23 89 ATC 253,256, the taxpayer must show that the application (in that case the objection) "was lodged as soon as circumstances reasonably permitted" after any excusable delay. Is there an acceptable explanation for delay which is sufficient to over-ride the prima facie rule that extensions outside the statutory period will not be allowed? The essence of the applicant's case is that the delays during this period could be justified because of the difficulties M had in getting information. The basis of this assertion is strenuously resisted by the respondent
  7. The applicant's second tax agent, M, explained further delays on the basis that obtaining the taxpayer's books and records was a long drawn out process and that during this period he did not have full knowledge about the nature of the objections even though he was well aware that it was well past the normal time for referring the matter further. M took over in March but it was not until September or October that some records arrived from F. The suggestion was made that F had "disappeared", but this was closely contested in cross-examination and it is difficult, given the urgency of lodging a reference, to escape the inference that F could have been reached with persistence. Particularly since he continued to practice and his name was in the telephone book. If the applicant and his new advisors had taken the 60 day time limit seriously, they would have made concerted efforts to get information at this time. At that time M said he had no real knowledge of the precise nature of the objections. He said he had trouble reaching anyone in the Tax Office who could deal with the matter. It was not until March 1992 that Ms B, a debt collection official, rang the applicant's solicitor. In cross examination it was put to M that it was a further 13 months before he applied for an extension of time and that he might have lodged a protective application for review in the meantime. It was put to M that a Tax Office employee, Mr C, had told M on 10 October 1991 that the objections were out of time but M could not recall this. But, whether told or not, the professional advisors should have known of the time limit. Ms B gave evidence in her affidavit, supported by a contemporaneous file note of the conversation, that, on 12 November 1991 she had advised the applicant's solicitor, A, to apply for an extension of time to refer the request to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal but A did not inform his new accountant M of such matters until 5 March 1992 and no action followed. Again, it was put to M that Ms B had told him on 9 March 1992 that the objections were out of time. No satisfactory explanations were given for further delays.
  8. Objection was raised to hearsay evidence from Ms B, relying on the file note of Mr C about the interview on 10 October. Whilst this Tribunal is not bound by the strict rules of evidence and the existence of a file note taken in the normal course of interviews might be taken to be reasonably reliable, 1 have not relied on this evidence because there was no adequate opportunity for cross examination of Mr C.
  9. M alleged there were difficulties in getting anybody in the respondent's office to talk about the applicant's affairs. He obtained some records in September to October, rang for more information in November and eventually in February/March 1992 Ms B rang him. A Freedom of Information application was lodged but not pursued after the first tax agent finally sent the relevant material. But the respondent's employee, E, gave evidence that, notwithstanding the fact that the required fee was not lodged and there was lack of full compliance with the formalities, endeavours were made to locate relevant documents. On 2 October the applicant's agent, M, suspended the application. So, by this date, by his own act, he seemed to be conceding he had the information he needed.
  10. During the relevant period there were a series of contacts and steps taken by the respondent to claim tax, to take processes to enforce it and to obtain responses by the applicant or his agents. These are detailed in the affidavit of Ms B dated 7 August 1992. There is a pattern of lack of responses to letters and interviews.
  11. There are a number of features about the applicant's evidence which raise questions about his credibility. He gave specific evidence that from 1981 to 1987 he worked in a specified coffee bar and that it made no money during this period. For the period from 1981 to 1986 the applicant conceded in cross examination that he received unemployment benefits. During the same period, he signed a number of applications for bank loans to the National Australia Bank which contained details of other income earned. In a loan application dated 10 March 1983 he stated that he received net pay of $1,560 per month and, during the period of the loan, made monthly payments of $396. In an application for a line of credit dated 18 March 1985 he stated that he had take home income from a business of $3,000 per month (shown as the same figure as gross income) and during the period of the loan made monthly payments of $396. He conceded in cross examination that he was prepared to tell a lie to get a loan from the bank. While declarations on his loan applications, which he admitted in evidence were untruths, does not necessarily mean that evidence on oath is untrue, it does invite the Tribunal to test all his evidence carefully and, in particular to test his claims that he was not earning income in the relevant period.
  12. The evidence before the Tribunal is contradictory about aspects of the applicant's employment. A number of loan applications were annexed to the affidavit made by an tax office auditor Mr M. One of these (admitted as representative, exhibit B) gives the applicants occupation as welder while others clearly state his occupation as the proprietor of a coffee lounge. Further complicating the matter, in late 1987 the applicant signed a statement that his only source of income between July 1981 and June 1985 was unemployment benefits. During much of the same period he both put down his occupation as and did not contradict the view that he worked in a coffee bar and was, from 1987, manager. Now, obviously, not all businesses make profits or pay their employees or managers in every year but the applicant continued in this capacity for a number of years and did not declare any income during the relevant period. During the same period he declared he made considerable income from business and managed to keep up payments on the loans, based on the "false" income assumptions, without default and to pay out the loans early. Coincidentally, there are no records of cash received or payments made by the coffee bar during the relevant period. I find on the balance of evidence that he did earn income, the amounts of which are unknown, from this source.
  13. The applicant gave evidence that, while he could speak English and read numbers, he could not read written English. Yet the tax official, Mr M, who took extensive notes of the audit interview with the applicant gave evidence that the applicant read over the notes of interview and made substantive corrections to the transcript. Further Mr M gave evidence, backed up by a contemporaneous file note (and a statement dated 17 September 1987, exhibit B), that the applicant said he earned income only from unemployment benefits in the tax years 1982 to 1985. So far as the evidence conflicts, I find Mr M the more credible witness. At other key points, there were inconsistencies in the applicant's account about how he obtained income. In evidence he maintained that cash flows could be explained by gifts and loans from his parents-in-law overseas, both in bank transfers to his local Westpac bank and when amounts of in the range of $30,000 were brought back after visits to the US in 1985 and 1988. The gifts from his father-in-law are covered. But other significant evidence was not raised during extensive audit interviews.
  14. Lack of prejudice to the respondent will not, of itself, be sufficient basis for allowing an extension of time but prejudice will be a relevant factor in deciding whether to grant an extension. In particular, as Hill J recognises in Stergis v Boucher (1989) 86 ALR 174, 180-1, the longer the period, the more onus there is to demonstrate an acceptable explanation for delay. If evidence is made more difficult to obtain or recovery of tax is made the harder, these will be relevant factors. The relevant period must be judged in the context of all the facts and in the light of the statutory period. Hill J emphasises the need to act with some promptness in order to gain finality. In that case, three months was not considered unreasonable in all the circumstances and the s11 extension case was allowed. In this case the delay is almost four years.
  15. In this case there are arguments based on prejudice. The respondent argues that bank records which would allow the confirmation of applicants allegations, available at the time of audit, were destroyed by the banks in the normal course. The respondent argues that recovery of tax will be made more difficult as a result of the delays. While the first of these factors is particularly significant and does disclose a degree of prejudice, there is evidence on the record on which the respondent can rely and this factor is not determinative. These factors are, however, weighed in the balance and assist the respondent's case.
  16. The applicant relied heavily on the argument that, after the appointment of the new tax agent, the level of enquiries and Freedom of Information proceedings were such that there was no resting on their rights. Granted this argument, certainty after the change of tax agents, it is a relevant factor in the balance but it can not be determinative. Someone who was dearly going to refer an unfavourable objection and, due to gross inefficiency and without any satisfactory explanation, fails to do so for a long period can hardly, for that reason alone, expect an extension of time. But it can and is relevant to some of the assertions of prejudice put forward by the respondent. The delay caused in getting the new information by the second tax agent can certainly explain some delay after this date. Putting aside the issue whether the imputed delays of his agent should be sheeted home to the applicant, the applicant's difficulties with the English language and unfamiliar procedures may go some way to excuse his alleged failure to know what was going on up to the change of agents. But weighing the overall delay when compared with the 60 day statutory period, the considerable delay after the appointment of the new agent, a degree of prejudice to the respondent, the failure to pursue the first tax agent with sufficient vigour and, in particular, the long period of time elapsing, even after the applicant was put on notice of problems of delay and could have gathered the information, the exercise of the discretion in this case can only be against the applicant.

28. Accordingly, for the reasons given, the Tribunal refuses the application for extension of time and accordingly this application falls outside the statutory period.
