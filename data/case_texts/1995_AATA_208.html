REASONS FOR DECISION



31 July 1995 Deputy President G.L. McDonald



THE APPLICATION AND THE HEARING



1. The applicant is appealing against a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection, dated 22 August 1994, against a numbered notice of assessment, issued on 29 July 1994, for the tax year ending 30 June 1991.



2. At the hearing, Mr R. Hart, a barrister and solicitor, represented the applicant. The applicant gave oral evidence. Mr P. Bowen, a barrister and solicitor, represented the respondent. The respondent called no oral evidence. The documents filed for the purposes of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”), supplementary documents produced on behalf of the applicant (exh B, pp.1-43) as well as a property valuer’s report (exh C) and, by consent, a file note taken by a bank employee tendered by the respondent, constituted the evidence before the Tribunal.



THE BACKGROUND



3. The applicant is, and has been since 1980, a self-employed builder. In 1988 he and a friend, who is a bricklayer, purchased, as partners, a suburban block of land in a town in northern Tasmania (“the town”). In August 1988, they commenced building two units on the land (“the units”). They applied for the land to be divided into two lots. Since both partners had other businesses and/or employment, the construction was carried out in their spare time, at the end of the day, at weekends and over holidays. In February or March 1990, the partners borrowed moneys from a bank (“the first bank”), secured against the property, to allow for the completion of the units. The construction of the units was completed in September 1990. The units were listed for sale from 29 August 1990, through local estate agents. The units were rented from 29 September 1990 to 4 December 1990. The contract for sale for each of the units was signed with settlement due on the first unit on 19 November 1990, and for the second unit on 15 November 1990. Strata titles were issued on 27 November 1990. In fact, settlement for both units occurred on 3 December 1990.



THE ISSUES



4. The Commissioner has assessed the profit arising from the sale of the units as income under the provisions of s.25A(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) on the basis that the applicant is involved in building properties for sale and that, consequently, the construction and subsequent sale of the units is considered as a part and parcel of his earning of assessable income under ordinary concepts (exh A, T2, p.3). The applicant challenges the assessment on the basis that the land was acquired and the units built on it with the intention of their being retained for purposes of earning assessable income by way of rental. The applicant claims the units were sold in circumstances connected with the applicant and his partner acquiring another property, also intended for purposes of retention to obtain rental income, and the sale of the units should, in those circumstances, be determined as a “. . . variation or transposition of investment . . .” (para 6 of the applicant’s statement of facts and contentions). The applicant bears the onus of establishing the assessment as excessive (s.14ZZK(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953).



THE FACTS AND CONSIDERATION



5. In addition to the facts set out in paragraph 3 hereof, the Tribunal is satisfied as to the following. The purchase of the land and the building of the units was not the first venture of the partners. The applicant told the Tribunal that they had previously built two houses and three units in the town for purposes of resale. They had also purchased another property, which they had rented over a five year period until the house on it burnt down, whereupon they had built and subsequently sold units on the same site. They had also purchased a forklift which was rented to a logging contractor. The applicant said in his evidence to the Tribunal that it was the intention of the partners at the time they purchased the property on which they built the units in question, to retain them as part of a strategy to build up a portfolio of assets which would return them income.



6. In June or July 1990, the partners’ interest was attracted to a factory for sale in the town. On 17 August 1990, after some negotiation, they entered into a contract to purchase the factory for $65,000, with settlement due on 21 September 1990. The applicant told the Tribunal that the partners regarded the purchase price of the factory as being very desirable, given that they had estimated its market value to be greater than the asking price. It would seem from a Notice to Complete, issued by the vendor of the factory, that settlement was extended to 3 December 1990, i.e. the same date as settlement occurred with respect to the sale of the two units. Shortly after entering into the agreement to purchase the factory, the partners, in order to secure an off-street parking facility for use in conjunction with the factory, considered and purchased a vacant lot adjoining the factory which had become available for sale. After purchasing the factory and the adjoining lot (together described as “the factory site”), the partners rented it to a friend or acquaintance who was a tradesman engaged in the building industry, and it returned an assessable income. Apparently some time later the tenant became a part-owner with the partners in the factory site.



7. The applicant said that the partners had attempted to offer the units as security to a bank (“the second bank”) for the purchase of the factory site, and that they had originally hoped to keep both the units and the factory site. This subsequently proved to be impossible. In his evidence, the applicant outlined a prospective arrangement with the second bank pursuant to which it was proposed that it would take security over the units in relation to the opening of a nominated account to allow the purchase of the factory site to proceed. This was confirmed by a letter from the applicant’s solicitors to the second bank, dated 14 August 1990 (exh B, p.32). The letter undertakes to provide a valuation from a nominated valuer, which in the context clearly relates to the units. Exhibit C is a valuation from the nominated valuer, dated 31 August 1990, but it values the factory site. No valuation was tendered for the units. A letter dated 9 October 1990, from the applicant’s solicitors to the solicitors acting for the vendor of the factory site, states inter alia (exh B, p.15):



“ . . . We confirm that [the taxpayer] is arranging funds to complete this purchase [ of the factory site] from his . . . account with [the second bank]. We confirm that our initial instructions were that security offered was to be [the units] jointly owned by [the partnership] named in the Transfer documentation. Subsequently [the units] has [have] been sold with completion due towards the end of this month or shortly thereafter. Obviously this property is no longer capable to be used as the substitute security under the [ second bank] financing arrangements. . . . .”



The letter goes on to say that a proposal had been put to the second bank to substitute the factory site for that of the units. A subsequent letter, passing between the same solicitors, dated 30 October 1990 (exh B, p.12), reports that the factory site was not considered a suitable substitute for the type of account that the partnership was seeking to utilise through the second bank. The letter also states that there had been a falling out between the partnership and the second bank and that finance would be secured through the first bank. A letter from the first bank, dated 26 October 1990, to the applicant’s solicitors confirmed that it would transfer the security it held over the units to the factory site. There is nothing in the correspondence, particularly in the letter of 9 October 1990, to suggest the decision to sell the units followed a decision by the second bank to reject the units as offering suitable security against the purchase of the factory site. If anything, the letter suggests that it is the sale of the units which has led to the partners attempting to change their arrangements with the second bank rather than a circumstance where the second bank has refused the security of the units or, if not refused them, imposed financial disincentives to make the offer of the units as security less desirable to the partners.



8. Exhibit 1, submitted by consent, is a witness statement from a bank teller employed by the applicant’s partner’s bank. In it the teller deposes, apparently in relation to an application for a loan being made by the partner for the purchase of a property unconnected with the property the subject of these proceedings, the following:



“ . . . [the partner] told me that he has a share in two units [being the units the subject of these proceedings] nearing completion. These were available for sale on the market at an expected price of $60,000 each. He also told me that the loan owning on these units will be repaid on the sale of the units.”



A connected diary note dated 19 February 1990 confirms the loan owing by the partner to his bank on the property on which the units were being built would be repaid on the sale of the units, which were noted as being nearing completion. The evidence would seem to show that, at least in as far as the applicant’s partner was concerned, he contemplated as early as February 1990, the units being sold upon completion. However, despite the Tribunal not being bound by the rules of evidence as to how it may inform itself (see s.33(1)(c) of the AAT Act), the Tribunal regards it as being unsafe to rely on evidence, even where admitted by consent, of a type which would seek to attribute to the applicant an intention expressed by the applicant’s partner to a third party. Accordingly, the Tribunal has disregarded this evidence entirely in reaching its decision.



9. It was submitted that the sale of the units was prompted only by the desire of the partners to purchase the factory site, which they regarded as a better long-term prospect. It was submitted that this suggests that the purchase of the factory site was in substitution for the retention of the units when it became apparent to the partners that they would not be able to finance the purchase of both the factory site and the units. The applicant said that the partners’ intentions with respect to the units was made plain by the fact that the units were rented after completion in accordance with the partners’ original intentions. The applicant pointed to the fact that the tenant in one of the units used a number of picture hooks which caused slight damage to the walls. The damage needed to be repaired and the walls repainted before the property was acceptable to an incoming purchaser. He said that if it had been the intention of the partners to sell the units at the time the properties were rented, it would have been a condition of the leasing agreement with the tenant that no pictures were to be hung on the walls. However, the Tribunal notes that the decision to sell the units was taken at the end of August 1990, long before the units were let. Having regard to this, the Tribunal is not able to conclude that the letting of the units was part of the realisation of a long-term desire by the partnership to retain them. The fact that some minor damage may have been caused to the walls as the result of the tenant mounting pictures is, given the occupations of both of the partners, so minor a feature as to lead the Tribunal to accord no weight to this aspect.



10. The respondent invited the Tribunal to consider the character of the business in which the partnership had been engaged for a number of years. The history of the partnership shows, with one exception, that it took profits from building and selling houses and units. With respect to the one exception, the partnership purchased a property which it retained for five years until the house on the property was burnt down. Thereafter, the partners built a new dwelling or dwellings on the site and it was sold - the sale being effected to allow the partnership to proceed with the building of the units the subject of these proceedings. In the context of the way in which the partnership had carried on its business with respect to real estate acquired by it over a number of years, the retention of one building for longer than was normally the practice of the partners, does not change the characterisation of their business, which the Tribunal is satisfied is one in which they were open to take profits where they could.



11. The applicant seeks to characterise the sale of the units as a realisation of a capital asset not acquired for the purposes of profit making by sale as part of a profit making undertaking or scheme. Mr Hart submitted that it is the intention of the applicant which should be determinative. It was said that that intention was expressed in the applicant’s evidence to the Tribunal and was supported by the evidence that the partners offered the units as security to secure a loan from the second bank to allow the purchase of the factory site. The Tribunal is satisfied that the evidence vis-á-vis the application for a loan from the second bank does not support the conclusion sought to be drawn from it. Further, despite the applicant’s oral evidence to the contrary, the Tribunal is satisfied that the units were sold when the opportunity arose, in accordance with the way in which the partners had been carrying out their business to date. In those circumstances, it cannot be regarded as constituting a long-term investment pursuant to which the partners hoped to gain assessable income. In reaching this conclusion the Tribunal has taken into account the activities of the applicant in his capacity as a partner with his friend only, and has treated as irrelevant the fact that the applicant operated in his full-time earning capacity a business as a builder.



12. The Tribunal was referred to a number of cases by both parties relating to the interpretation of s.25A(1) of the Act. In light of the findings made by the Tribunal as to the facts however the cases shed little illumination on the plain meaning of the section. The Tribunal is satisfied on the facts that the applicant has sold the units as part of a business in the course of carrying out a profit making undertaking or scheme and that profit so arising is assessable under the provisions of s.25A(1) of the Act.



13. For the reasons stated, the decision under review is affirmed.
