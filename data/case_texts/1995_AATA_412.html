Lenehan and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1995] AATA 412 (20 January 1995)

Last Updated: 23 September 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



Extension of Time - whether appropriate for respondent to attack credit of applicant in an extension of time hearing - whether obvious and easily demonstrated flaw in applicant’s case - whether appropriate to make findings as to the credit of an applicant and merit of an application in an interlocutory hearing.

Re: CHRISTOPHER LENEHAN

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 January 1995 Mr J. Handley, Senior Member



The applicant applied to extend the time to lodge an application for review pursuant to s.29(7) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the Act”). The decision sought to be reviewed was a decision of the respondent made on 10 August 1994 which declined to grant the applicant registration as a tax agent. The applicant applied to the AAT for review of that decision on 3 November 1994.

The relevant period of time for lodging an application for review is 28 days (s.29(2) of the Act). The applicant therefore was approximately 2 months out of time in lodging his application. The respondent opposed time being extended on the basis that the applicant had not provided an acceptable explanation for the delay and on the basis that the applicant and his application had no merit.

These reasons have been prepared at the request of the respondent pursuant to s.43(2A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 following the delivery of oral reasons for decision at the conclusion of the hearing on 9 December 1994. The proceedings were not recorded and there is no transcript of the extension of time hearing. These reasons therefore have been prepared as a result of notes taken by me during the hearing.

The Facts

The applicant had previously been registered as a tax agent in 1979. Registration was cancelled in August 1990 after the applicant pleaded guilty to 15 counts of misappropriation of clients’ funds from his trust account in an accountancy practice then conducted by him in Melton. The sum misappropriated was approximately $25,000. The applicant said that all of those funds were subsequently repaid.

The applicant explained that he had misappropriated the funds to meet the costs of establishing his accountancy practice in Melton and personal domestic expenses. Prior to commencing his Melton practice, the applicant had been engaged in property development in NSW and had lost heavily.

Initially he was sentenced to twelve months imprisonment (suspended for six months) but on appeal the sentence was reduced having regard to the applicant’s circumstances at the time namely, his obligations as a sole father to two dependent daughters, his apparent history in the Melton community, his charitable work, the fact that he had made restitution, and a number of character referees who gave evidence.

The applicant said that he now sought re-registration as an agent because he had not been able to obtain employment as a result of credit checks made upon him by potential employers and agencies from whom he had sought work. The applicant currently receives unemployment benefit.

At the hearing, the respondent, or at least its counsel, zealously attacked the applicant’s credit and attempted to prove that he was not a “fit and proper person”, within the meaning of s.251BC and s.251JA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

The cross examination of the applicant was directed towards his prior (admitted) convictions and alleged failure to lodge personal tax returns in the early 1980s, his occupation of premises in Collins Street Melbourne held by a corporation for which the applicant was a director, his prior practice as an accountant in Shepparton in rural Victoria and Nelson Bay in NSW, a business undertaking with respect to Messrs Morgan and Taylor and the intervention of the Victorian Mining Warden in 1991, his financial undertakings with Hallmark Credits and the circumstances of a scheme of repayment of certain debts, and his involvement in Supreme Court proceedings with respect to the Australian Society of Certified Practising Accountants.

This cross examination was all the more remarkable because the respondent at no time put the applicant (or the Tribunal) on notice that it intended to raise these matters in the extension of time hearing. Further, the respondent did not lodge any T-documents which might have given some indication to the applicant that he was on notice to deal with these issues, thereby enabling him to attend the extension of time hearing sufficiently prepared to meet the issues that the respondent raised.

The only document filed with the Tribunal, which was forwarded to the applicant, was a document entitled “Grounds of Opposition”. Excluding formal parts, this document said-

“1. The applicant’s explanation for the delay in lodging his application for review is unacceptable.

2. The application for review has no merit, the merits of the substantial application to be properly taken into account when a decision is being made as to whether or not to exercise a discretion to grant an extension of time (Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305 at 310-311).

The respondent reserves its right to add to or vary the above grounds”.

The respondent’s solicitors did however forward a letter to the Tribunal Registrar when it sought to have summonses issued. That letter briefly referred to the issue of whether the applicant was a “fit and proper” person, but it does not appear that a copy of that letter was forwarded to the applicant prior to the hearing.

Despite the persistence of counsel for the respondent introducing the above evidence and the protests of the applicant in having to meet those questions, I reminded the respondent’s counsel that she should be aware of recent decisions of the Federal Court in relation to whether the Tribunal should embark upon a full scale inquiry into the merits of an application when considering whether an extension of time should be granted. It needs to be said that the respondent’s approach to this particular application was remarkable by its vigour, aggression and conduct, altogether inappropriate in the context of administrative review and particularly in its attempt to ambush the applicant.

Findings and Conclusion

The principles to be applied in deciding whether time should be extended to lodge proceedings have been enunciated in Re Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation and Barbara (1987) 6 AAR 300 and Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344. It is noted also that it is no longer necessary that an acceptable explanation for delay must be given as an essential precondition for success in any application for an extension of time (refer Comcare v A’Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85 and Dix v Crimes Compensation Tribunal [1993] 1VLR 297).

I was satisfied that the applicant did demonstrate an acceptable explanation for his delay. The applicant said that he believed that there was a twelve month time limit on the commencement of proceedings to review a decision of the Tax Agents’ Board. He said he first learnt of the 28 day limit in late October or early November 1994 and I note that his proceedings were lodged on 3 November 1994. I was satisfied that the applicant did initially believe that there was a twelve month limit and I was satisfied also that upon him learning of the 28 day limit in late October or early November 1994, as may be inferred by the relevant dates, that he did act promptly to commence the proceedings. The applicant said that following the reconsidered decision being delivered on 10 August 1994 rejecting his application for registration he became disillusioned and “threw in the towel”. It was not until some time later, when the rejection was less upsetting, that he made appeal enquires and learnt of the 28 day limit.

The respondent conceded during the hearing that it did not allege any prejudice to it by the delay and I am satisfied that it did not suffer any.

To the extent that Re Barbara and Hunter Valley Developments referred to taking into account the merits of the substantial application in considering whether an extension of time should be granted, it would appear that this criteria has been modified slightly by the decision of Von Doussa J., in Windshuttle v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1993) ATC 4992 and Repatriation Commission v Tuite [1992] FCA 415; (1992) 37 FCR 571. In Windshuttle, His Honour, at 4999, said in part-

“..... that where the issue is whether leave should be given to extend time it is inappropriate for the Tribunal concerned to embark on a full scale trial of the merits of the underlying question which will be agitated only if time is extended. .... it would not be appropriate on an application to extend time to seek to attack the facts alleged on the ground that the credit of the applicant or that of supporting witnesses should not be accepted. Arguments of that kind are best left for later consideration if and when an extension of time is granted.”

I accordingly indicated to counsel for the respondent that I would not permit her to continue her line of examination and her submissions as to the applicant’s credit because those were matters which should be the subject of review by a properly constituted Tribunal at a hearing and only after the applicant has been given sufficient notice to meet the allegations that are intended to be put against him. Likewise I was not prepared to act upon the respondent’s submissions, amounting as they did to innuendo and inference against the applicant.

The respondent argued that the qualifying criteria as to examination of merit enunciated by Von Doussa J., in Windshuttle was relevant to the extent that His Honour said-

“Only where there is some obvious and easily demonstrated flaw in the applicant’s case would it be appropriate to challenge the factual basis for the asserted claim on an application to extend time”.

I was not satisfied then, nor am I now, that there was no obvious or easily demonstrated flaw in the applicant’s case.

I indicated to both parties that I made no finding of fact with respect to the allegations made by the respondent against the applicant, but did indicate that in the interests of fairness to the applicant he should be entitled to properly present his case and meet the allegations which will obviously again be put to him by the respondent at the substantive hearing.

Nothing should be inferred from these reasons that the Tribunal has dismissed the substantive case of the respondent. Nor should it be inferred that the applicant will ultimately be successful when the Tribunal reviews the respondent’s decision. All that I have sought to do by the oral reasons previously delivered and again by these written reasons is to indicate that I have extended the time for the applicant to lodge his application for review. He will now therefore have the opportunity to present his case at a properly constituted hearing, upon the merits and will not be denied that opportunity, as the respondent’s representative sought to do, by her attempted ambush on 9 December.

The Tribunal decides that time be extended to permit the applicant to lodge these proceedings. The application shall be adjourned to the February Callover and fixed for hearing forthwith.
