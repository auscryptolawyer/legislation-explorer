INCOME TAX - seaman - severance payment – whether sum received was in consequence of retirement from or termination of employment : s 26(d) - whether applicant had been and continued to be employed by the Maritime "Industry"

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 August 1988 Mr B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

1. The applicant is a seaman. He was born in December 1929 and first went to sea in 1944. Between 1970 and 1982, he served on the one ship. From 1970 to 1977 that ship was owned by Bulkships Pty Limited. From 1977 to 1982, it was owned by Broken Hill Proprietary Limited trading as BHP Transport. That company sold the ship in 1982 "off the coast" when it was decommissioned. Because of decommissioning in those circumstances, the applicant became entitled to a lump sum by way of severance pay under clause 37 of the Maritime Industries Seagoing Award. It was agreed at the hearing that if the applicant's services on board the vessel had been terminated prior to the decommissioning, he would not have been entitled to any lump sum payment. The entitlement arose purely and simply as a result of the operation of the relevant clause of the award. That clause is in the following terms -

"37 - SEVERANCE PAY AND REDUNDANCY

(a) An employee whose services are terminated because of the decommissioning and sale off the coast of a vessel (and in the case of an officer employee reasonably suitable alternative employment is not offered to him by the employer) shall be paid one and half weeks' salary at the rate to which he was entitled at the time of termination for each completed year of continuous service and pro rata for completed months thereof on such vessel or in the case of an officer employee with the employer. Severance payments and the provisions of this clause shall be reviewed in the event of other provisions being made with respect to the redundancy of employees in the industry.

(b) Continuous service shall not in the case of an employee being a member of the Seamen's Union or Stewards' Association include any service after 31 August 1977. Leave shall be reserved to the said unions and the employers party to the award in respect to redundancy provisions in the event of members of the said unions being de-registered pursuant to any reductions in quotas because of retrenchments from employment under the award as varied or replaced from time to time."
  1. Because of paragraph (b) of that clause, his entitlement was calculated up until 31 August 1977 when a different scheme affecting seamen came into operation. He was paid a lump sum of $4,077.
  2. He thereupon registered under the "roster" system. This is more correctly known as the "Marine Cooks, Marine Stewards and Seamen's Engagement System Schedule". It takes the form of a schedule to the Maritime Industries Seagoing Award and provides for methods of engagement of seamen on an orderly basis and for some remuneration in the event of their non-engagement. It is under the control of a Superintendent who is an officer of the Australian Department of Transport. The scheme provides for registration on termination of employment by employees who wish to obtain other sea-going employment. The relevant parts of the schedule are in the following terms -

"Requirements regarding availability for employment

(i) Except with the Superintendent's consent to leave of absence each rating registered in Register A shall be immediately available for employment in work of appropriate classification at the port of registration or for allocation to employment at such other port as may be determined by the Superintendent other than when:

(i) employed by a registered employer or, if not so employed, serving on an Australian ship;

(ii) certified medically unfit for employment or in receipt of wages pursuant to Section 132(1) of the Navigation Act 1912?

(iii) on award leave, actual or nominal, as recorded in Register A.

Rating to report to superintendent on termination of employment

(j) When a rating terminates employment on a ship at a specified port he shall thereupon report to the Superintendent or his representative at that port and his name, his port of registration and the date when he will be available for employment will then be recorded in Register A in relation to the appropriate port. If the termination occurs at a non-specified port the rating shall thereupon report to the Superintendent's representative at that port who shall arrange for recording of the relevant information in relation to the appropriate specified port. The date so recorded shall be the date on which the rating shall be available in accordance with subclause (i) hereof unless either:

(i) he elects an earlier date; or

(ii) the Superintendent approves a subsequent date.

A copy of the information so recorded in Register A shall be handed to the rating at the time of reporting and where it is necessary for the rating to "sign off" Articles following termination of employment, the time of reporting shall be the time of signing off Articles. If the rating fails to report or to supply the required information the required details will be recorded by the Superintendent on the basis of information extracted from the Articles of Agreement and the account of wages or failing this as supplied by the employer's representative."

4. Once recorded on Register A, a seaman is entitled to certain rights and is subject to certain obligations. He is entitled to move from port to port only with consent of the Superintendent. There is a quota of seamen allocated for each port. He is obliged to attend for engagement for employment and is subject to deregistration in certain circumstances. It is the function of the superintendent to attempt to obtain a new berth for a person in Register A. Until such a berth is allocated, a person on the Register is entitled to "attendance money". This is provided for in the following terms -

"4 - ATTENDANCE MONEY

(a) A rating registered in Register A not in employment shall be paid $35-06 (except Deckboys who shall receive $18-58) for each day Monday to Friday (including public holidays) on which the rating is qualified to accept employment and makes himself available for engagement for employment by registered employers as required by the Superintendent and is not engaged.

Attendance money shall not be paid

(b) (i) For a day for which the rating receives wages, leave pay (actual or nominal), standby fee or wages or compensation for illness or injury, provided that a standby fee counts in respect of only one day;

(ii) For any day following termination of the rating's last employment if that employment is terminated before the ship first sails from the port following engagement of the said rating at that port, unless the employment is terminated by the rating because of the illness, injury or for other reasonable cause, provided that this paragraph shall not disentitle a rating from payment of attendance money on more than ten days;

(iii) For any day on which the rating does not accept engagement or join a ship as required by the Superindentent or for any subsequent day until the rating joins a ship.

(c) Payment of attendance money will be made by employers through the employers' representative at the port either weekly or on engagement of ratings for employment on the basis of information supplied by the Superintendent."

  1. The rate of payment for attendance money is considerably below the rate of payment for the classification of seaman. The latter rate ranges from $23,993 per year to $30,121 per year, depending on the seniority of the seaman and the type of ship upon which he is serving. After leaving his decommissioned ship, the applicant took a few weeks leave that was due to him and then "went on the roster". Almost immediately, he was found employment on another ship, owned by Lombardo Marine Pty Limited. He served with that company for some time and subsequently on another ship owned by Australian Shipping Commission. About 2 years after payment of the lump sum, he joined a ship owned by his previous employer, Broken Hill Proprietary Limited.
  2. The applicant claimed that the amount of $4,077 was assessable only to the extent of 5% because of the terms of s 26(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. At the relevant time, that subsection was in the following terms -

"26. The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -

(d) five per centum of the capital amount of any allowance, gratuity or compensation where that amount is paid in a lump sum in consequence of retirement from, or the termination of, any office or employment, and whether so paid voluntarily, by agreement or by compulsion of law "

The Commissioner rejected the claim giving as his stated reason that the sum was not received in consequence of retirement from or the termination of any office or employment. This application is brought to review that objection decision.

7. On the hearing, the Commissioner's representative supported the objection decision on the basis that at all relevant times the applicant had been and continued thereafter to be employed by "the industry" and that therefore there had been no termination of employment. This argument was put on the basis that the roster system provided for payment of attendance money while the applicant was not being paid wages by any individual employing company and that consequently at no time was he unemployed. I have considerable difficulty in accepting that argument. Although I was not referred to its constitution or role in the operation of the roster system, the respondent's advocate referred to an entity called Australian Maritime Industries Limited which (it was said) is the legal form, by which the collective of employers and their organisations is represented. I am quite unable to see, however, that this results in that company being the employer of the applicant throughout his whole time in the industry either while he was on board his ship, or while he was on the roster. On board his ship he was paid wages by Bulkships Limited initially, and after the first sale of the vessel by Broken Hill Proprietary Limited. There was no evidence before me that he was paid any attendance money while he was on the roster. However even if he was paid such an amount, it would have been in accordance with paragraph (c) of clause 4 of the Award above, and not because of any employment relationship between him and the employers' collective company.

8. The nature of attendance money is quite different from the nature of wages. The amounts are significantly different and the reasons for payment of the money are quite distinguishable. The payment of attendance money by any person or organisation whether statutory or industrial does not, in my view, bring about a relationship of master and servant or, to use the words of the Act, a relationship of employment. Attendance money is paid because of the operation of an award which has the force of a statute. It is payable by a statutory group formed for that purpose. Wages are paid by a company that engages the services of the employee. An employee would certainly take the view that he was "out of work" if all he was receiving was attendance money. It is fanciful, in my view, to say that, throughout a seaman's working life, he is employed by such a body as the "industry", namely the same body that would pay him attendance money in between engagements.

  1. The payment of the severance pay became due only when the applicant's services were "terminated" in specified circumstances. This would, to my mind, lead one inevitably to the conclusion that because he received the payments, his services were terminated, and that the payment was made in consequence of the termination of those services or in other words, in consequence of the termination of his employment. There is however authority on this matter. Decisions going back to 1977 appear to be to the contrary and appear to have been the basis of the administration of the Act for many years. Income Tax Ruling 246, subsequently confirmed in Ruling 2303, for example, relies upon the correctness of these earlier decisions.
  2. The first of these was Case J33 77 ATC 324. This was a decision of the No 2 Board of Review, comprising Mr A N Donovan (Chairman) and Mr R K Todd (who is now a Deputy President of this Tribunal). In circumstances which seem to me indistinguishable in essence from the circumstances of the present application, the Board held that the receipt of the payment had nothing at all to do with the taxpayer's employment, since it was quite possible for him to continue to be employed on another ship operated by the same employer after the receipt of the amount in question. Uninstructed by authority, I would have thought that such a finding necessarily meant importing into the section the words "in an industry" after the word "employment" in s 26(d). It would, on the face of it, bring into s 26(d) only payments made in consequence of "permanent" retirement from the whole maritime industry. As Stephen J commented in Western Australia v The Commonwealth [1975] HCA 46; (1975) 134 CLR 201 at 251: "To read words into any statute is a strong thing and, in the absence of clear necessity, a wrong thing " I would have thought that such decision was legislative rather than judicial in nature and extended the concept beyond the scope of the ordinary meaning of the words in the section. I would have thought that the fact that the applicant could have obtained other employment within the industry was irrelevant in deciding whether or not his employment with a particular employer had been terminated. Nevertheless, the decision is there and, as I have said, has been acted upon for the last 11 years.

11. It was followed in Case M47 80 ATC 323. This was a decision of the No 1 Board of Review, whose Chairman was Mr H P Stevens (a former Senior Member of this Tribunal). Mr Stevens expressed his reservations about accepting an "industry employment" concept whereby it was of little significance who were the owners of the vessels upon which a person performs services. He repeated his reservations in Case S17 85 ATC 213 at 214. These are reservations which I respectfully support. In my view, it can not be said that "the industry", as such, is the employer at all relevant times, any more than such an amorphous organisation as "the music industry" could be said to be the employer of individual musicians who are engaged to play at various functions through assistance derived from their union and through employers seeking their services through their union. How can "an industry" contract? It is not a legal entity. One may be employed in an industry but hardly by it. Similarly (contrary to the understanding of some) one may be employed on a ship but not by it. Case M47 however, again appears to give weight to the fact that the applicant could have sought re-engagement after signing off. The Board held that the severance payment was not made in consequence of the taxpayer's retirement from, or termination of, employment, but was paid to him only because his employment on the vessel was terminated as the vessel was to be sold off the coast. This seems to me, with respect, to beg the question. Nevertheless, this decision, following an earlier decision, has also been the basis of the administration of the Act for some time.

  1. At first glance Case S17 85 ATC 213 appears to diverge from the stream of authority. A significant difference however between that case and Cases J33 and M47 on the one hand, and the present case on the other hand, is that in Case S17, the taxpayer had achieved the status of permanent employment on a Caltex vessel, long before he came under the industry roster system. He was paid, not just in respect of his service on the vessel sold off the coast, but in respect of his total period since he first joined a Caltex vessel.
  2. In Case U15 87 ATC 167 the taxpayer again was operating outside the roster system. As the fortunate holder of "permanent employment" he did not need the support of the Award obligations in order to justify severance payment. The decision notes, in fact, that the applicant was not entitled to receive his payment pursuant to any statutory or industrial award. In that sense payment by the employer was gratuitous on its part. In any event, Case U15 is more concerned with the application of the recent "eligible termination payment" provisions, rather than with the section 26(d) which preceded it and with which I am presently concerned.
  3. Had it not been for the decisions to which I have referred, I would have set aside the objection decision under review. I concede however, that it is a decision which is apparently supported by authority. I consider also that I should regard myself bound by those decisions. Whatever reservations I may have about the correctness of the cases, I do not consider it good administrative practice for a Senior Member of this Tribunal, (particularly sitting alone) to dissent directly from previous decisions of this Tribunal or of its predecessors. That is a function more properly exercisable by a higher court of authority. For the sake of orderly administration of the Act, there should be uniformity of decision amongst the Members of this Tribunal. If the views taken are not correct, then they may be corrected by higher authority and administrative practices may be changed.
  4. In the circumstances, I feel obliged to affirm the objection decision under review for these reasons.
