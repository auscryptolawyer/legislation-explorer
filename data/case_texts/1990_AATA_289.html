QT90/190 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 289; (1990) 21 ATR 3836; 91 ATC 109 (30 November 1990)

Last Updated: 3 June 2009



INCOME TAX - Assessable income - Taxi business - Cessation of business within twelve months - Recoupment of depreciation upon vehicle and radio included in assessable income - Whether profit on sale of licence to hire assessable income - Whether a sale of a licence to hire or a sale of a licensed vehicle - Whether an amount on disposal of licence to hire assessed under provision other than sec 26AAA(2) - Whether licence to hire is "property" - Decision on objection affirmed.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 secs 26AAA(2), 26AAA(5)(a), 54, 59

State Transport Act (Qld) 1960 sec 17(2)



FC of T v Mullins 81 ATC 4643

Thomas v Sorrell 124 ER 1098

Reg. v Metropolitan Police Commissioner; ex parte Parker [1953] 1 WLR 1150

Ridge v Baldwin [1963] UKHL 2; [1964] AC 40

REASONS FOR DECISION



1. This application proceeded on the following agreed statement of facts:



" 1. Applicant and Spouse purchased Licence to Hire No. 75 in November 1979.



2. The amount of $19,400 paid by Applicant and Spouse was apportioned as



Meter $ 50

Radio $ 350

Goodwill $ 19,000



3. A vehicle was purchased by Applicant and Spouse in November 1979 for $3,649 in a separate transaction to the purchase of the Licence to Hire.



4. Applicant and Spouse sold Licence to Hire No. 75 on 31 July 1980.



5. Gross amount of $27,500 received by Applicant and Spouse on sale was apportioned as



Vehicle $ 3,000

Meter $ 100

Radio $ 400

Goodwill $ 24,000



6. Commission on the sale amounted to $500 apportioned as:



Licence $ 436

Meter $ 2

Radio $ 7

Vehicle $ 55



7. Depreciation under Section 54 of the Income Tax Assessment Act was allowed as follows:



Year ended 30 June 1980

Applicant's half share



Vehicle $ 638.00 $ 319.00

Radio $ 112.00 $ 56.00



Year ended 30 June 1981



Vehicle $ 77.00 $ 36.00

Radio $ 7.00 $ 4.00



8. Following the disposal of the assets, a balancing charge was included in the applicant's assessable income, as required by Section 59 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, as follows



Applicant's half share



Radio $ 12.00 $ 6.00

Vehicle $ 109.00 $ 55.00



9. Two arithmetic errors were made in the assessment.



(a) Purchase price of the licence was calculated as $19,400, not $19,000. This decreased the assessed profit by $400.



Applicant's half share $200



(b) Commission on sale was understated as $426, not $436. This increased the assessed profit by $10.



10. Net profit made on the sale of Cab Licence/Goodwill was not disclosed on the taxpayer's return of income for the year ended 30 June 1981.



11. It is common ground that the sale and purchase were subject to the provisions of the State Transport Act.



12. The Licence to Hire was not physically attached to the vehicle meter."



2. The agreed facts record that the applicant (and his spouse) sold their taxi business within twelve months. The primary issue is whether the sum of $2,282 (calculated after correcting for the arithmetic errors identified in the agreed facts), which represents the half-share of the applicant in the profit on sale of the taxi licence, is assessable income pursuant to sec 26AAA(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), or whether such sum is excluded from sec 26AAA(2) by operation of sec 26AAA(5)(a) of the Act.



3. Sec 26AAA(2) of the Act provides:



" Where -



(a) a taxpayer has purchased property after 21 August 1973 and before the commencement of this section or purchases property after the commencement of this section; and



(b) the taxpayer has, whether before or after the commencement of this section, sold the property or an interest in the property before the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he purchased the property



then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or interest."



Sec 26AAA(5) provides that sub-section (2) does not apply in relation to a sale by the taxpayer of property if -



" (a) the property was included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer and, as a result of the sale, an amount will be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income under a provision of this Act other than this section;



(b) ......



(c) ...... "



4. Mr Finnimore, the tax agent who represented the applicant, submitted that the assets of this taxi busines, viz the vehicle, meter, radio and licence/goodwill, were all necessary for the operation of the enterprise. Thus, he asserted "that the cab without the licence is of no use, and the licence without the cab is of no use". The observations of Mr Finnimore are supported by sec 17(2) of the State Transport Act 1960 (Qld) (as amended) and Regulation 47 to the State Transport Act. Sec 17(2) provides:



"A licence to hire shall not be issued, renewed or transferred in respect of a vehicle unless -



(a) if the vehicle is required to be registered under 'The Main Roads Acts, 1920 to 1959', a certificate of registration or of renewal of registration thereunder in respect of the vehicle is current;



(b) a certificate is produced to the Commissioner which satisfies him that such vehicle complies with the requirements of 'the Inspection of Machinery Acts, 1951 to 1960'; and



(c) such vehicle is constructed and equipped as prescribed,



at the date of the issue, renewal or, as the case may be, transfer of such licence."



Regulation 47 to this Act states -



" (1) A licensee selling or disposing of his licensed vehicle shall, within three (3) days thereafter, give written notice to the Commissioner of the sale or disposal and of the name and address of the purchaser or of the person acquiring it.



(2) A licensee who sells or disposes of a licensed cab shall -



(a) before completing that sale or disposal, remove or cause to be removed any printing, painting, equipment or sign indicating or implying or that may be construed to imply that the vehicle is a cab or is licensed for hire or is used for hire, including registration plates issued in relation thereto, unless the vehicle is sold to another person who holds a licence in respect of that class of vehicle; and



(b) substitute therefor another vehicle within fourteen (14) days or such further period as may be approved by the Commissioner."



5. Mr Finnimore, relying heavily on the decision of the Full Federal Court in FC of T v Mullins 81 ATC 4643, claimed that a taxi licence issued by the Commissioner for Transport becomes attached to, and indivisible from, the other assets of the business, in much the same way as fixtures become part of the land. On that view, he submitted that the "property" that the applicant sold - a licensed vehicle - was one and indivisible and, a balancing charge of $61 arising upon the disposal of the radio and vehicle having been included in the assessable income of the applicant pursuant to sec 59 of the Act, the profit on the disposal of the licence was excluded from the assessable income by operation of sec 26AAA(5)(a).



6. At first blush, Mr Finnimore's reliance on Mullins sems highly persuasive. In that case, the taxpayer and his wife had purchased some eight acres of land on which they carried on a business of pig farming in partnership. They sold the land at a profit within 12 months of acquisition. At the time of acquisition, there were some badly damaged fences on the property, valued at $200, in respect of which an amount of $7 was claimed for depreciation. The taxpayer, having repaired the fencing, its value upon disposal was increased to $400, thus enabling him to claim his share of the balancing charge accruing from the improved value of the fences in his assessable income pursuant to sec 59 of the Act. The primary question was whether this small amount, representing a recoupment of allowable depreciation, could properly be regarded, for the purposes of sec 26AAA(5)(a), as included in assessable income "as a result" of the sale of an estate in land.



Deane J, with whom Lockhart J agreed (McGregor J dissenting), held -



"The question which arises is whether this notional apportionment should be treated as converting, for the purposes of sec 26AAA(5)(a), the one sale of an estate in land on which fences were erected into two sales, namely, a notionally distinct sale of the fences and a sale of the balance of the land and improvements. If it should, the consequence would be that the inclusion, in the taxpayer's assessable income, of the amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation would be seen as the result of the notionally distinct sale of the fences and not as the result of the sale of the estate in the land. The provisions of cl. (a) would not exclude the application of sec. 26AAA(2) to the sale of the estate in the land.



If there had, in truth, been a combined sale of two distinct pieces of property, it would be possible to divide the sale for the purposes of sec 26AAA notwithstanding the fact that such division would involve a notional apportionment of the proceeds of sale. The plain fact of the matter in the present case is, however, that the taxpayer sold but one 'property'. That 'property' was his estate or interest in the land on which were situated improvements and fixed structures (including fences) (see sec. 26AAA(1)(a): 'a reference to property ... includes a reference to an estate or interest in property...'). That was the 'property' which he sold for the purposes of sec. 26AAA(2) of the Act. It is to the 'sale' of that 'property' to which both the introductory words of sec 26AAA(5) and the words of cl. (a) refer. (Note, in this regard, the reference, in sec 26AAA(5)(c)(i), to the 'property' consisting of 'an estate or interest in land on which is situated a dwelling'.) The case is not one where, because of lack of identity between what was purchased and what was sold, the application of sec 26AAA necessarily involves some notional division of either the purchase or the sale. The fact that some notional apportionment may be accepted for the purposes of sec 59 does not alter the fact that there was, for the purposes of sec 26AAA, no other distinct sale by the taxpayer. It is not, in my view, permissible to use the notional apportionment of the proceeds of sale for the purposes of sec 59 as a basis for constructing a hypothetical sub-sale of fences for the purposes of sec 26AAA(5)(a) with the result that the amount included in the assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to sec 59 was so included as a result of a sale of the 'fences' and not as a result of the sale of the estate in the land. In my view, the amount included in the assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to sec 59 was so included as the result of one sale of property for the purposes of sec 26AAA, namely, the sale of the estate or interest in the land on which the fences were erected." (p.4646)



McGregor J concluded -



"I do not agree that as a result of the sale of the estate or land there will be an amount included in the assessable income of the respondent, i.e., other than under sec 26AAA. When para (a) of subsec (5) refers to 'the sale' it is of 'the property' (estate in or land); it was not as a result of that sale that an amount could be included in the assessable income; but by reason of the disposal of fences and the attribution in the Income Tax return of a higher value to them." (p.4652)



7. The judgment of Deane J in Mullins represents an application to sec 26AAA(5)(a) of the maxim quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit, i.e. the fences having been annexed to the land, became part of it so that there was merely one sale of property; viz, the sale of the estate or interest in the land on which the fences were erected. His Honour considered that it would be impermissible to convert what was one sale of property into two distinct sales, namely, a notionally distinct sale of the fences and a sale of the balance of the land and improvements. The majority thus held that the recoupment of depreciation on the fences (included in the assessable income of the taxpayer) was so included "as the result of" one sale of property for the purpose of sec 26AAA, namely, the sale of the estate or interest in the land on which the fences were erected.



8. The facts in the instant case, however, are a far cry from those in Mullins. We are not here concerned with a sale of realty upon which there are improvements and fixed structures, but with the sale of a taxi (meter and radio included) and a licence to hire. The concept that a licence can, somehow become a "fixture" does not lack boldness. Nevertheless, I am satisfied that there is no substance in the submission that we are here dealing with but a single sale of property, viz. a licensed vehicle, which sale, having resulted in the inclusion of a sum of $61 in the assessable income of the applicant pursuant to sec 59 of the Act, somehow activated sec 26AAA(5)(a). Upon the view of the evidence I have formed, the sale on 31 July 1980 was not a sale of a composite piece of "property", but a combined sale of four distinct pieces of "property": the licence to hire, the vehicle, the meter and the radio. The mere fact that "a cab without a licence is of no use, and the licence without the cab is of no use" does not of itself activate the operation of sec 26AAA(5)(a). The evidence records that the licence, meter, radio and vehicle were separate and distinct items of "property", if only because the vehicle was acquired by the applicant subsequent to the purchase of the other items (agreed facts 1 to 3). Further, Mr Finnimore stated that if a taxi operator wished to terminate his business, then his cab company would find a buyer for the licence, the cab, the radio and meter; he accepted that it is "not necessary for a cab to be sold at the same time as the licence, the meter and the radio" (Tr. p 4).



9. I am therefore satisfied that the licence, radio, meter and vehicle are not a single indivisible unit of "property" but are separate and distinct items of "property". It was a mere act of fortune that the applicant was able to dispose of all items on 31 July 1980.



10. It follows that the claim of the applicant to exclude his half share of the profit on the sale of the licence pursuant to sec 26AAA(5)(a) of the Act must fail. I have concluded that the relevant sale of "property" for purposes of sec 26AAA(5)(a) is that of the sale of the licence to hire, rather than the sale of a composite licensed vehicle. The claim of the applicant therefore cannot succeed, if only because no amount has been included in the assessable income of the applicant as a result of the sale of the licence to hire.



11. It occurred to me during the hearing that a more relevant question in this reference was whether a licence to hire is "property" within the ambit of sec 26AAA.



12. Regrettably, Mr Finnimore was unable to assist me on this question and the Commissioner's representative frankly conceded that he came unprepared to deal with this point, although he should have been alerted to this argument by the (accidental) inclusion in the Notice of Objection "that sec 26AAA(2) of the Act does not apply". In the result, the Tribunal did not have the assistance it is entitled to expect.



13. In earlier times it was accepted that a mere licence does not create any estate or interest in the property to which it relates. Thus, in Thomas v Sorrell 124 ER 1098, Vaughan CJ stated:



"A dispensation or licence properly passeth no interest, nor alters or transfers property in any thing, but only makes an action lawful, which without it had been unlawful." (p 1109)



14. The above statement had, until recent times, been accepted as definitive. Thus, in Reg v Metropolitan Police Commissioner; ex parte Parker [1953] 1 WLR 1150, Lord Goddard, speaking of a London cab driver's licence issued under the London Cab Order 1934 stated:



"Indeed, leaving out of account such very exceptional things as irrevocable licences granted under seal and possibly licences coupled with an interest, the very fact that a licence is granted to a person would seem to imply that the person granting the licence can also revoke it. The licence is nothing but a permission, and if one gives a man permission to do something it is natural that the person who gives the permission will be able to withdraw the permission." (p 1154)



15. In time, this view began to be rejected. Its correctness was questioned by the House of Lords in Ridge v Baldwin [1963] UKHL 2; [1964] AC 40 and by the Australian High Court in Banks v Transport Regulation Board (Vic) [1968] HCA 23; (1969-1970) 119 CLR 222. In the latter case, the High Court was asked to consider whether a commercial transport licensing board was empowered by statute to revoke taxi licences granted by it if the conditions attached to such licences were not complied with. The Transport Regulation Act (Vic) required the Board to submit any decision to the Governor in Council, and provided that no such decision should have any force or effect until reviewed by the Governor in Council and that, in reviewing any decision, the Governor in Council might by order, within six months of the Board's giving a decision, approve or disapprove the decision or make any determination in the matter which the Board might have made. The Board was required to give effect to every such order. In dealing with the question whether a commercial taxi-cab licence issued under the Act was "property", Barwick CJ stated -



"I would find it difficult in any case to reconcile the decision of the Court of Appeal in this case with the decision of the House of Lords in Ridge v Baldwin ... In particular, I do not find the description of the licence which found favour with the Lord Chief Justice appropriate to a statutory licence to which a fit and proper person has a right and which relates to such an occupation as that of a cab driver. I do not think such a licence can be equated to the mere grant of a permission by a private person in respect of his own property. But even if Lord Goddard's explanation of the nature of the cab driver's licence in that case should be accepted as correct for the purposes of that case, it does not, in my opinion, touch the question whether the licence itself was or was not property within the meaning of a statute defining the cases in which an appeal may be brought.



Pollock B in Smelting Co of Australia v Commissioners of Inland Revenue [1896] 2 QB 179, 185 evidently regarded the definitions in Thomas v Sorrell and in cases which relied upon them as "depending 'upon technical rules of law which in no way assist one in coming to a conclusion as to the meaning of the word 'property'" in a statute. In the case before his Lordship, the question was whether the sale of a sole licence to exercise a patent was an agreement for the sale of any interest in property within the meaning of a Stamp Act. His Lordship, observing upon the width of the words of the statute, said that they included 'property of whatsoever kind'. He said 'that looking at the object and scope of this statute the word 'property' does include this licence'. That decision was affirmed on appeal: see same case [1897] 1 QB 175. So it seems to me that even if it be right (though I think it is not) to extend Vaughan CJ's statements in Thomas v Sorrell to the case of a licence granted under statute to ply a trade or to operate a vehicle upon a public highway, they cannot be definitive when the question is whether or no such a licence is property within the meaning of a section such as s35(1)(a)(2) of the Judiciary Act. ... I find in the terms of such a provision no warrant for giving to the word 'property' any narrow or technical meaning. In my opinion, it includes, to use Pollock B's expression, 'property of whatsoever kind'. In my opinion, the licence granted under Pt II, revocable only in the stated circumstances and transferable to a fit and proper person, is property within the meaning of s 35(1)(a)(2) of the Judiciary Act." (p 231-232)



16. This view of the law has since been adopted by Kirby P in Ackroyd v Whitehouse [1985] 2 NSWLR 239 at p 245.



17. I am therefore satisfied, albeit without the assistance of the parties, that in the present state of authority a licence to hire issued by the Commissioner for Transport (accompanied by the issue of a plate for attachment to the vehicle: Regulation 88 of the State Transport Act Regulations 1987) is an item of property capable of being purchased and sold within twelve months. It follows that the profit on the sale of the licence to hire (and any associated goodwill) is assessable pursuant to sec 26AAA(2) of the Act.



18. Any similarity in this case to persons, living or dead, is purely accidental, as is any resemblance - however remote - between the law and the arguments submitted by the parties.



19. For the above reasons the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection is affirmed.
