ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - assessable income - additional tax assessed on sick leave lump sum payments - validity of retrospective legislation

Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act s- 51(xxxix)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.26(d), Pt III, Div 2 sub-division AA

The King v Kidman and Others 20 CLP. 425

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 June 1987 Mr B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. The applicant was employed by a municipal council of a Sydney suburb. On 3 May 1984 he decided to leave that employment in order to take up another position. Because of his financial position at the time, he elected to take a lump sum of $3,155 representing his then unused sick leave. He believed that tax would be payable on 5% of that sum, pursuant to the then existent section 26(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). In fact, he gave evidence that he had worked out exactly how much he was to receive. Appropriate deductions by his employer confirmed his belief.
  2. Some time after 30 June 1984 he lodged his income tax return and received an assessment increasing his assessable income by $1,454 representing a calculated proportion of this termination payment. The calculation was made in accordance with the new Part III Division 2, sub-division AA, which had been introduced into the Act after his employment was terminated.
  3. Being unable to understand why an additional amount of tax had been levied, he made enquiries of his former employer. A letter from the council confirmed that the tax instalments deducted from his termination payment were correct at the time that they were made. The letter went on to say that some 3 months after he had left, namely on 7 August 1984, the council had received advice from the Australian Taxation Office relating to legislative changes concerning taxation on lump sum termination payments. The council informed the applicant that the effect of these changes was to introduce a tax on sick leave lump sum payments retrospective to 1 July 1983. The council confirmed its advice to him that as tax was not deducted from his sick leave payment it would appear that he was liable to pay tax on a proportion of that amount by way of assessment following lodgement of his 1983/84 tax return.
  4. The applicant then objected to the assessment. The only ground of objection raised both then, and now before this Tribunal, is the validity of the amending legislation which may have a retrospective effect in his case.
  5. In his objection he said:

"I hereby formally object to this assessment and in particular that part which relates to tax due on my sick leave termination pay from [employer] council,

This tax, imposed by retrospective legislation, is both unfair and unjust. It may be legal according to the appropriate legislation, but I am prepared to defend its validity in my case, in court."

  1. As a result of the objection, the amount to be included in the applicant's taxable income was recalculated and a reduction was made from $1,454 to $1,343. Otherwise, the objection was disallowed. The applicant now seeks a review by this Tribunal of that objection decision.
  2. A statement was issued by the Government on 19 May 1983,

giving a general outline of the proposed legislation. According to the Minister's second reading speech, subsequent Government statements made on 30 May and 7 August 1983 altering the original proposals were to clarify details. Notwithstanding these statements, legislation giving effect to the proposals was not introduced until June 1984.

8. The sub-division above referred to was inserted by Act No. 47 of 1984 which received the Royal Assent on 25 June 1984. The effect of the new sub-division is to tax differently lump sum termination payments made in respect of service prior to 1 July 1983 from those made in respect of service after that date. In the applicant's case, as he was no longer in the employ of the party paying the termination payment when the legislation became effective, he became liable thereunder to pay an appropriate amount by way of an additional assessment. With some justification, he regards this as retrospective taxation.

9, I was referred to the Minister's second reading speech on the introduction of the legislation. In the course of that speech the Minister said:

"I must emphasise that the measures contained in this Bill involve no element of retrospectivity. There is no diminution of any benefit relating to employment before 1 July 1983. Lump sums paid after 30 June 1983 will continue to attract the previous tax rules to the extent that they relate to service before the change-over date.

Of course, no one is happy to pay more tax. The expected effect on revenue of the tax changes demonstrates the moderation, and absence of retrospectivity, of the government's proposals. The additional revenue collected from the taxation of lump sums is estimated to be $70m in 1984/85. This represents some $20m collected on assessment in respect of lump sums received in 1983/84 and collection of some $50m on a PAYE basis in respect of lump sums received in 1984/85."

10. It is not my function to analyse the meaning of second reading speeches by Ministers, nor is it my function to comment on the justice of the proposals introduced by them. However, as the terms of the objection squarely raise the issue, I should state what I regard to be obvious on the face of the above statements that retrospectivity used in the way the Minister has used it has been given a Humpty Dumpty meaning. He was making a speech introducing a Bill to become law which would attract tax for the first time on transactions that took place 12 months previously. This is acknowledged by his statement that some $20m was to be expected in this way. The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary gives more generally recognised meanings to the word 'retrospective'. The primary meaning attributed to the word is "directed to, contemplative of, past time". The secondary meaning in relation to statutes is given as "operative with regard to past time". On any reading of the legislation it must be acknowledged that it has an operation in respect to acts done prior to its coming into existence. It is therefore retrospective in operation. To say otherwise is, in my respectful view, an idiosyncratic use of language.

  1. It might be said that the applicant, an assistant rates clerk, should have been more assiduous in his reading of Government statements. Had he read the statement on 19 May 1983 he might have been aware of the then existing intention of the Government. He might have ordered his affairs on the assumption that a Government statement is or will shortly be the law. Had he done so he would have fallen into error because that statement was amended on 30 May (some 11 days later) and again on 7 August 1983.
  2. He could have assumed that Government statements are always a sure forerunner of Government legislation, notwithstanding the fact that the Government did not have control of both Houses of Parliament. He could have assumed that the government would not have abandoned or modified its proposals after the extensive consultation that was bound to take place after the making of such a statement. He could, like so many who confuse Ministerial statements with the law, assume that the Government would not, as it has in the past, completely forego proposals after assessing the effect of representations.
  3. He could have insisted that his paymaster deduct appropriate sums on his retirement from his position in May 1984, notwithstanding the fact that there was no legislative basis for deduction of these amounts. The paymaster similarly could have made appropriate assumptions instead of waiting until he received advice from the Australian Tax Office, some 2 months after the new sub-division became law, and some 3 months after the applicant had ceased to be employed by the council.
  4. Whenever it became available to the public, the applicant, despite his lack of legal qualifications, could have read the terms of the new sub-division contained in section 27 of the Act and occupying some 20 pages of the statute book. He could have made enquiries as to the passage of the legislation and the giving of the Royal Assent. The legislation was expressed to take effect upon the happening of that latter event. It is not for me to comment whether his failure to anticipate the Government's legislation was unreasonable on his part, or whether the failure on the part of the Government to introduce its legislation until some 12 months after the preliminary announcement had been made, was unreasonable on its part. I can only take the law as I find it.
  5. For that matter, the respondent is similarly bound. My review of the respondent's decision is to establish whether it was correct (if governed by law) or preferable (if governed by discretion). There is no discretion apparent on the face of the legislation. I am restricted therefore in what I may do.
  6. The applicant, in his objection, said that he was prepared to "defend" the validity of the retrospective legislation. I take that to mean that he was prepared to challenge it. There is no other basis upon which the objection is put forward. There is no dispute as to the calculation of appropriate amounts and to the liability for payment of those amounts if the legislation is to be regarded as valid.
  7. In my view, there can be no doubt that the Commonwealth does have power to legislate retrospectively. The power of Parliament to make laws on any of the matters reserved to it by the Constitution is plenary and includes the power within the limits of the Constitution to make ex post facto laws. Furthermore, the power conferred by placitum 51(xxxix)" of the Constitution to make laws with respect to matters incidental to the execution of any of the powers therein mentioned, is as plenary as any other of the powers to make laws. The extent of these powers was settled quite soon after Federation by the High Court of Australia in The King v Kidman and Others [1915] HCA 58; 20 CLR 425.
  8. At page 451 Higgins J. said:

"We have not been referred to any words in the Constitution which point to any limitation of the plenary powers of the Federal Parliament so long as the Parliament keeps within the ambit of the subjects of legislation specifically assigned to it. The British Parliament, admittedly, has power to make its laws retroactive; and I know of no instance in which a Legislature created by the British Parliament has been held to have overstepped its powers by making legislation retroactive. There are plenty of passages that can be cited showing the inexpediency, and the injustice, in most cases, of legislating for the past, of interfering with vested rights, and of making acts unlawful which were lawful when done; but these passages do not raise any doubt as to the power of the Legislature to pass retroactive legislation if it see fit."

19. At page 453 His Honour went on to say:

"It is clear that pl. xxxix. of sec.51 was not meant to limit, it was meant to increase, the powers of Parliament to make laws; and there is not one word, from first to last, to indicate an intention to withhold from the Federal Parliament the same absolute discretion as the British Parliament itself has, with regard to past events as well as present and future - provided that the Federal Parliament confine itself to the specified subjects and matters indicidental to the execution of the legislative executive and judicial powers.

20. The power to legislate with retrospective effect has, in my view, been validly exercised in the legislation to which I have referred. Whatever I feel about the abstract justice of the result, I have no alternative but to affirm the objection decision under review.
