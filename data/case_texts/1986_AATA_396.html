Last Updated: 5 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Taxation - Company recoupment tax - assessments against vendors - whether vendors sold at all - whether vendors held shares capable of controlling more than 90% of voting power whether vendors "bare trustees" - burden of proof - failure by Commissioner and taxpayers to call available witnesses – Taxation - (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982, s 3, 5; Income Tax Assessment Act, s 190.

Yenidge Tobacco Co Ltd 1916 2 Ch 426

United Dominions Corp Ltd v Brian Pty Ltd [1985] HCA 49; (1985) 59 ALJR 676

Petelin v Cullin [1975] HCA 24; (1975) 132 CLR 355

Cuninghame v City of Glasgow Bank (1878) 4 AC 607

Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1958) 101 CLR 298

Re: NOS. TUCT 53/1985, TUCT 52/1985

And: Commissioner of Taxation

AAT Decision No 3076

MR H P STEVENS and MR P M ROACH, SENIOR MEMBERS

5 December 1986

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Applicants

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr H P Stevens (Senior Member)

These applications concern assessments raised pursuant to the provisions of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 upon two shareholders Aco and Bco in a company formerly known as Zco.

2. Aco and Bco are the family companies of individuals A and B who with another person X were associated in selling domestic equipment per medium of another company Salesco. Later A, B, X and a fourth individual Y (a solicitor) came to be associated in property dealings and the assessments in question arise out of these latter activities. Such assessments are vendors recoupment tax assessments issued 6 June 1984 and follow earlier assessments issued to Zco on 2 September 1983 to which objection was taken but in respect of which no "appeal" action was taken upon the disallowance of those objections on 19 December 1983.

3. Section 8(1) provides that "where at any time a vendors taxable amount exists in relation to a person, the person is liable to pay the tax imposed on that amount by the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax - Vendors) Act 1982" whilst in terms of section 3(1) a vendors taxable amount "means an amount that is a primary taxable amount ... in relation to the person". Section 5(1) sets out that where certain criteria are satisfied "a primary taxable amount ... shall be taken to exist ... in relation to that person". For present purposes the relevant criteria are:

"Where -

(a) under a scheme entered into ... on or after 1 January 1972 and before 4 December 1980, some or all of the shares in a company were sold;

(c) immediately before the first sale time, the person or persons who sold the shares referred to in paragraph (a) was or were, by virtue of rights attaching to the shares, capable of controlling more than 90% of the voting power in the company;

(d) the total consideration paid or given in respect of the sale or sales under the scheme of the shares referred to in paragraph (a) exceeds an amount ascertained in accordance with "a formula"

whilst in terms of section 3(1) the word scheme "means any agreement, arrangement, transaction, understanding or scheme, whether formal or informal, whether express or implied and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings."

4. Having regard to the above provisions it was the representative of the applicants' view that the assessments were not "valid" in that

(a) Aco and Bco did not beneficially own and genuinely control more than 90% of the shares in Zco;

(b) there had not been a genuine sale of the shares in Zco; and

(c) the applicants did not receive the proceeds of the sale of those shares.

As the requirements of the Act were cumulative it was only necessary for him to show any one of the above situations existed.

5. This is an unusual case in that it was the representative's basic contention that A, B, X and Y (or their companies) were involved in a four-way partnership with Zco being the vehicle through which the partnership operated - each individual or their company having a quarter interest in Zco which entity at all relevant times however had only two (2) issued shares. It was also basic that A and B had left all the "book work" to X and Y and that in signing certain documents they had merely acted as instructed by X and Y without knowing what they were doing. Although these were the basic contentions, the Tribunal has not had. the advantage of evidence from X and Y nor has it had before it any of the contemporaneous records such as cheque butts, bank statements, cash books, solicitor's files and the like.

6. With this background, it is necessary to turn to the facts as they emerge from the evidence which was placed before the Tribunal.

7. The property dealings were said to have commenced when B "found" a property Blackacre and "bought" it for $20,000 expecting initially a three way split (A, B and X) - Y later "cutting" himself in. However, B did not in fact acquire Blackacre - rather it was acquired by a shelf company L incorporated on 20 June 1979. The first minute appearing in its records is one of 20 June 1979 allotting two (2) $1 shares to the subscribers D and E and appointing those individuals its first directors. L changed its name to Zco by an extraordinary general meeting of members on 20 June 1979 but no minutes of this meeting appear in the company's records - notice of change of name signed by Y. There does appear in the records stamped share transfers dated 20 January 1979 signed by D and E with the transferees shown as Aco and Bco but no signatures for the transferees. There also appears signed but undated notices of resignation as directors of D and E and copy (unsigned but dated 20 June 1979) notice of change of directors etc showing the resignation of D and E, the appointment of Y as secretary and of A, 3, X and Y as directors. All these matters (share transfers etc) would (or should) have been dealt with in the missing minute. The next minute in the records is an unsigned one relating to another extraordinary general meeting of 21 June 1979 with A, B, X and Y shown as being "present". The business discussed thereat is recorded as relating to two properties - Blackacre and Whiteacre - with it being resolved inter alia, to authorise the directors to enter into a contract to acquire Blackacre for $20,000 and to affix the company's seal to that contract. The actual contract of purchase was not sighted. A further extraordinary general meeting of 1 September 1979 (all "present") was held to discuss the granting of a mortgage over Blackacre in the sum of $45,000 to "assist in completion of purchase and renovations" and it was so resolved.

8. Blackacre was transferred to Zco on 4 September 1979 and, after renovation, was said to have been sold for $125,000 yielding a profit of $80-85,000. However, whilst it was in fact transferred to an arms length third party for $125,000 on 25 January 1980, it was not so transferred by Zco for Zco had on 16 January 1980 transferred it to another company S (extent of interests therein of A, B, X or Y uncertain) for $75,000. Accordingly, Zco was not the vehicle A and B thought it had been in relation to Blackacre whilst its profit would have been $50,000 less than they thought. A and B were said to have received $13,000 each which they considered represented one quarter of the profit in Blackacre (balance retained for tax). However such amounts were apparently never returned as income by Aco and Bco whilst the source of these amounts is unknown. There is no evidence of any dividend resolutions and perhaps they were regarded as "loan account" transactions. It is also possible that the $13,000 each did in fact come from Zco (also that they did not have that source) and that they were the only ones to receive amounts from that source. Without the bank records etc for the "group" and/or the files of Y (who acted and was paid fees for so acting, as solicitor in relation to the transactions) one cannot be certain that this was so but one cannot be satisfied it was not the position. All in all the available contempraneous documentation (property transfers) does not support the assertion that Zco was the "four way split" vehicle for the "partnership" in respect of Blackacre.

9. Just as the transaction concerning Blackacre was not passed in its entirety through Zco so the second property Whiteacre the subject of the minute of 21 June 1979 was also not so dealt with. This property was said to have been acquired for $420,000 and sold for $680,000 - a profit of $260,000 in respect of which A and B said they had received nothing. Although Whiteacre consisted of a single structure, it had been built on three separate parcels of land (57-61, 63-69 and 71-75) and the transfers show the purchase considerations as $109,200, $184,800 and $126,000 respectively with the respective sales considerations of $130,000, $400,000 and $150,000 (supporting a profit of $260,000). However, the whole three parcels were not dealt with through Zco and this was apparently always intended for the minute of 21 June 1979 concerned only 63-69 resolving to authorise the directors to affix the company's seal to a Deed of Trust of X as trustee for the company for the acquisition of 63-69 for $184,000. Another minute of 31 August 1979 (A, B, X and Y "present") authorised the directors to mortgage 63-69 for $240,000 to assist in the purchase of 63-69 and renovation of Blackacre.

10. X in fact acquired the three parcels in his own name on 7 September 1979 for $420,000 with X transferring 57-61 on the same day for $1 to a company in which Y was interested and transferring 63-69 also on the same day to Zco for $1. The remaining parcel 71-75 was transferred by X on 7 November 1979 to a company in which he was interested. The three parcels (upon sale for the considerations set out above) were the subject of separate transfer documents each dated 18 July 1980 from the parties to which X had transferred the individual parcels to the purchaser of the three parcels. The account or accounts into which the proceeds of the sales of the parcels were deposited and how such were subsequently dealt with is unknown. The above dealings are consistent (insofar as Zco were concerned) with the terms of the minute of 21 June 1979 and does not indicate that Zco was to have everything channelled and split four ways through it. Perhaps X and Y may have misled A and B and not carried out "instructions" for a four way structured entity but the official documentation indicates other than such an entity and the Tribunal cannot rectify official documents - particularly not when A and B are somewhat imprecise in their evidence and X and Y are not called.

11. The only documentation possibly the contrary is a profit and loss statement for period 20 June 1979 to 29 July 1980 for Zco prepared by an accountant from information given him by somebody - source unknown - for the purpose of the exercise giving rise to the assessments in question. Zco was the target company and prior to settlement its assets had to be sold and its liabilities assigned so that the Balance Sheet as at date of settlement would be in the following form:

Provision for Income Tax

Represented by

A copy of the Target company's Balance Sheet and Profit & Loss Account made up to the date of settlement was required to be presented at the settlement meeting and the accountant from the information constructed an account showing an "operating profit and accumulated profits July 29, 1980" of $131,481 and a Balance Sheet showing:

Since the accountant had no information relating to the source of the various items, the calculations are not really very helpful. In particular, 57-61 and 71-75 are brought in (but "profit" to Zco only) whilst the renovation costs of Blackacre are shown as $48,585 (far in excess of what A and B thought). Nevertheless, they represent the only contrary "documentation" in respect of Whiteacre. They on the other hand, support the treatment of Blackacre except in relation to possibly the renovation costs unless the $13,000 each to A and B (para 8) has been included therein as an "expense" in which case there is no inconsistency -and the sale price thereof.

13. The applicants' representative conceded that the burden of proof lay upon him and it is fair to say it is difficult to be satisfied (on the balance of probabilities) when the documentation does not support the assertions of A and B (allowing for their reliance on X and Y) and X and Y are not called. Admittedly they could have been called by the Commissioner and it would be possibly "fatal" not to do so if the evidentiary onus had passed to him but, if he does not do so, it cannot be said the failure to call them allows an adverse inference to be drawn. The applicants' representative quite deliberately decided not to call X and Y and any adverse inference falls on the applicants. Not that I think there is any need in this case to rely on any such inference and I refrain from so doing. It is sufficient to find that I am not satisfied that Zco was the partnership vehicle for all four as distinct from A and B only. It follows I reject the representative's contention as set out in item (a) para 4.

14. Turning to item (b) para 4, the known factual situation is that someone other than A or B contacted a firm and a "Company Acquisition" sheet was filled out. The target company named therein was Zco (registered office Y's place of business) with the accountant's firm (para 11) named both as "contact" and "accountants". Attachments to this sheet named A, B, X and Y as directors (Y also as Secretary and Public Officer), listed the shareholders as Aco and Bco and indicated there were "Fees" of $10, 519. Page 2 of the sheet stated inter alia that prior to settlement:

  • The Directors of the Target Company must open a branch Register of Members in Darwin. (Draft Minutes will be forwarded to the Target Company).
  • The shareholders of the Target Company must request in writing that their shares be transferred to the Darwin branch register. (Draft requests will be forwarded to the Target Company).
  • The Secretary of the Target Company must open a Sydney copy of the Darwin branch register and enter on it the transmittal of the shares from the company's principal register.
  • The above information should be advised to the offices of Baker Hughes Montgomery & Co to allow the despatch of information to Darwin.
  • A draft Sale Agreement will be forwarded to the Target Company shareholders. Any amendments should be agreed upon well before settlement. This Agreement will be executed in Darwin under Powers of Attorney.
  • Powers of Attorney must be executed by all shareholders of the Target Company and returned to the offices of Baker Hughes Montgomery & Co, to be forwarded to Darwin along with the agreed upon Sale Agreement.
  • The Directors of the Target Company must produce at the settlement meeting, all matters itemised on the Fourth Schedule.
  • If it is required that the company change its name, this should be done prior to settlement and the necessary Form 7 (Special Resolution) lodged with the Corporate Affairs Commission.

whilst the Fourth Schedule indicated there were required to be presented at the settlement meeting:

8. Form 48 showing opening of Branch Registeer.

The sheet gave the "Settlement - Date and Time" as Friday 11.00 am and name of Bank as Bank of New Zealand.

15. The date the above contract etc was made is unknown but on 29 July 1980 an account in the name of Zco was opened at the named hank pursuant to a meeting of directors of same date - no minute is available whilst one of the signatures on the form ie that of A is not genuine. At some time prior to 1 August 1980 (a Friday) A and B were requested by X to attend a meeting on that date and to bring with them their companies' seals. As requested, they attended at a city high rise building and found X and Y already there together with other persons unknown to A and B. In due course, they were taken to another office where they signed a number of documents. They did not read the documents merely signing and sealing where instructed to do so. When this was done, they left, leaving X and Y still there. A and B deposed they arrived about 10-10.30 am and left about 30 minutes later and that this was the only meeting attended by them that day.

16. Exactly what documents A and B actually signed at this meeting is somewhat uncertain for they professed little knowledge of it or its "purpose". Insofar as its "purpose" is concerned, I reject their claim that they thought it related to another property transaction. There is no doubt they signed the following:

(a) requests for removal of Aco's and Bco's shares in Zco to a Darwin Branch Register (countersigned by Y as secretary although he, not the secretary of Aco or Bco);

(b) powers of attorney to a person in the Northern Territory to execute Agreement for Sale as annexed - also signed by Y as director although not a director of Aco or Bco;

(c) notices of resignation as Directors of Zco (X and Y also resigned as directors and Y as secretary and public officer).

There are no minutes of Aco and Bco dealing with these matters and authorising the affixing of the companies' seals. In relation to (b), it is doubtful whether the agreements referring to sale of the shares for $60,811 each were actually annexed although as para 4 indicates a draft sale agreement was to be forwarded to the shareholders and any amendments "agreed upon well before settlement".

17. Two other "meetings" took place on 1 August 1980 - both of Zco. The minutes of the first held at Y's address at 9.00 am indicated A, B, X and Y as "present" and that it was resolved to open a branch registry at Darwin and that following the tabling of share transmittal requests from Aco and Bco it was resolved that they be approved and following opening of the branch register, the branch registrar be informed and requested to enter them on the branch register. The minutes of the second held at Y's address at 10.30 am again shows all four "present" and records inter alia, the acceptance of the various resignations referred to above and the transfer of the shares from Aco and Bco to two other companies - the attorney having executed an agreement of 1 August 1980 on behalf of both Aco, Bco and each of the "purchasers". That person also executed share transfers on behalf of both transferors and transferees on the same date consideration being sum of $60,811 paid to me by transferees. Without the banking records, it is not known how the overall transaction was effected but it is clear $121,622 would not be "paid" out without the shareholder's loans (para 11) being "repaid". Probably a "round robin" took place since clause 4(1)(a)(iii) of the sale agreement required there to be produced at the settlement "a bank cheque for the sum (representing all of the net assets of the Company as set out in the Accounts) naming the Company as the payee". Without these records, it is also impossible to know where the "proceeds" of the sale ended up although it seems as if another company in which A, B, X and Y were interested may have "received" them. Perhaps this company had earlier been the recipient in relation to the sale of three parcels constituting Whiteacre (para 9).

18. As indicated in para 15, A and B denied any knowledge of the above two meetings of Zco, the minutes of which were signed by Y as Chairman. These minutes give all the appearance of being "draft minutes" forwarded as foreshadowed in para 14 with names etc being typed in the blank spots - sent either to the accountants or to Y's address as the registered office of Zco.

19. The representative's basic argument in respect of this requirement (b) was that it was a paper sale not intended by the applicants to have any real effect ie it was a sham in the true sense. He said the evidence taken cumulatively, support a description of what took place on 1 August 1980 as non-events of no legal effect and not intended to have legal effect. There had been a scenario drawn up, a scheme set up by a promoter with A and B just signing whatever they were asked to sign without knowing what was to happen.

20. I have no doubt there had been a scheme set up by a promoter and a scenario drawn up but this does not mean of itself that a sham was involved. The test as set out in Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786 per Diplock J. at p 802 is stated as follows:-

"I apprehend that, if it has any meaning in law, it means acts done or documents executed by the parties to the 'sham' which are intended by them to give to third parties or to the Court the appearance of creating between the parties legal rights and obligations different from the actual legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties intended to create. But one thing, I think, is clear in legal principle, morality and the authorities ... that for acts or documents to be 'sham', with whatever legal consequences flow from this, all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating."

In the present scheme and/or scenario, it was an essential element that the existing shareholders in the target company should relinquish their interest therein to another set of shareholders absolutely, entirely and irrevocably. It was not a matter of just creating an appearance that they had relinquished their interests whilst behind that cloak "operating" as usual. It was the intention of all parties that this should take place and I cannot see that a "sham" was involved.

21. It may be that due to defects in procedure, it might be said that certain steps would not "hold up" in law but that is a different matter to the existence of a "sham". The representative appeared to appreciate this for towards the end of his address he said:

"I have thought again about this question of a sham and ... I am going to modify my submissions ... the word "sham" is quite inapposite and the real submission I would like to substitute for that is that the sale just did not happen ..."

He then submitted having regard to

i) A and B's non attendance at the 9.00 and 10.30 meetings at Y's office - the minutes thereof being false minutes of meetings that didn't happen;

ii) lack of authority for affixing the seals of Aco and Bco; and

iii) Y signing as director and secretary when not holding close positions - so false documents of no legal effect

the attorney was not "authorised to sign the sale "documents" and consequently there was no sale as required by s 5(1)(a). He conceded that, if a purchaser acted on the basis of the attorney's ostensible authority, Aco and Bco would be estopped as against the purchaser, but submitted it did not follow there was a sale within the meaning of the TUCT Act. There must be a sale in fact not just a sale by estoppel - estoppel not operating in relation to third parties.

22. The terms of s.5(1)(a) have not been the subject of judicial scrutiny except that in March & Anor v FC of T 85 ATC 4345 Ryan J. pointed out, when dealing with a submission "that the sales bore no resemblance to the type of case against which the Act is directed", at p 4363:-

"In my view this approach to the interpretation of the Act cannot be supported. The criteria which must be satisfied before a primary taxable amount exists are set out in precise and elaborate detail in the Act, and the question for determination must be whether these criteria are fulfilled in the circumstances of a particular case. It can only lead to error to start from an assumption as to the scope and purpose of the Act and to deny its applicability though the statutory criteria are fulfilled. The only sure guide to the intention of Parliament in enacting the legislation is the language which it has used to express that intention. It is required by s 15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 that in the interpretation of a provision of an Act a construction that would promote the purpose or object underlying the Act is to be preferred to a construction that would not promote that purpose or object. But that provides no warrant for reading into the language of a provision limitations which are not to be found in it, or for refusing to give effect to the ordinary meaning of a provision by reason of some assumption as to the purpose or object underlying the Act."

Bearing these comments in mind, what constitutes a sale within the meaning of the phrase "under a scheme entered into ... some or all of the shares in a company were sold?"

23. It is a basic concept of statutory construction that words cannot be looked at simply in isolation but must be considered taking into account the context in which they appear. Here it has to be a sale "under a scheme" and the term "scheme" is very widely defined in s 3(1). It follows, I think, that the term "sold" takes its meaning from this context and is apt to include transactions that would not constitute a sale if one were considering situations under, for example, The Sale of Goods Act. Here what was done was part of the scheme to transfer the interest of the existing shareholders in Zco to new shareholders for a nominated consideration and, in my view, it can be properly said that shares "were sold" under that scheme.

24. This leaves for consideration item (c) as per para 4 but the evidence does not enable one to find that the applicants did not receive the proceeds of sale of the shares. As previously stated, no banking records were adduced and it is not known how the proceeds of sale of Blackacre and Whiteacre were dealt with other than that in some way or another they found their way into another entity in which the four individuals were interested. It may be correct that the applicants did not physically receive the sales considerations in that there was a round robin and a series of book entries but that does not mean they did not receive the benefit thereof - albeit by way of a ledger account in the books of that other entity. In these circumstances, I am unable to find that the applicants did not receive the proceeds of sale of the shares.

25. It follows that I would for the above reasons affirm the decisions of the Commissioner.

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr. P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In September 1983 A and B, two businessmen who were respectively the principal officers of Aco and Bco - two proprietary companies with limited liability - received notice that the Commissioner was moving towards raising recoupment tax assessments against Aco and Bco as "vendor shareholders" in a company formerly known as Zco. They received copies of a notice of assessment against Zco (under its new name) fixing its taxable income at $131,481 with primary tax liability of $60,481.26 and additional tax for late return of $30,240.23 (a total of $90,721.49); and of an assessment of Division 7 tax in the sum of $10,649.50. Although the new name of the target company was unknown to them, A and B recognised it by its former name, Zco, as a company with which they had been associated in the course of joint business activities with associates X (a businessman) and Y (a solicitor-cum-businessman).

2. They consulted a Mr. Peter Clyne. He investigated the background circumstances and provided a lengthy Memorandum of Advice which in due course came to be incorporated in the notices of objection before the Board. The Memorandum dealt with a proposal for possible settlement on the basis that A, B, X and Y would contribute in equal shares to satisfy in full the claims of the Commissioner. It was stated that X and Y had already agreed to so contribute); and the possibility was canvassed of Aco and Bco successfully defending the proposed assessments on the bases (inter alia) that they never had been shareholders in Zco and, if they had, that they had not sold either at all or in circumstances which would give rise to liability. The latter contentions were founded in criticisms indicating that documents purporting to record the transfer of shares in Zco to and from Aco and Bco and of meetings relating thereto were illusory and that the transactions they referred to had not occurred or not been legally effective.

3. The Commissioner, being unpersuaded by the Memorandum of Advice, proceeded in due course to raise assessments against Aco and Bco in identical amounts pursuant to the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act ("the TUCT Act"). The unpaid company tax was specified at $60,481.26 (cf. ante) and additional tax for late payment of the company assessment was set at $8,053.12, a total of $68,534.38. One-half of that total - $34,267.19 - was claimed against each applicant by assessments of 26 June 1984. Objection was taken in each case and the matters came to a Taxation Board of review for review. They were heard together by consent. The hearing was protracted. The two days in February 1986 set aside for the hearing by the Chairman of the Board, after conferring with the parties, proved to be inadequate with the result that the hearing had to be adjourned until May 1986 when it took up a further three days. In a written submission by way of summary of argument presented to the Board following the hearing, Mr. Clyne summarised the matter by saying:-

"The issues which remain at the end of the day to be decided are those which I stated at the very beginning of the case ... i.e. were the appellants shareholders at the relevant time, and, if yes, was there a sale?"

4. Since then the Boards of Review have been abolished pursuant to the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act and pending applications were transferred to this Tribunal. Pursuant to that legislation the two members of the Board which heard the evidence and argument have been appointed to make the decisions upon the references.

5. It will be necessary to examine the evidence in relation to the contentions referred in paragraph 3 above in detail. However, at the outset it is appropriate to observe that, although the taxpayers (Aco and Bco) are bodies corporate and their liability to tax is alleged to arise out of unsatisfied tax liability of another company (Zco), all three of those companies and each other company to be referred to in these reasons is nothing more than a legal fiction. That being so, what is important factually is the conduct in all respects of the individuals involved as affecting the relationships between those persons. That conduct becomes the foundation for attributing acts and omissions to the companies. It will also be appropriate in due course to consider the significance to be attached to the circumstance that neither the taxpayers on the one hand nor the Commissioner on the other saw fit to call either X or Y to the witness box.

6. The principal witnesses to give evidence were A and B. Their respective spouses, Mrs. A and Mrs. B, also gave evidence, although briefly. In each case husband and wife were the sole directors of Aco or Bco (as the case may be). Much of the evidence related to the business relationships between A, B, X and Y which were reflected in the activities of a number of companies, including in particular Zco. (In these reasons I propose to refer to those four persons as "the partners" as that concept was used in Yenidje Tobacco Co. Ltd., (1916) 2 Ch 426.) A and B attribute their problems with the Commissioner in relation to Aco and Bco in large measure to having misplaced their trust in X and Y.

7. As to Aco, the evidence of A and Mrs. A was that they controlled that company as "a family company". Both were directors and, according to the records, A was appointed Managing Director in November 1973. The evidence of Mrs. A was that she knew nothing in relation to shares acquired in Zco and nothing of any sale of such shares. Happily married since 1973, as a matter of course she did not discuss her husband's business affairs with him. I accept that evidence. I am satisfied that the "controlling mind and will" of Aco was to be found in the mind and will of A; that Mrs. A consented to A acting alone in all matters relating Aco; that he had her authority to do so; and that he enjoyed her complete confidence.

8. As to Bco, both B and Mrs. B. gave evidence. They had controlled Bco as a family investment company from about 1976 when they had married. The company itself had a longer history and 3 had been associated with it from 1966. Except for a period in the mid-70's he had been a Director from that time. Since the critical period (August 1980) B and Mrs. B had separated; been divorced; resumed their relationship; and, at the time of hearing, were preparing to remarry. The evidence of Mrs. B was that she had "heard of" Zco without knowing anything in detail about it. She knew that B "invested in real estate" without knowing any details. She recalled signing documents as a Director or Secretary from time to time, and. that in a period related to the marriage break-up their accountant had been authorised to sign some company documents in her place. I accept that evidence. As with Aco, I am satisfied that the controlling mind and will of Bco was to be found in the mind and will of B; that Mrs. B consented to B acting alone in all matters relating to Bco; that he had her authority to do so; and that at all material times so far as is material he enjoyed her complete confidence in that regard.

9. The background to the present problem arose out of the circumstance that A had been involved in selling minor, but necessary, domestic equipment for many years and in that activity he had been associated with X. In about 1977 A and X first associated with B and shortly thereafter they established a new business conducted by a company "Salesco". A and B were the "partners" most actively involved in the selling aspects of the business of Salesco. X was more interested in property dealings and through him A and B became involved in those property dealings through their respective companies. Those property dealings were facilitated in that Salesco had a substantial cash flow which assisted in the financing of property transactions. At about this time A and B met with Y, solicitor to X. Y later became a fourth and equal "partner" although in retrospect A and B, particularly the latter, felt unjustly treated in that regard because (they contended) Y's principal contribution to any enterprise was to provide his professional services for which he charged professional fees before participating equally in the ultimate profit distribution.

10. The evidence of property dealings was neither precise nor detailed. There was the evidence of A and B but that was limited because they said that they had left the legal, accounting and financial details to X and Y. There was information reproduced or otherwise obtained from public records. That evidence I accept although its significance is not always clear. Further, a Minute Book of Zco was produced. I accept the evidence of A and B that they could not vouch for the accuracy of the Minutes as Minutes. However, I am satisfied that in so far as the Minutes are consistent with the information derived from public records that they do provide a formal record, which is in substance accurate, of matters quite informally agreed between the "partners".

11. Against that background I am satisfied that property dealings occurred as follows:- Zco was incorporated as a "shelf-company" by third parties on 20 June 1979 and next day came under the control of X, Y, A and B, who thereafter met often, although usually informally, to consider property dealings. The expectation of both A and B was that they, through Aco and Bco respectively, would enjoy a one-quarter interest in property transactions to be undertaken using Zco as a corporate vehicle for all or some aspects of those transactions. It was decided to purchase a property damaged by fire (Blackacre), to renovate it and to resell it. B played a large part in effecting the renovations but he had little enough to do with the legal, financial and accounting aspects of the matter. His evidence - (the figures were approximate) - was that the property was purchased for $20,000; renovated at a cost of $20,000; and sold at a cost of $125,000. He further gave evidence that his belief was that "tax" had been deducted from the profit of $85,000 and that the resulting "after-tax profit" had been distributed at $13,000 to each of the four entities entitled. (A thought distributions took place "on a couple of occasions" in amounts of "$15,000 or something like that".) I am not persuaded that B's belief was accurate although I do accept that substantial amounts - possibly $13,000 - were received in each case payable to the use of Aco and Bco. More importantly I find that there was a profit derived by Zco and that it was not distributed by way of dividend or reduction of capital. Aided by the public records and the Minute Book I find that the property was purchased for a price of $20,000; that it was mortgaged to a third party to secure repayment of $45,000; that it was sold to a company associated with some at least of the partners on 16 January 1980 for $75,000 and to a third party for $125,000 on 25 January 1980. It may be that the gross profit to Zco was of the order of $30,000 or $80,000 but, whichever it was, I am not persuaded that it had been distributed so as to diminish the net worth of Zco. It follows that by 1 August 1980 the profit remained part of Zco's assets. It had not been assessed to income tax.

12. The dealings with the second property (Whiteacre) were more complicated. The evidence shows that in this instance X contracted in his own name to purchase Whiteacre at $420,000. When he took title he contemporaneously transferred one portion for $1 to Zco; a second portion for $1 to a company which by its name appears to have been identified with Y; and a third and the last section for $1 to a company by its name apparently identified with X. Contemporaneously the entirety of Whiteacre was mortgaged to an independent finance company for $240,000 and A, B, X and Y each guaranteed the performance by Zco of its obligations under the mortgage. In this instance the entire property was able to be later resold without having been the subject of any redevelopment. The resale was effected by contemporaneous transfers by the then three registered proprietors to one third party with the consideration being expressed to be $400,000, $130,000 and $150,000 respectively, thereby generating a gross profit of the order of $260,000. Once again the evidence of A and B was that they expected by their respective companies, Aco - Bco, to be entitled to one-quarter of the entire profit. Once again there is no evidence to suggest that the net profit, which upon the evidence would have been gross profit less holding costs, or so much of it as was due to Zco, did not remain with Zco at 1 August 1980.

13. Those findings leave me persuaded that at 1 August 1980 substantial profits which might have been assessed as income against Zco were not only untaxed but remained with Zco in the sense that they contributed to the then net worth of Zco. Further, I am not persuaded that the net worth of Zco at 1 August 1980 - leaving aside any question of liability or contingent liability to income tax - was less than $131,483 or its total untaxed income was less than that sum.

14. The evidence of A and B was - and I accept it - that they largely left arrangements in relation to property transactions in the hands of X and Y. They trusted them. At 1 August 1980, and for some time afterwards, they placed in X and Y that measure of confidence which reasonably is to be expected between "partners" whether the particular legal form of their relationship be that of a limited liability company (as in the case of the two "partners" in Yenidje Tobacco Co-ante); or a joint venture (United Dominions Corporation Ltd v. Brian Pty Ltd [1985] HCA 49; (1985) 59 ALJR 676); or a partnership as defined by the Partnership Acts. A and B expected that all four men would act towards each other "with utmost good faith".

15. That finding is basic to my consideration of the evidence touching the events relevant to 1 August 1980 - the alleged date of "sale" of Zco shares. I am satisfied that in the course of a few days, and on 1 August 1980 in particular, the result of the actions of A, B, X and Y was that a purported disposition by sale of the entire shareholding in Zco took place in circumstances whereby the shares in Zco were disposed of for $121,622 - substantially the net worth of the company without any allowance being made for any contingent or potential tax liability of Zco.

16. The sale, if there was a sale, was effected pursuant to a contract of sale whereby Aco and Bco, the vendors under the contract, warranted themselves to be holders of all the issued capital in Zco (one ordinary share each) and agreed for the sale of the same in each case for $60,811. That contract was executed on behalf of the vendors and the purchasers by a person claimed to be an attorney for his principals. The contract was executed in Darwin. The price was consistent with the evidence of property profits gained by Zco and not lost to the company by distribution of dividend or equivalent. Evidence was presented before the Board in the form of a handwritten "spread sheet"; a Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet; and a "purchase sheet" (with instructions for settlement procedures annexed), all of which were identified and deposed to by the accountant who had wholly, or substantially, prepared each document. He gave evidence of having prepared the "spread sheet" by taking into account information provided to him by a superior. The details of that information were not available to him in giving evidence and could not be recalled, but his evidence was - and I accept it - that the information was not presented to him in the form of the primary accounting records of Zco. Since the "purchase" figures were derived from the Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet; and the Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet were derived from the spread sheet; and the spread sheet was based on information unknown from uncertain sources, I do not rely on that evidence. It is significant only in that it casts no doubt on the accuracy of the matters established from more reliable sources.

17. The first difficulty in the reference arises from a contention that deficiencies in the documents touching the acquisition and disposal of the shares in Zco are said to produce a result whereby neither taxpayer ever became a shareholder in Zco and alternatively, never sold shares in Zco.

18. I am satisfied on the evidence before us that in one instance a forgery was involved. On 29 July 1980 arrangements were made with the Bank of New Zealand for an account to be opened in the name of Zco. The authority to open the account purported to be signed by two directors. One signature purported to be that of A. A denied that the signature was his and I accept that denial. Although that action may shed light on the standards adopted by those more closely concerned in the sale than A and B, it does not directly assist in relation to the critical issues.

19. The documentary records of 1 August, 1980 and later relating to the sale to the extent which were produced in evidence, in the sequence they represent, were as follows:-

(a) The execution of Aco and Bco of share transmittal requests.

(b) Minutes of a Meeting of Directors of Zco said to have been attended by Y (as Chairman), X, A and B at North Sydney at 9 a.m. which authorised the establishment of a branch register of members at Darwin and the transfer to it of shares in response to share transmittal requests from Aco and Bco.

(c) Appointment by Aco and Bco of John Doe as their attorney.

(d) The execution by the attorney of a contract of sale in Darwin.

(e) The execution by the attorney of share transfers in Darwin.

(f) A further meeting of Directors of Zco chaired by Y and attended also by X, A and B in North Sydney at 10.30 a.m. approving transfers from Aco and Bco to joint purchasers; providing for the appointment of new Directors, Secretary and Public" Officer, followed by resignations of the outgoing officers; the relocation of the Registered Office; and the opening of a bank account with the Bank of New Zealand.

(g) The writing up of company registers to record not only the vesting of shares in the purchasers but also - for the first time - the vesting of the shares in Aco and Bco.

20. I am satisfied that no meetings of the Directors of Zco were held at the times or at the place recorded in the Minutes. What occurred on August 1, 1980 so far as A and B were involved was as follows. On 1 August 1980 - some few days only after the sale of Whiteacre was finalised - at the request of X, A and B attended together at offices in mid-city not previously known to them. They knew that the object of the visit was to affix company seals for their respective companies. On arrival they found that X and Y were already in attendance. Several other persons, all strangers to A and B, were also present. After a brief wait admiring the view from the high-rise office, A and B, with X and Y, went into another room in which documents were laid out on a table. As requested, both A and B affixed their company seals and their own signatures to documents presented to them. Their evidence was - and I accept it - that they did not read any of the documents and did not know with any precision what they were. When all the signing and sealing required of them was completed, they left. X and Y remained. The documents remained. I accept all of the foregoing. However, in so far as it was suggested that both A and B believed that the documents related only to interests in land, I reject that. I am satisfied that they were content to do what was requested of them by X and Y and that they intended that their actions should take effect as X and Y wished. They trusted their "partners".

21. From the evidence before us, it appears that in each case the documents executed were:- a request for removal of one ordinary share in Zco from its Sydney to a Darwin Register; and the appointment of one John Doe with a power of attorney sufficient to enable him to complete the planned transaction. (The Power of Attorney referred to power "to execute agreements for sale as and when advised of the type annexed hereto and marked with the letter A". I accept that more probably than not the agreement for sale to be later mentioned was not annexed at the time A and B signed and sealed those documents.)

22. Having departed from the meeting neither A nor B (nor, upon the evidence, any other person) in the interests of Aco or Bco did anything in relation to the affairs of Zco (such as to act as directors of Zco or to relieve themselves of the responsibilities of that office); and no action was taken by Aco or Bco to realise their investment in Zco or their claim to share in any profit arising from the property dealings previously mentioned (whether reflected in the accounts of Zco or not).

23. It is contended for the applicants that that inaction is explained by the circumstance that Aco and Bco (by A and B) were unaware that they had ever become registered as shareholders in Zco and, accordingly, they were unaware that their shares had been transferred (or purported to be transferred) upon the sale. I am not persuaded that that is the appropriate explanation. I find that the relationship of trust between A, B, X and Y at 1 August 1980 was such that A and B were so completely confident in their "partners" - that they would act in all respects in the best interests of the "partnership" - that, having that confidence, they were prepared to execute any documents placed before them by X and Y and to assume for themselves and for Aco and Bco such rights and responsibilities in the interests of the group as X or Y requested. That being so, I conclude that A and B, for themselves and, subject to considerations yet to be addressed, for Aco and Bco, acted so as to allow themselves to become the only registered holders of issued capital in Zco; to appoint an attorney with full and sufficient power to enter into an agreement for the sale of those shares; and, by their attorney and by X and Y, to give effect to an agreement for the sale of all those shares; and, by their attorney or X or Y (or any of them) to receive payment upon the sale.

24. Numerous contentions were advanced for the applicants proposing that there had been no "sale" in any relevant sense. Among those contentions were the following:-

(a) Neither Aco or Bco was paid the purchase price. In the sense that there was no direct remission of moneys from the purchaser to the applicants I accept that, but that is a very narrow proposition and falls far short of establishing that the purchase price was not paid in circumstances sufficient to enable the purchaser to have successfully defended an action brought against it by Aco and Bco to recover the price. I accept that A and B suspect that the price may have passed to, or to the use of, X and Y but even if it did there is nothing in the evidence which would indicate that it has not been brought to account by them and fully accounted for in other dealings between the partners, their associated companies and ventures.

(b) That neither the "agreement for sale" nor any of the transactions purporting to be implemented by or recorded in the documents was intended by either of the vendors or by the purchaser to be of legal effect. I have accepted that the meetings of 1 August 1980 as recorded by the Minutes did not occur at the times and place recorded. However, I am not persuaded that the purchaser did not intend the purchase to be effective. We simply have no evidence of the state of mind of the purchaser. Further, as to the vendors, in so far as the intention of either vendor is to be determined by reference to the state of mind of A and B, I am satisfied that in each case they intended that the documents executed and the transactions embarked on on 1 August 1980 would be effective for their terms, whatever they might be, in accordance with the wishes of X and Y.

(c) That the vendors are not bound by the documents relating to the sale and are entitled to rely on the plea non est factum. That submission was only lightly pressed which, having regard to the joint judgment of the Full High Court in Petelin v Cullin [1975] HCA 24; (1975) 132 C.L.R 355, is hardly surprising. There the High Court said (at p. 359):-

"The class of persons who can avail themselves of the defence is limited. It is available to those who are unable to read owing to blindness or illiteracy and who must rely on others for advice as to what they are signing; it is also available to those who through no fault of their own are unable to have any understanding of the purport of a particular document. To make out the defence a defendant must show that he signed the document in the belief that it was radically different from what it was in fact and that, at least as against innocent persons, his failure to read and understand it was not due to carelessness on his part. Finally, it is accepted that there is a heavy onus on a defendant who seeks to establish the defence."

(d) That the applicants did not become shareholders at all and, therefore, could not have sold the shares because each only assented to becoming a shareholder on the basis that it would be one of four equal shareholders. In my view, the submission fails on the evidence. I am not persuaded that the applicants were not willing that they should be registered as the only holders of shares to be held in the interests of the partners as a whole. (I shall consider other aspects of beneficial entitlement in due course.) However, more significantly it fails because by the agreement for sale executed by their attorney they agreed to sell the entirety of the share capital; and they have not established that the sale did not eventuate or that the price was not paid (cf. Cuninghame v Citv of Glasgow Bank (1878) 4 A.C. 607 at 611).

25. I now turn to arguments based on "deficiencies" in the execution of documents by the taxpayer companies. I accept that the Articles of Association of both Aco and Bco only allowed the company seal to be used by authority cf the Directors and that the Articles further required that documents to be sealed should be signed by a Director and counter-signed by the Secretary or another Director or by a witness appointed for that purpose by the Directors; that no prior meeting of Directors authorised the use of the seal in relation to any of the documents in question; that no prior meeting of Directors authorised any person to counter-sign on the affixing of the company seal; and that, although signed by a Director (A in the one case and B in the other), the counter-signature was not that of another Director or of the Secretary or of a person appointed for the purpose by any formal resolution of directors. However, I am of the opinion that as between the applicants and the purchaser those matters are of no consequence. The critically important document was the agreement for sale executed on behalf of the applicants as vendors by the appointee pursuant to the disputed power of attorney. There is no basis in the evidence before us for holding that he was aware of any deficiency in the execution of the instrument appointing him or that he did not act to his detriment in holding himself out as an agent capable of bringing into existence an enforceable agreement for sale between the applicants (as vendors) and the purchasers. That being so I am of opinion that each company has failed to prove that the agreement for sale was ever less than valid and enforceable according to its terms between the applicants as vendors and their purchaser. Furthermore, that conclusion cannot be averted by a finding - as I do find in the case of B - that at the time of execution he realised that the process of sealing was incomplete. Even if it was thought in each case that the document was ineffective as it stood, nonetheless it was then left with X and Y to be used as they approved, which approval extended to putting it within the power of X and Y to "complete" the documents and do what else they thought fit in completing the task they were about. I further hold that, even if X and Y fraudulently exercised the power so entrusted to them, that does not alter the fact that the sale took place, there being no evidence suggesting that the purchaser either knew or had reason to suspect such misuse of power. I conclude, therefore, that the applicants have failed to persuade me that there was not on 1 August 1980 a sale by the applicants for a price of the entire issued share capital in Zco.

26. The question which now needs to be addressed is whether the circumstances outlined above give rise to liability in the applicants under the TUCT Act and, in particular, s 5 thereof. The applicants seek to establish that they are not so liable by a series of arguments.

a) The first is that they contend that not having become registered as the shareholders of the issued capital in Zco, they could not sell, and therefore did not sell the share capital in Zco. I reject the argument. The question is not whether the names of the applicants were correctly entered on the register of Zco prior to the sale of the shares by persons duly authorised to make the entries. Rather the question is whether the applicants "sold" the shares in Zco. As appears from earlier in these reasons I am not persuaded by the applicants that they did not "sell" all of the shares in Zco.

b) The second argument is that, even if the applicants did sell all the shares in Zco, one-half of the issued capital was held by them in trust for X and Y or their nominees; and that being "bare trustees" they were not capable of controlling 90 per cent of the voting power in the company. In order to consider the substance of the submission, I will assume that the applicants were trustees and only "bare trustees" as contended. I reject the submission. Voting power can only be exercised by persons entitled to vote. Such persons may be under an obligation to others to exercise their voting power in a particular way, but the voting power exercised is that of the person entitled to vote and not that of persons entitled to direct the vote holder as to the exercise of that power. I am confirmed in that view by the fact that the definition of "person" (s 3) includes reference to "a person in the capacity of trustee of a trust estate".

c) The third submission in this regard for the applicants is that they contend that between them they held a half-interest in the issued capital of Zco on trust for X and Y or their nominees and that for that purpose they were "bare trustees" within the meaning of that phrase in s 5(5) of the TUCT Act. If so, it is contended that the effect of s 5(5) is that so far as those shares were held in trust they were outside the scope of s 5(1) and that liability, to that extent, was the liability of X and Y (or their nominees) by force of s 5(5).

27. Before evaluating the evidence as to that trusteeship, it is appropriate to have regard to the absence from the witness box of X and Y and to the failure of those who did give evidence to testify more precisely as to other matters. Tax litigation as much as any other civil litigation is conducted on the basis that each party is at liberty to present his case as he or his adviser chooses. For persons such as applicants in tax litigation it is necessary for them to adduce sufficient acceptable evidence to persuade the Tribunal on the balance of probabilities as to facts which in law will support the conclusion contended for by the applicants. For the Commissioner it is enough that the Tribunal should not be so persuaded by the evidence presented for the applicants. It is not necessary that the Commissioner call witnesses but the evidence of such witnesses as he does choose to call may strengthen the case for the Commissioner. It is trite law that there is no "property" in a witness. Subject to questions of privilege, both the Commissioner and the applicants are entitled to call on any person to attend and give evidence. It happened in these proceedings that neither saw fit to call on X and Y to attend as witnesses. That being so it is in order for the Tribunal to conclude that X and Y would "not have assisted" the party who might reasonably be expected to have called them (cf. Jones v. Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1958) 101 CLR 298 at 308). In this case the applicants put forward a case which, if successful, would relieve them of liability to pay tax to the extent to which X and Y (or interests associated with them) would be exposed to tax liability. On the other hand, the Commissioner's case is that X and Y have no such liability because the entirety of the liability to tax lies with Aco and Bco. That being so, the party who might reasonably be expected to have called X and Y is the Commissioner. He did not do so. I conclude that the absence of X and Y from the witness box does not assist the Commissioner in establishing the liability of the applicants.

28. For the applicants it is contended that they did not receive the proceeds of sale. If that is only to be understood to mean that they did not at a particular time receive a specific sum quite distinct from other amounts and quite clearly appropriated to them as the sale price of the shares, I accept the evidence. That is not to say that they have not received that sale price in some other way, perhaps as an element in the settling of accounts on a broader base. The evidence, although brief and undetailed, was that some time after the sale of the shares in Zco there was a winding up of all business dealings between A and B for themselves and their respective interests and X and Y and their respective interests. That evidence was only put in general terms. It was not detailed. It was not supported by, or by reference to, any accounts or any terms of settlement such as one might have expected to attend the finalisation of such financial relationships. It is to be expected that upon such final settlement A and B would have been concerned for themselves and the interests they represented to ensure that they received all of they were entitled to in relation to all ventures in which they had participated. That would have extended to an accounting in relation to the ventures which generated the assets which made valuable the shares sold by the applicants. It is reasonable to expect that such accounts or evidence of them under the control of A and B might have clarified matters but no such evidence was produced and no explanation was presented as to why such evidence was not produced. I conclude that such evidence "would not have assisted" the applicants in establishing their case.

29. I now turn to the question whether on the balance of probabilities it has been established by the applicants that Aco and Bco were "bare trustees" in relation to the share capital of Zco. Some evidence points one way; other evidence points the other; and the evidence of X and Y and more detaied evidence as to the finalisation of accounts which might have clarified the matter was not presented. At the end of the day I am not persuaded that Aco and Bco were merely "bare trustees" in relation to any of the share capital in Zco or that Aco and Bco were not beneficially entitled to one-half each of the whole issued capital in Zco.

30. Having reached that conclusion it is unnecessary to consider whether the grounds of the objection lodged by each applicant are in terms sufficient to enable the relief sought to have been granted; or the further question, which may now arise pursuant to the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act, whether the grounds of objection should be amended to allow all the matters argued to be relied on.

31. I would affirm the decision of the Commissioner upon the objections.

32. I would confirm the determination of the Commissioner upon the objections.
