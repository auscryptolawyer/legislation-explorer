TT84/77 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 84; (1987) 18 ATR 3307; 87 ATC 363 (11 March 1987)

Last Updated: 15 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Contributions for superannuation benefits to approved section 79 fund - whether deductions only available to person "gainfully engaged" - what is necessary as prerequisite 'to "retirement"

Income Tax Assessment Act S6, 82AAS and 82AAT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

NO: TT84/77

AAT Decision No 3281

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

11 March 1987

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. TT84/77

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In this reference the Applicant contributed to a fund under the name "(Life Office) Employed Persons Superannuation Fund" ("the Fund"), which was acknowledged by the Commissioner' s representative to he a superannuation fund approved by the Commissioner for the purposes of section 79 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. For the year of income ended 30 June 1983 the Applicant claimed an income tax deduction in the sum of $1,200 as a deduction allowable pursuant to section S2AAT (1) of the Act, $1,200 being the maximum permitted deduction.

2. So far as is material, section 82AAT (1) provided:

"...there shall be allowed as a deduction from the assessable income of an eligible person .... the amount of any contribution co a qualifying superannuation fund, being contributions made to obtain superannuation benefits for the eligible person or in the event of the death of the eligible person for the dependents of the eligible person."

3. It is common ground that the contribution was made; that it was made by the Applicant; that it was made during the year of income; and that it was made to a "qualifying superannuation fund." That being so, the questions for determination are whether the Applicant was "an eligible person"; and, if he was, whether 'his contribution was made "to obtain superannuation benefits" for himself, or in the event of his death, for his dependents.

4. In my view he was an eligible person. Sub-section 2 of Section 82AAS provides that all persons are eligible persons unless specified disqualifying factors - which do not arise in the circumstances of this case - exist. However, the Commissioner contends that he was not eligible for membership of the Fund to which his contributions were made, and that, for that reason, he could not and did not become a member of the Fund; and did not become entitled to "obtain superannuation benefits" from that Fund.

5. The Commissioner stated his then position in a letter of March 1984 which accompanied the notice disallowing the objection made by the Applicant. The Commissioner said:

"Section 79 only applies to a fund if, amongst other requirements, benefits are to be provided to members on retirement "from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment". Excluded are persons whose only income is as a beneficiary of a trust or from investments."

"As you earned no personal exertion income in the year of income under review you cannot be regarded as being gainfully employed in that year and as a result you were not eligible to make superannuation contributions to a section 79 fund. The contributions made therefore are excluded from consideration under Sub-Division AB as they were not made to a qualifying Superannuation Fund as defined."

At the hearing it was also contended that to be eligible for the deduction the individual must be in gainful employment such as to be capable of "retiring" from it. The phrase "gainful employment" finds no place in the Act.

6. The Applicant suffered the handicap of serious physical disability. He owned five conjoint one-bedroom flats in a country town which he let partly furnished and a house which he also let, but on an unfurnished basis. All the properties were within about three kilometers of his own residence. All tenancies were effected by written agreement. Tenants were "screened" by an estate agent who also collected the rents, but the Applicant had an active part in the management of the let premises. He attended at the flats on about three days per week and his evidence was that he "inspected" them virtually daily - I understand the latter to mean no more than that he saw the premises as he passed them on days when he did not attend at them. He occasionally mowed the lawns. He grubbed and sprayed the weeds about twice per annum. He cleaned the windows. He cleaned the premises on changes of tenancy and did "touch up" painting as occasionally required. He transferred rubbish to the tip. He negotiated with prospective tenants and, at the request of tenants, arranged for matters of maintenance and. repair to be attended to. He generally maintained the premises and grounds. The things he did were such as might have been done by a managing agent or by a casual handy-man. He asserted, and I accept it, that for him to have paid others to do the things he did would have adversely and significantly reduced profit margins on the properties. As to the level of activity undertaken I am satisfied that it occupied the Applicant for some part of his time without being onerous or demanding - although calling for frequent attention. It gave him something to do and the doing of it was significant in the totality of his affairs. I also accept that it gave the Applicant the personal satisfaction of thinking that he was usefully and productively employed rather than idle, and that he was eligible for membership of the superannuation fund.

7. Gross rents amounted to $13,001. Expenses incurred were: insurance $225, rates $275, repairs $1,088, agent's commission $650, depreciation $2,054, freight $17, gardener $60, legal expenses $50 and vehicle expenses $180. His only other assessable income came from "interest only" investments and amounted to $13,917. After payment of his "superannuation contributions" $1,200 and allowances for gifts, he calculated his taxable income at $18,537.

8. A copy of the trust deed of the Fund was placed in evidence and I accept it as setting forth accurately the terms material to establishing the relationship between the Applicant and the Fund. Membership was stated to be open to any person who was an "employed person" as defined, although the Deed also unnecessarily stated that the Trustee had. an "absolute discretion" to accept or refuse applications for membership. It was provided that "where the context so admits and unless the contrary intention appears.... " employed person" means a person who is an employee or who is gainfully engaged in any business, trade, profession vocation, occupation or work but shall not include a.... self-employed person." A " self-employed person" was defined as a person who is or becomes a resident of.... and who is a person whose net income from personal exertion in that Financial Year exceeds one half of his total net income for such year and who for the purposes of the Act, carries on business or a professional practice, either alone or as a partner, and who in such year derives from that business or professional practice a net income which exceeds one half of the net amount of income from personal exertion derived by him in such year." In my view the Applicant was neither "an employee" nor a " self-employed person".

9. Despite the many references in the trust deed to considerations which were more material to the interests of the Commissioner than to any dealing between the member and the Fund present no doubt because the Fund wanted to maintain recognition from the Commissioner - I am satisfied that as a matter of contract the Applicant did become a member of the Fund and at the date of hearing remained entitled to all benefits as if he had at all times been a member of the Fund. If membership was procured by a misrepresentation - I accept that if there was any misrepresentation on the part of the Applicant it was an honest one - it would have only made the contract voidable at the option of the Fund, and there is no suggestion that the Fund had made any move towards that end.

10. That being so, the question then becomes whether the contributions were made "to obtain superannuation benefits" for the eligible person or his dependents. The phrase "superannuation benefits" is a loose one which means many different things to many different people. Section 6 of the Act provides that. "unless the contrary intention appears, "superannuation benefits" means individual personal benefits, pensions or retiring allowances": a definition which does little to advance the matter. As the Applicant's claim relates to contributions to an approved Superannuation Fund - that is, a fund approved by the Commissioner - it is appropriate to have regard to the provisions of section 79 in a general way to ascertain some of the characteristics of such a superannuation fund. Such a fund is established to provide benefits for members in the event of "retirement of the member from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which he is engaged or for the provision of benefits for the dependents of such a person; or for either of those purposes". Such benefits were not to be payable to the member prior to age 60 except in the event of death, sickness, permanent incapacity for work or in other circumstances approved by the Commissioner; and any pension or annuity to be paid was to commence not later than age 70. Further, the benefits had to be "reasonable in amount".

11. The Commissioner seems to have considered the Applicant as if he were a mere investor - a person deriving income from real estate as another might derive interest income from fixed interest investments or from the ownership of a stable share portfolio. He considered that the Applicant was not "gainfully employed" as if that meant "not wholly occupied". That approach underrates what the Applicant did, although what he did do fell far short of being an intense, demanding full-time occupation. However, the level of activity he maintained was such that he might not always be able to maintain it or be willing to do so. In those circumstances he might cease his management activities such as they were, and he might then say of himself that he had "retired".

  1. In any event, by gaining admission to membership in the Fund he had gained an entitlement to benefits payable in accordance with the general requirements of section 79 of the Act as to retirement. In the absence of "death, sickness or permanent incapacity for work" nothing would have been payable to him before attaining the age of 60 years - a date by which an ever increasing percentage of the population is said to have "retired"; but with an obligation to take benefits on obtaining the age of 70 years - an age by which "retirement" (but not inactivity) is commonly compulsory.
  2. I accept that underlying the concept of "superannuation" is the idea that it is in the national interest to encourage persons to invest in the future development of the country by setting aside monies otherwise available for current expenditure, doing so in the expectation of only realising on their investment later in life, when by reason of age or disability such persons are likely to be less active and less productive and likely to become dependent on the public purse for their support if in the past they had not made adequate provision for their future. Hence the special favourable tax treatment of section 79 funds, but conditioned upon the Commissioner's approval of the constitution of those funds in an endeavour to ensure that they will not be so structured or managed in ways which the Commissioner considers to be unreasonable.

14. As I understand it the Commissioner's view is that: -

  1. Such funds should not be approved if they are so structured that persons not "gainfully employed" are to be admitted as members. The Fund in question was not so structured.
  2. The Applicant was not entitled to membership of this Fund because he was not "gainfully employed". No-one was entitled to membership of the Fund and the constitution of the Fund expressly provided that the persons not "gainfully engaged" would not be entitled to membership.
  1. That the Applicant did not become a member of the Fund because he was not entitled to or eligible for membership. Despite that the Applicant applied for and was admitted to membership and remains a member even though it might be within power for the fund to seek to avoid that contractual relationship by reason of misrepresentation.
  1. That the Applicant did not become entitled to "superannuation benefits". Having secured membership he was entitled to the benefits conferred by that membership: and those benefits are aptly described as "superannuation benefits", whether the term be used to characterise the benefits payable to the generality of members or to the, Applicant in particular.

15. In summary, it is my view that two distinct problems have come to be confused. The first problem relates to the status of the Fund as an approved section 79 Fund. That is not an issue before me. The second problem is whether the Applicant is entitled to the deduction he seeks. In my view he is. He was an "eligible person". He secured membership of an approved superannuation fund and became entitled to future benefits. Those benefits would be "superannuation benefits". He made a contribution of $1,200 to that fund in the year in question to obtain those benefits and having done so, he is entitled to the deduction claimed.

16. For those reasons I would uphold the objection of the taxpayer.
