DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No QT97/254-261

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicants



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION

Tribunal Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date 2 June 1998

Place Brisbane

Decision The Tribunal decides that:

(a) the objection decisions for the years of income ended 30 June 1989, 30 June 1990 and 30 June 1991 are affirmed;

(b) subject to the claimed outgoings being substantiated the objections for the year of income ended 30 June 1988 are allowed;

(c) liberty to apply in relation to the 1988 year assessments will be reserved to both parties; and

(d) this matter has terminated in a manner favourable to the applicants.



(Sgd) K L Beddoe

Decision No 12945 (Senior Member)



CATCHWORDS

TAXATION : Income Tax - allowable deductions - repairs - motor vehicle business - storm damage - principles considered - improvements constructed after acquisition of property - repairs effected prior to acquisition - remission of additional tax



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 53, 223, 227

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Regulations 19(5)



W Thomas & Co v. FCT [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 14 ATD 78

Case V167 88 ATC 1107

Morcom v Campbell-Johnson [1955] 3 All ER 264

Law Shipping Co Ltd v. Inland Revenue Commissioners (1924) SC74; 12 Tax Cas. 621

Case 74/96 96 ATC 662

REASONS FOR DECISION

2 June 1998 Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



  1. The applicants seek review of objection decisions by the respondent refusing deductions for claims for repairs relating to a building in which the applicants carry on business. The objection decisions relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1988 to 30 June 1991.
  2. Section 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) reads as follows:
“53(1) Expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income for repairs, not being expenditure of a capital nature, to any premises, or part of premises, plant, machinery, implements, utensils, rolling stock, or articles held, occupied or used by him for the purpose of producing assessable income, or in carrying on a business for that purpose, shall be an allowable deduction.

53(2) Expenditure incurred upon repairs to any premises or part of premises not so held occupied or used shall not be an allowable deduction.

53(3) Where the premises, part of premises, plant, machinery, implements, utensils, rolling stock or articles referred to in subsection (1) were held, occupied or used by the taxpayer only partly for the purpose of producing assessable income, or only partly in carrying on a business for that purpose, so much only of the expenditure that, but for this subsection, would be an allowable deduction under subsection (1) as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, is reasonable in the circumstances shall be an allowable deduction.”
  1. At the hearing the male applicant conducted the applicants’ case and Mr Coulsen of counsel appeared for the respondent. The applicants are husband and wife who carry on business in partnership. The documents lodged in the Tribunal for all the applications were in evidence and marked as the T documents. Further documents were tendered by the parties and marked as exhibits. Oral evidence was given by the male applicant (“H”), an auditor employed in the Australian Taxation Office and a valuer employed in the Australian Valuation Office.
  2. Taking into account the oral and documentary evidence before me I make the following findings of fact. In these findings I have referred to the building work as “refurbishment” - a generic term for these purposes with no specific meaning:
(a) H belongs to a family which conducted a wide and diversified business in a provincial city - one aspect of that business was a motor vehicle dealership and motor garage (“the motor garage”) conducted by the family company for many years.

(b) From 1974, H was manager of the motor garage business as a division of the family business.

(c) The family company which I will call “Motor Garage (No 1) Pty Ltd” was incorporated on 12 July 1974, the directors and equal shareholders being H and his father (“H Senior”) (Exhibit B) and thereafter conducted the motor garage business.

(d) Motor Garage (No 1) Pty Ltd ceased conducting the motor garage business as at 30 June 1987 and the applicants took over the business and commenced trading as partners from 1 July 1987.

(e) The freehold property at which Motor Garage (No 1) Pty Ltd carried on business was owned by H Senior and his wife at all relevant times until 17 October 1988 when they signed a Memorandum of Transfer in favour of the applicants as joint tenants in consideration for the payment of $100.00. The transfer was registered on or about 13 December 1988.

(f) During the years in dispute the applicants paid substantial amounts in relation to the refurbishment of the premises occupied by the motor garage business. Claims for outgoings for repairs made in the Taxation Returns of the partnership are as follows:

Year ended 30 June Amount

$

1988 21,962

1989 132,940

1990 63,596

1991 56,661

(g) The circumstances of the outgoings had their genesis in a storm which caused damage to the roof of the business premises. As a consequence the applicants arranged for a builder to undertake repairs. However, the builder reported substantial and widespread white ant infestation with the consequence that the basic structure of the roof was no longer viable and needed to be replaced.

(h) There was also white ant infestation in the walls of the premises which on the initial survey was thought to be repairable. However a consideration of photographs included in Exhibit 4 indicates that there was major damage to the building timbers.

(i) On 19 December 1988 the Municipal Authority approved a building work application dated 21 July 1988 for “Repairs and Alterations Garage” at an estimated cost of $56,000.00. The general description of the building work was said to be:

“External walls - Brick and Iron

Internal walls - Virsilux

Frame - Steel

Roof Covering - Cor [sic] Iron” (T31)

The municipal authority originally rejected the application because it came to the view that it was in fact an application to build a new building which would thereby bring into operation various requirements of the Building Code (Exhibit 2).

(j) Before refurbishment the premises consisted of two separate constructions made at different times together with modifications. Essentially the premises were timber frame with corrugated iron cladding in some sections, cement sheeting in other sections, timber lining boards in some sections and varying floor construction.

(k) In the course of refurbishing the premises a new roof was pitched over the whole building. The roof was a steel frame clear span construction with corrugated iron cladding and standing on new footings without internal uprights. Cost of materials for the roof was calculated by Coopers and Lybrand as $29,936.40 (T22). The building operations were staged so that the applicants continued to carry on business in the premises while the building work was being undertaken. The work was undertaken between December 1988 and March 1989.

(l) A new reinforced concrete floor was laid to replace the existing floor which was the source of moisture for white ants and required treatment. The original floor had in the main been of similar construction. Cost was determined as $25,716.75 (T22). Two new fuel storage tanks of 5000 capacity each were also installed under the new concrete floor.

(m) The original walls with various wooden claddings were replaced with brick and masonry walls at a basic cost of $31,520.00 with additional costs (T22). The masonry walls were erected because the Municipal Authority insisted on fireproof walls rather than replacement with cladded frame walls.

(n) A new and relocated toilet block was constructed in a more convenient location within the premises at a cost of $16,804.30 (T22).

(o) The layout of the premises retained its original character but some re-arrangement of the internal rooms took place with new partitions and new ceilings for the showroom, office and spare parts areas. External entrances were increased with the roller doors previously used for the main street entrances transferred to new side street entrances and new roller doors installed on the main street.

(p) New street access to the showroom was provided.

(q) Electrical installations were of necessity replaced to a similar standard and layout as had previously been the case.

(r) Ducted airconditioning from units installed on the roof replaced mobile units used previously.

(s) Because of the Municipal Authority’s requirements a new oil separator was installed.

(t) Exhibit 5 is a report by a valuer employed in the Australian Valuation Office made in January 1998 following an inspection on 7 January 1998. The report contains 20 photographs of the subject building which when compared with the photographs of the prerefurbished building in Exhibit 4 make a compelling impression of a new building.

(u) “Motor Garage (No 2) Pty Ltd” was incorporated on 13 August 1993, i.e. after the relevant time.


  1. The applicants submitted that there had been a continuity of interest in the premises since 1974 because H was a shareholder in Motor Garage (No 1) Pty Ltd since 1974 and he had managed the business on behalf of the company from that time. That submission is, however, inconsistent with the proved freehold holding of the premises which was in the ownership of H Senior and his wife until October 1988 when following a resubdivision of the land the relevant portion was transferred to the applicants.
  2. It is the essence of the applicants’ case that they only set out to repair the existing premises because of the storm damage and it was the eventual requirements of the Municipal Authority that caused them to undertake a lot more work than was their original intention.
  3. The applicants rely on the judgment of the High Court in W Thomas & Co v. FCT [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 14 ATD 78 at 87 where Windeyer J said:
“The works in question can all be fairly described as repairs to the building. They were done to make good a deterioration that had occurred by ordinary wear and tear or by the operation of natural causes during the passage of time. I was referred to various definitions of the word ‘repair’ and to a number of cases illustrative of its meaning. I need not discuss them. Cases arising between landlord and tenant are only of indirect assistance. And nothing much is to be gained by comparing the particular facts of other cases with the facts of this case. And understanding the concept of ‘repair’ is not much aided by contrasting that word with other words that in themselves gain only by contrast whatever precision of meaning they have in this field. The words ‘repair’ and

‘improvement’ may for some purposes connote contrasting concepts; but obviously repairing a thing improves the condition it was in immediately before repair. It may sometimes be convenient for some purposes to contrast a ‘repair’ with a ‘replacement’ or a ‘renewal’. But repairs to a whole are often made by the replacement of worn-out parts by new parts. Repair involves a restoration of a thing to a condition it formerly had without changing its character. But in the case of a thing considered from the point of view of its use as distinct from its appearance, it is restoration of efficiency in function rather than exact repetition of form or material that is significant. Whether or not work done upon a thing is aptly described as a repair of that thing is thus a question of fact and degree. But the answer to that question does not of itself decide whether the expenditure on the work is properly to be considered as an outgoing upon capital account or upon revenue account. And that is what must be decided when the question is whether that expenditure is an allowable deduction in the ascertainment of taxable income.

Expenditure upon repairs is properly attributed to revenue account when the repairs are for the maintenance of an income-producing capital asset. Maintenance involves the periodic repair of defects that are the result of normal wear and tear in operation. It is an expense of a revenue nature when it is to repair defects arising from the operations of the person who incurs it.”


  1. They also rely upon a decision of the Tribunal reported as Case V167 88 ATC 1107 which essentially followed the above quoted dicta of Windeyer J and the decision of the Court of Appeal in Morcom v Campbell-Johnson [1955] 3 All E.R. 264. In that case Denning LJ said he had difficulty in distinguishing between an improvement and a repair as required by the relevant legislation. After citing earlier decisions his Lordship formulated the text at page 266 as follows:
“It seems to me that the test, so far as one can give any test in these matters, is this: If the work which is done is the provision of something new for the benefit of the occupier, that is, properly speaking, an improvement; but if it is only the replacement of something already there, which has become dilapidated or worn out, then, albeit that it is a replacement by its modern equivalent, it comes within the category of repairs and not improvements.”


Hodson LJ at page 269 and Morris LJ at page 272 essentially agreed with the test formulated by Denning LJ.

  1. The respondent submits that the work carried out by the applicants did not constitute repairs within the terms of s 53 of the Act. The applicants constructed a new building albeit that it was of similar size and layout to the old building.
  2. As a second argument the respondent submits that the repairs were carried out as initial repairs having been carried out shortly after acquisition.
  3. As an alternative argument the respondent also submits that if the applicants were properly entitled to deductions then those deductions are properly claimed in the year of income ended 30 June 1989 when the expense was incurred.
  4. The overall impression I formed from the evidence is that the applicants constructed a new building. I accept that is not what they set out to do. They set out initially to make good the storm damage but the whole process of putting the building back into a viable condition eventually resulted in the staged construction of a new building.
  5. I have come to the conclusion that this is a classic Law Shipping case which falls squarely within the decision in Thomas (supra) and I am therefore bound by that decision. At pages 87-88 of the report (14 ATD) Windeyer J discussed the principle as follows:
“But if when a thing is bought for use as a capital asset in the buyer’s business it is not in good order and suitable for use in the way intended, the cost of putting it in order suitable for use is part of the cost of its acquisition, not a cost of its maintenance. The decision of the Court of Sessions in Law Shipping Co Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioners (1924) SC74; 12 Tax Cas. 621, is commonly cited as authority for that proposition. The principle is obvious without the need for any supporting authority. I may nevertheless quote as a clear exposition a passage from the judgment of Woodhouse, J. In the New Zealand case of Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v. A. B. Donald Limited (1965) 9 A.I.T.R. 501 at p.506. That case, like the Law Shipping Company’s Case, was concerned with the acquisition of a vessel. His Honour said:

‘When this general principle is applied to the present case the issue becomes one of deciding whether the expenditure upon the vessel is part of the company’s organization of capital in order to earn profits with her, or whether it arises from causes associated with the course of operations embarked upon for that purpose. In this regard nobody would doubt that to maintain such an asset which otherwise would deteriorate by its use in operations directed to produce income is a revenue charge. Work of this sort is done to preserve the asset, and following the work the character of the asset is left unchanged. But equally clearly the initial purchase of the asset involves an outlay of capital - it is part of the organization of capital by the taxpayer to enable income-producing activity to be carried out. How then must one label an expenditure which remedies some flaw in the asset existing at the time of purchase? To me there can be only one answer. To the extent that such initial defects are restored, the result is an improvement in the quality of the asset purchased, and, in my opinion, there has been an outlay of capital. To treat this outlay as a revenue loss is no more justified, in my opinion, than to treat the value of the improvement as an income gain. Defects may arise gradually over an extended period or develop from unexpected or sudden causes, but in so far as they have matured by the time of purchase by the new owner, they affect the qualify of the asset he has acquired. Any subsequent need to remove them must be regarded as a legacy inherited by him as part of his bargain. It follows, therefore, that I take the view that the restoration of defects in an asset cannot be classed as a revenue charge unless the defects have arisen out of the taxpayer’s application of that asset in his search for income and unless the work is limited to those defects and does not become enlarged into such a reconstruction that a permanent improvement in quality has been effected.’

Applying the principle to the present case it seems to me that the expenditure of £5,082 upon repairs upon the Berry Building in the year ended 31st October, 1961 was of a capital nature. It seems to me immaterial that when the taxpayer acquired the building it did not know of some of its defects, those in the basement floor for example. That means only that the cost of obtaining an asset suitable for its purpose was greater than had been expected. It is equally immaterial that some parts of the work of repair were done progressively as parts of the building were taken into actual use. Expenditure of a capital nature does not cease to be so because made for work the doing of which was spread over a period of weeks or months and paid for as it was done. It is perhaps possible that some part of the expenditure might perhaps on a more close analysis than the evidence permits be shown to contain elements that would, if they could be separated, be a justifiable charge to revenue account. There are always some difficulties in applying in a strict way the concept of repairs, as limited to those resulting from a taxpayer’s own operations, to work such as painting that is periodically done not merely to make good defects but also to prevent defects developing. A stitch in time is as much a repair as would be the nine it saves. And the cost of it may be in the same category as would the cost of nine. It may be too that the need for some of the work done on that building in the second half of 1961 was contributed to by the use that the taxpayer had made of it during the first half of that year. But I can see no basis for apportioning between capital and revenue the actual expenditure that was incurred on any of the items listed in the statement. Looked at as a whole, each was I consider expenditure of a capital nature. As Kitto, J. Said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Western Suburbs Cinemas Ltd [1952] HCA 28; (1952) 86 C.L.R. 102, at p.109; [1952] HCA 28; 9 A.T.D. 452, at p.456 ’if a total expenditure is of a capital nature, so is every part of it’.”


  1. On the material before me I am satisfied that the expenditure incurred in refurbishing the premises on or after 17 October 1988 was expenditure that comes precisely within the case decided in Thomas and I am bound to find that expenditure was of a capital nature.
  2. As to expenditure incurred before 17 October 1988 and after 30 June 1987 I am satisfied that expenditure incurred by the applicants for repairs to premises occupied by them in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income is an allowable deduction under s 53. I am also satisfied that the expenditure incurred prior to lodging the application with the Municipal Authority on 21 July 1988 could reasonably be inferred as being in the nature of repairs as explained by Denning LJ and set out above. I have come to that view because I am satisfied, and find, that the applicants originally set out to make good the storm damage to a building in which they carried on business. The initial work was undertaken for this purpose and should therefore be accepted as repairs that did not constitute improvements albeit that the damaged materials were replaced with their modern equivalents. At that time the applicants did not own the building.
  3. On the material before me I am satisfied that the outgoings for repairs effected during the year ended 30 June 1988 were not incurred in the course of refurbishing the building but rather should be characterised as outgoings of a non-capital nature incurred in repairing the building.
  4. Of the $21,962.00 claimed as a deduction for repairs in the return of income for the year of income ended 30 June 1988 the respondent has conceded $12,276.00 (T4). I have come to the conclusion that the balance of the claim is allowable provided the claim is substantiated.
  5. I would therefore allow the objection for the year of income ended 30 June 1988 in relation to the claim for repairs. There will be a consequential adjustment for the Division 10D deduction for depreciation which has been allowed ($549.00).
  6. I am not satisfied that the applicants have established that the assessments are excessive for the years of income ended 30 June 1989 to 30 June 1991. As already explained I am not satisfied that the refurbishment of the building in 1988/1989 can be correctly characterised as repairs to the building but even if the work can be so characterised then the Law Shipping principle as explained in Thomas applies to characterise the outgoings as being of a capital nature.
  7. The objection decisions relating to assessed taxable income for the years of income ended 30 June 1989, 30 June 1990 and 30 June 1991 will be affirmed.
  8. Little was said about the additional tax assessed, however, the issue was raised in the respondent's submissions. Counsel told me that the additional tax imposed by s 223 of the Act had been remitted to 20% plus a per annum interest factor. Those calculations may be seen at document T26. I understand the per annum component is part of the remission made under s 227.
  9. There is nothing before me that suggests that I should further remit the additional tax. The claims for repairs were made in circumstances that justified an explanation of the facts rather than a one line claim in the return of income of the partnership (T34).
  10. In Case 74/96 96 ATC 662 I discussed the meaning of “reckless” in the context of the Commissioner’s guidelines in IT2517. I adopt that discussion at paragraphs 24-30 for the purpose of deciding this case. That leads me to the conclusion that a further remission of additional tax imposed for the years ended 30 June 1989, 30 June 1990 and 30 June 1991 should not be made. The objection for the year ended 30 June 1988 is to be allowed.
  11. A Deputy Registrar made orders under Regulation 19(5) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Regulations that only one fee was payable on each applicant's applications. In these circumstances I will certify that these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant because I have allowed the objections for the 1988 year subject to the claimed outgoings being substantiated by the respondent's (or the Tribunal’s) satisfaction.
  12. Liberty to apply in relation to the 1988 year only will be reserved to both parties.

I certify that this and the ten (10) preceding pages are a true copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Associate
