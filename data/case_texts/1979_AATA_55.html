Hare and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 55 (11 May 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits -benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force - invalidity retirement on ground of condition specified in certificate - application for review of decision confirming determination of benefit entitlement under s. 66(2) of Superannuation Act 1976 - evidence of condition not specified in certificate causing invalidity - question arising as to disclosure of condition causing invalidity - consideration of scope of review of a "decision".

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 16, 66, 73, 154, 184;

Superannuation Act 1922 s. 5; Superannuation Amendment Act 1978 s. 77; Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ss. 25, 43



DECISION AND REASONS

Re: G. A. J. HARE

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

AAT Decision No 187

No. 78/12027

Mr R. K. Todd (Senior Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

Mr W. J. Smith (Member)

Date: 11 May 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

No: 78/12027

Re: G. A. J. HARE

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal decides to remit to the Commissioner for Superannuation the matter of the determination of the benefit entitlement of the applicant for re-consideration such re-consideration to take into account (i) the findings of fact and the reasons expressed in the accompanying Reasons for Decision and (ii) the question of the applicability of s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976.

The Tribunal further decides to reserve leave to the applicant and to the Commissioner for Superannuation to request that the Application for Review be brought on for further hearing or be otherwise considered.

DATED this 11th day of May, 1979

(R. K. Todd)

Senior Member

(Dr M. Glick)

Member

(W. J. Smith)

Member

REASONS FOR DECISION

This application for review is one in which the applicant seeks review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 27 February 1978, and confirmed upon reconsideration under s. 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976, that the benefit entitlement of the applicant on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 22 November 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Act. The decision was based upon an opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition, namely hypertension, specified in a benefit classification certificate which was deemed by s. 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to have been issued and which was still in force immediately before the retirement of the applicant.

The effect of the Commissioner's decision is that the applicant is entitled not to a pension, but under s. 73(l) of the Act to a lump sum benefit amounting to one half of the amount that was the amount per annum of the applicant's final annual rate of salary, that amount being greater than the amount that is three and a half times the applicant's accumulated basic contribution, his contributory service having been only a little over two years.

By way of introduction, where the question is, as here, one of the extent of the benefits available upon retirement having regard to s. 66 of the Superannuation Act 1976, it is necessary to understand that a person applying for a permanent position in the Public Service is medically examined and if he meets certain prescribed medical standards is accepted for membership of the Superannuation Scheme. A contributor coming in to the Public Service after the commencement of the "new" Superannuation Scheme on 1 July 1976 is affected first by the provisions of s. 16 of the Superannuation Act 1976. That section provides in ss. (2), (3) and (4) thereof as follows:

"16. (2) A person who proposes to become, or becomes, an eligible employee shall undergo such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner requires.

(3) A report or reports of the result or results of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified."

The effect of these provisions is seen when s. 66 comes to be considered when an eligible employee retires on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age. The question then arises whether there is in force in respect of the employee, immediately before his retirement, a benefit classification certificate. If there is, the final question is whether the incapacity which was the ground of the employee's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a condition connected with such a condition.

These provisions, however, would not be apt to cover the case of a person who, like the present applicant, was a contributor to the Provident Account pursuant to the provisions of the superseded Act, that is to say the Superannuation Act 1922. Broadly, the position under the superseded Act was that if a medical examination indicated that it was more likely than not that the employee would not attain his full retirement age, he would be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account, rather than to the Superannuation Fund. A further alternative was of course non-acceptance of the employee for the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account. As under the superseded Act there was no provision for the issue of a benefit classification certificate, it was necessary, when the 1976 legislation was passed, to give life to s. 66 in such a case, and to make some equivalent provision accordingly. This was sought to be achieved by s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976. This section provided, (subject to the pre-requisites contained in s. 184(2) (a), (b) and (c)) that "for the purposes of this Act ... there shall be deemed to be in force, on the commencing day,... a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under section 16(4) specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act".

Such a certificate is a notional concept, not an actual document, although it has been the practice of the Commissioner in a situation in which retirement occurs on the ground of invalidity, and a question of the applicability of s. 66(2) arises, in fact to issue an actual certificate purport. to reflect what in the view of the Commissioner should be the condition or conditions specified in the deemed certificate. It should be said that the difficulties arising from this cone are as ephemeral as they are real, for from 24 April 1978 s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976 was by s. 77 of the Superannuation Amendment Act 1978 repealed, and a new section substituted by the terms of which provision is made for the issue of an actual certificate with attendant provisions. Further reference to this repeal is made later in these reasons.

Having so explained the statutory background, we can now identify the problem in this case. It is undoubted that the applicant was an existing contributor who immediately before the commencing day (namely 1 July 1976) was a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act, that his period of contributory service on 1 July 1976 would have been less than 20 years if he had ceased to be an employee on that day, and that at the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976 he had not attained his maximum age. The requirements of s. 184(2) (a), (b) and (c) were satisfied, with the consequence that a benefit classification certificate was deemed to have been force on 1 July 1976. The questions therefore are, (1) what was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate and (2) what was the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, and was it caused or substantially contributed to by the condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a condition connected with such a condition or such conditions?

With this background, we can now turn to our findings of fact. Prior to 1970, the applicant, who was born in 1924, had been a contractor, originally as a painter. He had done work for the Victorian Public Works Department. Then he responded to calls to tender for repairs to war service homes and he won several contracts to repair houses that had been damaged by fire or had been neglected. He had been doing this for about 2 years when in 1970 he was offered a position as a works supervisor with War Service Homes (now Defence Service Homes Corporation). The position was a temporary one.

At this point the only comment to be made about the applicant's health is that in 1965 he had an operation for a peptic ulcer, at which stage his blood pressure was satisfactory. This was stated in evidence by Dr Bruce, a medical practitioner in a group practice which the applicant attended from about 1961.

In 1970, apparently soon after commencing with War Service Homes, the applicant was out making an inspection and was in the course of taking a ladder out of the boot of his car when another car that was going past sounded its horn. The applicant swung round with the ladder and injured his back. This caused him pain and distress but the more significant effect of it was that it seems to have triggered off a nervous reaction that came to bulk larger and larger in the applicant's mind to a point where he became much affected and eventually sought medical treatment. He had to drive many miles every week in the course of his duties and eventually it got to the stage at which, as he said:

"if a car tooted I sort of recalled this thing each time and I became very, very nervous driving, and yet I was getting more and more driving to do. "

The applicant had about a month off work on sick leave and workers compensation as a result of this incident. Asked whether the time off work was because of the back injury or because of his nervous condition, he answered:

"I think probably the nervous condition. I suffered the back because I thought there was nothing I could do about it. It was nerves and headaches which worried me far more than the back. The back was all right once I was in the car; it was getting in and getting out, and if someone tooted fairly close. "

The applicant's nervous condition was not helped by the general work situation. He gave us a good deal of detail about this. We do not propose to go into it. Suffice it to say that we accept that as a conscientious man the applicant was deeply troubled by various attitudes and procedures that developed at his work and by the way some senior men differed and quarrelled, in one case shouting through a partition at each other while seated at their respective desks, and then, in the case of the loser of some particular verbal battle, taking out his feelings on some lower grade staff member. The result was that the applicant became more and more depressed. Later, he said that he felt that he was going mad, but at what point his condition really deteriorated it is hard to say.

Dr Bruce's evidence, while in general very clear, left us in some doubt as to when the applicant commenced to receive treatment for depression. At the very latest, Dr Bruce's associate Dr Hamilton prescribed concordin on 9 May 1974, but it seems likely that concordin was also prescribed in March 1973 by another doctor in the practice. The patient was said to be depressed and "after that time it was fairly frequently repeated so he was obviously having the medication for some time... Certainly for 12 months by the look of the card". Indeed the records suggested that Dr Hamilton wrote that he was "depressed, etc." in September 1972. This accords with the applicant's own evidence, which was that the treatment with concordin started "about '72". After describing the incident with the car and the ladder he was asked and replied:

"Q. And this nervous condition then got worse after that?--A. It did worsen, yes.

Q. Did you have treatment for that nervous condition over that period?--A. The concordin, I was told, were tablets to quieten my nerves.

Q. Your treatment with those commenced... ?--A. About '72 that would be".

It follows, we think, that the applicant was subject to a nervous condition which had its origins in the 1970 incident and which by 1972 or at least 1973 was identified by him as one requiring treatment and which was treated by his doctors, although we should add that the dose prescribed was "pretty small by today's standards", as Dr Bruce put it.

From 1973 new staff that were taken on in the applicant's department entered as permanent members of the public service. After a while the applicant was the only "temporary" left. He eventually raised the question of permanency and after a substantial delay he was invited to apply accordingly. There is some doubt about dates. The Notice of Separation indicates that the date of his permanent appointment was 29 August 1974. At all events he went for a medical examination in February 1975, the examining doctor being a Dr Bridges. In answering the questions set out in the Medical Report Form under the heading "Personal" the applicant answered the first group of questions as follows:

"Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions? Answer 'Yes' or 'No' in space provided.

1(a) Nervous fatigue No

(b) Mental or nervous conditions No

(c) Anxiety state No

(d) Neurasthenia No

(e) Fits No

(f) Epilepsy No

(g) Persistent headaches No

(h) Dizziness or turns No

(i) Attack of unconsciousness or weakness No

(j) Migraine Yes

(k) Disturbance of sensation or muscular activity No

(l) Persistent breathlessness No

Dr Bridges took blood pressure readings of 190/120 and after rest 180/120, and recommended that the applicant be rejected. The reasons were stated as follows:

"The applicant is severely hypertensive and according to the Act should be rejected. It is considered that with treatment a working span of at least 7 years could be anticipated. The applicant has been told to report to his private doctor so that the hypertension can be treated. Consideration may be given to deferment of this examination for six months. If the examination is then favourable a decision would be necessary regarding the acceptance to the Provident Fund of a controlled hypertensive under therapy. "

This recommendation came under the view of a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Delaine, on 12 March 1975. He noted that the application should be deferred for 11 months "view Provident".

As recommended by Dr Bridges, the applicant went to his own doctor for treatment of the hypertension that had not, apparently, been previously identified. On 21 March 1975 the reading was 160/110, on 9 April 1975 165/100 and in August 1975 there were readings of 130/80 and 140/80 so that apparently the treatment was having an effect. He was at that time under treatment with aldomet and chlotride. In fact the applicant was seen by Dr Delaine in August 1975, who made the following notation and recommendation:

"BP 160/112. Massive doses of medication. Suitable for Provident Account. "

In view of the history set out above, it is undoubted that the condition "hypertension" was specified in a benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force on 1 July 1976 in respect of the applicant, and that it was the only such condition so specified by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act to the Superannuation Fund.

We have already set out the sequence of events in relation to the applicant's nervous troubles. It will be apparent that they commenced well before the beginning of 1975. He was being treated for them in 1973 or possibly 1972. They had their origins in the incident in 1970. But the applicant did not mention them when he answered the questionnaire, or when he saw Dr Bridges, or Dr Delaine, in 1975. We shall have to return to this topic later.

The applicant's difficulties with his work situation increased as time went by, until we come to the critical time in the latter half of 1977. Dr Bruce saw a lot of him at that time. In early August there was a blood pressure reading of 160/100. Then the applicant had two weeks off work for which Dr Bruce gave him a certificate. Dr Bruce noted:

"22 August: Depressed, unable to cope with work. Started off but could not face going to work. "

He saw him a week after that and his blood pressure had settled to 140/90. He was still off work. Dr Bruce said that "he was really not too bad in himself and I urged him to in fact go back to work. He was very depressed at the time. " On 5 September he was still off work and still depressed but his blood pressure reading was quite acceptable at 130/90. Dr Bruce noted:

"Patient says unable to go to work".

In September he noted:

"Has been off work for six weeks. Now feeling much better. Blood pressure 130/80. May go back to work".

Dr Bruce was not sure whether he did go back to work: "There were a number of times when I said that I felt he ought to go back to work and I felt that there was nothing really to stop him going back to work from his blood pressure point of view but he just felt he could not cope with the work, whatever the work environment was". He was taking aldomet and chlotride through this time "but in spite of the treatment remaining pretty well the same there were large fluctuations in his blood pressure for which there was no very good reason other than that perhaps we do see with patients under stress that their blood pressure does fluctuate and if we rest them then after it seems to settle down".

Dr Bruce's hopes were not however realized, and he proceeded to recommend that his patient seek retirement. De Delaine saw him on 14 September 1977, and his assessment was that the applicant should be retired. An extract from his report is as follows:

"Brief History of Disabilities Since early 1975 under constant medical care for hypertension, nervous tension and persistent headaches. Currently takes Aldom Navidrex and Concordin PRN when necessary. For the last 2 years finds it more difficult to cope with duties.

Clinical Notes on Examination Reports: Irritability, poor concentration, intolerance, critical attitude to co-workers and others, persistent headaches even when driving a car. In sum nervous tension probably stemming from hypertension and vice-versa. After 6 weeks holiday and? after medication - BP now 150/100.

Prognosis: Condition most unsettled – will probably deteriorate with age. "

Dr Delaine explained that his diagnosis was that the applicant suffered from hypertension which had been controlled to a degree by aldomet and that being a man then aged 53 years and hypertensive for a considerable period of time and having regard to the available data, particularly that relating to the diastolic blood pressure, the conclusion was, not knowing the history of the past, that the nervous tension could be due to "early arteriosclerotic brain syndrome". On this basis he had later, when asked by the Commissioner for Superannuation in July 1978 to allot the cause of retirement as between hypertension and nervous tension on a percentage basis, assessed the hypertension as contributing to the extent of 70 per cent and severe nervous tension as contributing to the extent of 30 per cent. Dr Delaine had concluded that the treatment of the applicant by his local doctor had concentrated upon the hypertension, but this seems to us to be contrary to Dr Bruce's evidence. This is not to say that Dr Delaine's conclusions were unreasonable having regard to the evidence which he had before him, but at the end we are persuaded that we should give full weight to the opinion of Dr Bruce as the doctor who had the applicant under long-time, continuous and obviously very sympathetic care. Dr Bruce said that he had always been of the opinion that the applicant's anxious personality and the tension resulting from his employment had been:

"more significant causes for his frequent attendance in seeking our advice. I think that really sums up Mr Hare as far as I see him as a family doctor. His hypertension has been something which has been present but I do not think it has been the cause of his incapacity and I do not really think it has interfered with him being able to do his work as he normally would like to have done it. I think there were probably other much more significant factors which were the cause of what has happened ... I really do believe that given a different individual with a different make-up and the same level of hypertension he would have been quite happy to keep on working until his ultimate retirement at 65 years of age. That is really my opinion. "

After having heard Dr Delaine's evidence he did not change that opinion:

"... after being employed by the Commonwealth Government it appears that these tensions related, as he claims, to his work... he did seem to be agitated about his ability to perform what was expected of him, and I do not really believe that these were related to his hypertension. I believe that his hypertension was about the same - not very well controlled unfortunately.... I believe that it was related to the type of work that he was perhaps temperamentally unsuited to perform. I would not agree ... that... it was in fact a kind of organic hypertensive brain syndrome... In fact I find him very much better since he has stopped employment; he is more relaxed, he requires less medication and I find him a completely different person, and I can only attribute that in retrospect to being the right decision on our behalf to recommend that he retire... "

On the basis of the evidence of Dr Bruce, we are of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was severe nervous tension. We are unable to conclude that this was caused by the condition hypertension that was specified in the benefit classification certificate, nor do we think in the light of the evidence given in this particular case that the nervous tension was substantially contributed to by the hypertension. It had its own causes and was nourished by the inability of the applicant's personality to cope with a very difficult situation at work. The great improvement in the applicant's condition since his retirement lends strong support to the view that his nervous disorder was the predominant if not the sole reason for his invalidity. We are of the opinion that the nervous tension was not "connected with" the hypertension in terms of the concluding words of s. 66(2)(c) of the Act of 1976. We think that such a connection must be a real connection, demonstrable upon the evidence and not upon the basis of a supposition of a connection made likely or possible by experience of other cases.

We might add that we think that the opinion which the Commissioner formed was a proper one at the time having regard to the then available information. The same may be said of the medical judgement formed by Dr Delaine. But they both must in our opinion bow to Dr Bruce's evidence, given at the hearing.

Without more we would feel obliged simply to set aside the decision of the Commissioner that the entitlement of the applicant should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Act of 1976. But it was submitted by the representative of the Commissioner that in the proved circumstances the provisions of s. 184(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 should apply. That sub-section should be set out in full:

"(3) Where -

(a) an eligible employee who is an existing contributor (whether or not he was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act) has ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he had not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) a benefit classification certificate was not, by virtue of sub-section (2), deemed to have been in force in respect of him on the commencing day or, if a benefit classification certificate was, by virtue of sub-section (2), deemed to have been in force in respect of him on the commencing day -

(i) the certificate was not in force in respect of him at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee; or

(ii) the certificate was in force in respect of him at the time but there was not specified in the certificate the physical or mental condition or conditions which, in the opinion of the Commissioner caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement on the ground of invalidity; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied -
(i) that, at or in connection with a medical examination which the person was required to undergo by virtue of section 5 of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished false information, he would, immediately before the commencing day, have been a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act or, if he was, immediately before that day a contributor to the Provident Account, there would, by virtue of sub-section (2), be deemed to have been in force in respect of him at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee a benefit classification certificate in which the physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in paragraph (c), or a physical or mental condition connected with that condition or those conditions, would have been deemed to have been specified,
the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate in which the condition or conditions referred to in paragraph (c) is or are specified and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been in force in respect of that person at the time of his death or retirement. "

It seems clear enough, in view of the findings of fact that we have made, that the provisions of sub-clauses (a), (b) and (c) of s. 184(3) have been satisfied, and that the medical examination which the applicant attended on 27 February 1975 was one which he was required to undergo by virtue of s. 5 of the Superannuation Act of 1922 (the superseded Act). For the Commissioner, it was contended that it was open, on the evidence set out above, to conclude further that the applicant had failed to furnish information required to be furnished by him, namely that he then had, or had at least previously had, a mental or nervous condition or possibly a condition of anxiety state. This being so, he having been immediately before the commencing day a contributor to the Provident Account, if the Commissioner were satisfied that if he had not failed to furnish such information there would have been deemed to have been in force in respect of him at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee a benefit classification certificate in which the physical or mental condition which caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement on the ground of invalidity, or a condition connected with that condition would have been deemed to have been specified, the Commissioner would be required to issue a benefit classification certificate accordingly specifying the condition in question.

In fact the Commissioner's representative, as we understood his submission, contended that if the Tribunal were satisfied that the requirements of s. 184(3) had been met it could itself exercise the power of the Commissioner under that sub-section to issue a certificate. It is, however, quite clear to us that this is not so. The decision which is the subject of this application for review is the decision of the Commissioner that the benefit entitlement of the applicant should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976. That decision was reconsidered pursuant to the provisions of s. 154, and became subject to the processes of review provided for by s. 154 itself, and by the provisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, notably by s. 25 thereof. But the provisions of s. 43(l) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act must be noted:

"43. (1) For the purpose of reviewing a decision, the Tribunal may exercise all the powers and discretions that are conferred by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision and shall make a decision in writing -

(a)affirming the decision under review;
(b)varying the decision under review; or
(c)setting aside the decision under review and -

(i) making a decision in substitution for the decision so set aside; or

(ii) remitting the matter for reconsideration in accordance with any directions or recommendations of the Tribunal. "

It is true that once invoked this section confers upon the Tribunal power to exercise "all the powers and discretions that are conferred... on the person who made the decision.. ", but this, is so only "For the purpose of reviewing a decision". Thus unless a decision has in fact been made, there is no scope for review and thus no scope for assumption by the Tribunal of the powers and discretions conferred on the person who made the decision. It is worth referring to an important discussion of this topic in the decision of the President of the Tribunal, Brennan J., in Re Lawlor 1 A. L. D. 167 in a passage commencing, significantly enough, as follows:

"The Tribunal is not a primary administrator. It is not the original repository of powers and discretions under an enactment. "

The power to issue a benefit classification certificate pursuant to s. 184(3)(d) is a power conferred upon the Commissioner and is not in our opinion one that can be exercised by the Tribunal unless and until the decision to exercise it has first been made by the primary decision maker and the decision then subjected to the available processes of review. We therefore decline even to embark upon the question of the applicability of s. 184(3)(d). In our opinion the only proper course is to remit the matter to the Commissioner for his consideration.

The situation that thus emerges is admittedly not altogether satisfactory. Various considerations must be borne in mind. The Tribunal should not in any way pre-empt or impede the consideration by the Commissioner, in the light of the evidence, of the applicability of s. 184(3). To proceed now to set aside his decision upon the question the subject of the application for review might have the effect of unfairly creating rights during an interim period in the course of which consideration is being given by the Commissioner to the applicability of s. 184(3). It would be quite wrong for the applicant's entitlement to be considered other than by reference to all of the provisions which, in the light of the facts now proved, might be seen to be possibly applicable.. We think that in all the circumstances the proper course is to withhold our formal decision upon the question whether the decision of the Commissioner that the benefit entitlement of the applicant should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 was correct, while expressing our clear opinion that it was not. We propose to remit the question of the applicant's benefit entitlements to the Commissioner so as to permit the same to be reconsidered. In the course of such reconsideration it will be possible to take into account the findings of fact and the reasons expressed herein, and the question of the applicability of s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976. In the meantime the hearing of the Application for Review should stand adjourned, with leave being reserved to the applicant and to the Commissioner to request that the application be brought on for further hearing or be otherwise considered further. Should the Commissioner come to the conclusion that there is no factual ground for the application of s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976 or that the same is in any event of no force or effect having regard to what we say below, it would be necessary then for the Tribunal to make its formal decision in terms of the opinion hereinbefore expressed. We have considered whether some time limit might be imposed within which the Commissioner's further consideration of the matter should be required to be completed. We feel confident however that the matter will in all the circumstances be considered expeditiously, and prefer to leave the position as one in which it can simply be re-listed for hearing if a decision is not forthcoming within a reasonable time.

We should add that it was not suggested at the hearing that the provisions of the new s. 184 as substituted by s. 77 of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 could have application, and it would be undesirable for us to canvass this point in any way. In what we have said we emphasize that in these reasons we have been speaking of s. 184 of the Superannuation Act 1976 as it stood prior to its repeal. Indeed, we are bound to say, although the point was not referred to in argument and indeed in view of the comments made above had no occasion to be argued, that it has occurred to us that one matter that would need to be considered by the Commissioner would be whether in view of its repeal, effected by s. 77 of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978, s. 184(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 has ceased to confer any power upon him to issue a benefit classification certificate in the manner suggested.
