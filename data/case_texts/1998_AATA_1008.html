Rydell Australia Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation [1998] AATA 1008; (1998) 40 ATR 1197; 99 ATC 2050 (25 November 1998)

Last Updated: 17 September 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT97/205-207

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION
)

RYDELL AUSTRALIA PTY LTD

Applicant
And

Tribunal
Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member


Date 25 November 1998

Decision
The Tribunal varies the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent with a direction to amend assessments of income tax based on the applicant's income of the years ended 30 June 1991 and 30 June 1992 in accordance with the reasons for decision.
Liberty to apply to the Tribunal is given in the event of any dispute between the parties as to the calculation of assessable income.



........(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe..................

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - interest on funds borrowed - security of life policies - whether an allowable deduction - whether funds borrowed for purpose of carrying on business - assessment of beneficiary of trust - whether assessable income in accordance with section 97 of the Act

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Glenn & Anor v Commissioner of Taxation 97 ATC 2144

Case 14 98 ATC 201

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v J D Roberts and Smith 92 ATC 4380

Zeta Force Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4681

REASONS FOR DECISION



25 November 1998
Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member




1. These are applications to review the decision of the respondent to disallow in part objections to amended assessments of income tax based on the applicant’s income for the years ended 30 June 1991 and 1992. The amended assessments were issued to increase assessable income derived as a beneficiary of two discretionary trusts which, in turn, derived income from the holding of units in a unit trust. The increase in assessable income resulted from a decision of the respondent that certain amounts of interest paid by the unit trust were not an allowable deduction. The objections had been allowed in part and the dispute centred primarily on the question of deductibility of the interest.

2. At the hearing the applicant, Rydell Australia Pty Ltd (“Rydell Australia”) was represented by Mr Halperin, a barrister and solicitor, and the respondent by an officer of the respondent. Evidence was given by Mr Over and Mr Glenn, the two directors of the trustee company of the unit trust, Rydell Holdings Pty Ltd (“Rydell Holdings”).

3. Rydell Holdings as trustee of the Rydell Unit Trust carries on a business of importing, manufacturing and distributing industrial belting. On or about 15 December 1986 Mr Glenn and Mr Over individually took out life insurance policies with the Prudential Assurance Company Ltd (“Prudential”) on their own lives for a sum assured of $1 million. The annual premiums were $24,370 and $25,290 respectively and this total amount of $49,660 was paid each year thereafter by Rydell Holdings. These policies were described as “shareholder protection policies” and were said to have been taken out to provide funds to enable the survivor of the principals to acquire the interest in the business on the death of the other. There appears to have been some confusion as to the ownership of the policies. Statements filed by the applicant initially described the policies as having been taken out by Rydell Holdings on the lives of each of the directors. If the purpose of the policies was as stated, it might have been expected that each of the principals would have taken out a policy on the life of the other.

4. The premiums on the policies were due in December each year and were so paid in 1986, 1987, 1988, 1989 and 1990. No premium was paid in December 1991 and the policies were surrendered on 27 February 1992 with that outstanding premium deducted from the surrender proceeds. Commencing on 12 January 1988, after the second premium on each policy was paid, funds were borrowed against the security of the policies. In each of January 1989, January 1990 and December 1990 further funds were borrowed against the policies. Interest both paid and accrued on these loans was claimed as a deduction in the return of income of Rydell Unit Trust. The funds borrowed were deposited to the credit of Rydell Holdings bank account.

5. On or about 21 December 1988, Rydell Holdings took out life assurance policies with Prudential on the lives of the two directors, Mr Glenn and Mr Over, with the sum assured in each case of $3 million. These were described as “Keyman insurance policies”. The annual premiums were $78,780 and $81,840 respectively. A Deed of Agreement bearing the date of 15 December 1988 was prepared between each of the directors and Rydell Holdings. The recital to the agreement stated that the company wished to provide for the loss it may suffer in the event of the death of the employee/applicant and that the employee wished to provide for the welfare of himself and dependants. The agreement then provided for the company to effect a life policy on the life of the employee and to hold it upon trust for the employee under the terms of the agreement. The company was to be liable for that portion of the premium which would be payable under a separate policy of term insurance providing identical cover equal to the sum assured on the life of the employee to his normal retirement date. The employee was liable to reimburse the company for the balance of the premium. If the employee died before the normal retirement date, the company was to be entitled to an amount equal to the sum assured and the employee’s estate entitled to the balance of the policy proceeds. The policy was to vest solely in the employee on the normal retirement date or on earlier termination of the agreement by consent, termination of employment or failure of either party to make payments required by the agreement. There is some doubt whether the agreements were executed but minutes and correspondence with Prudential referring to the policies as “Split Dollar Insurance” and setting out calculations of the expected costs and benefits between the company and the director appear to make it clear that the intentions of all parties were in accordance with the terms of the agreements. In addition, these same policies were the centre of previous applications to the Tribunal (Glenn & Anor v Commissioner of Taxation 97 ATC 2144). In that case it was accepted by the applicants that such agreements were in force and the only dispute was the quantum of bonus assessable to the applicants under section 26H of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). However, notwithstanding this, Rydell Holdings appears to have capitalised the premiums in its balance sheet as investment in life assurance policies. The first premiums were paid on 21 December 1988 and then on 29 December 1989 and 17 December 1990. These policies were surrendered on 25 February 1992 and the outstanding premium due in December 1991 deducted from the proceeds. On 4 January 1990 and 18 December 1990 Rydell Holdings borrowed funds against these policies. The amounts borrowed were similar in amount to the annual premiums. Interest both paid and accrued on these loans was also claimed as a deduction in the return of income of the Rydell Unit Trust.

6. The respondent disallowed as a deduction to Rydell Unit Trust a proportion of the interest on the loans from Prudential on the four policies of life assurance. The basis of the disallowance was that the respondent accepted that loans taken against the security of the policies prior to 1 July 1989 were used entirely for the purposes of gaining or producing assessable income. Consequently, interest on further loans was apportioned on the ratio of loans to 1 July 1989 over total loans with the proportion applicable to post 1 July 1989 loan disallowed. Total interest on loans against the Keyman insurance policies were disallowed. The applicant did not dispute the calculations but argued that the principles behind the calculations were wrong. The amounts disallowed were $22,263 in the year ended 30 June 1991 and $37,455 in the year ended 30 June 1992. Initially, the respondent had disallowed interest for the year ended 30 June 1990 but allowed the objection in full on the basis that section 170 of the Act precluded the issue of amended assessments for that year. These amounts disallowed were included in the assessable income of Rydell Australia as an increase in the net income of the two unit holding discretionary trusts of which it was a beneficiary. It will be necessary later to consider the basis of the inclusion of this income pursuant to section 97 of the Act.

7. It was accepted by both the applicant and the respondent that only the amount of the premiums on the Keyman insurance policies relevant to the risk proportion which would produce a fixed sum to Rydell Holdings on the death of a director was deductible. The balance of premiums on the Keyman insurance policies and the whole of the premiums on the so-called shareholder protection policies was not deductible as being of a capital nature. The respondent took the view that the funds were borrowed to finance the payment of the premiums in the two relevant years and that the interest on the loans was non-deductible. The reason for non-allowance of the proportion relevant to the deductible amount of the premiums escapes me.

8. It was the primary submission of the applicant that the funds borrowed were not for the purpose of paying premiums on the life policies but for use as general working capital in the business of the Rydell Unit Trust. It was argued that in all cases the payment of the premiums preceded the borrowings and that the funds borrowed were used to either re-finance earlier borrowings, pay recurrent outgoings of the business, repay a commercial bill or to invest in an interest bearing bank investment account used as a working capital reserve.

9. At the hearing, both parties appeared to accept an approach that interest incurred on funds borrowed and utilised in meeting non-deductible expenditure was itself non-deductible. Consequently, submissions at that time concentrated solely on the question of whether the funds borrowed were directly attributable to expenditure on life assurance premiums. Subsequent to the hearing, the Tribunal requested that the parties provide submissions on whether it necessarily followed that, if the Tribunal was to find against the applicant on its primary submission, the interest in dispute was non-deductible. Submissions were requested also on the question of whether the amended assessments correctly reflected the provisions of section 97 of the Act. In the subsequent submission the respondent submitted that, to the extent that funds were borrowed to meet the cost of the shareholder protection policies and the investment proportion of the Keyman insurance policies, the expenditure had no business purpose but applied the funds for the private or domestic purposes of the two directors. For the applicant, it was submitted that interest will always be of a revenue nature and should be deductible whether or not the borrowings were used for the purpose of non-deductible expenditure. In the alternative, it was submitted that the investment proportion of the premiums on the Keyman policies was an investment of the unit trust in the course of conducting its business. Finally, and as a further alternative, it was submitted that the non-deductible premiums were incurred as a relevant aspect of employment of the two directors and the funding of such premiums was incidental and relevant to the conduct of the business.

10. There are three issues to be considered in this case. The first is a consideration of the purpose of borrowing the funds on which interest was paid and the use to which such funds were put. The second issue is the deductibility of the interest on such borrowings. The third issue is whether to the extent that any such interest is non-deductible, the applicant has been correctly assessed on the additional net income of the unit trust.

11. Dealing with the first issue, I am in no doubt that the funds borrowed from Prudential were directly referable to the payment of premiums on the four life assurance policies. In all cases, within a matter of days after payment of the second annual premium, Rydell Holdings borrowed back from the insurance company in cash a net amount closely approximating the amount of the premium. Copies of correspondence by the insurance agent at the time of taking out the Keyman policies appear to make it clear that borrowed funds to meet the cost of premiums was intended from the outset. Whilst accepting that the borrowings were made after payment of premiums and not before, I have no doubt that this was done deliberately in order to allow the argument that the expenditure of borrowed funds was on items other than the premiums. However, the close proximity and the regularity of the borrowings allow me to find that the borrowings were made to replace funds expended on the life assurance premiums. In an earlier decision relating to a claim for deduction of interest and reported as Case 14 98 ATC 201, I summarised several of the decisions of the Courts and said (at page 204):

“It is clear from the many decisions of the Courts that, in determining the essential character of an interest outgoing, it is necessary to look to the uses to which the borrowed funds were put.”

In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v JD Roberts and Smith 92 ATC 4380, Hill J said (at page 4388):

“As the cases, including Kidston, all show, the characterisation of interest borrowed will generally be ascertained by reference to the objective circumstances of the use to which the borrowed funds were put. However, a rigid tracing of funds will not always be necessary or appropriate.”

In this case, I am satisfied that the use to which the borrowed funds were put in the two years in question was in the payment of the premiums on the four life assurance policies and repayment of earlier borrowings from the same source and for the same purpose.

12. In considering the issue of the deductibility of the interest, it is appropriate to consider each of the two categories of policies separately. The shareholder protection policies were taken out and owned by the two directors for the stated purpose of providing funds in the event of the death of either for the purchase of equity in the business by the survivor. They were acquired primarily for the provision of financial security to the two controllers/owners of the business in that capacity. They were not relevant to the ongoing business operated by Rydell Holdings. In turn, borrowings against those policies could be and were made only to the owners of the policies; the two individuals. While the proceeds of the loan were paid into Rydell Holdings’ bank account and the premiums on the policies paid out of that bank account, it must be said that both transactions were on behalf of the individuals not the unit trust. As such, any interest on such borrowings was not incurred in carrying on the business of the unit trust. For the applicant it was submitted that, if the borrowings were made by the individuals as owners of the policies, the funds were on-lent on similar terms to the unit trust and the payment of the premiums was, effectively, a form of remuneration to the two employees. I do not accept either of these views. They were simply transactions by the unit trust on behalf of Messrs Glenn and Over in their capacity as controllers of the equity in the unit trust and neither the premiums on the policies nor the interest on funds borrowed to finance the payment of the premiums was an allowable deduction. The respondent has allowed a proportion of the interest relevant to pre-1 July 1989 loans and it was not suggested that this was incorrect.

13. On the other hand, the Keyman insurance policies were taken out and owned by Rydell Holdings in its capacity as trustee of the unit trust. Notwithstanding submissions to the contrary on behalf of the applicant, I am satisfied that Rydell Holdings assigned the benefit of the investment portion of the policies to the two directors under the so-called Split Dollar Insurance arrangement and the individuals were entitled to that benefit. However, I am clear, also, that the assignment and the continuing payment of the full premium by Rydell Holdings was in connection with the individuals as directors and key employees. Whilst the proportion of the premium relevant to the investment portion of the policy has not been claimed or allowed as a deduction, I am prepared to find that the whole of the arrangements in relation to these policies was relevant to the business carried on by the unit trust and had a business purpose. As such I am then prepared to find that the interest on funds borrowed to finance such premiums was an allowable deduction. From the Audit Case Report included as document T6 in the documents filed by the respondent under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the amounts of interest disallowed in relation to these loans were $11,286 in the year ended 30 June 1991 and $25,901 in the year ended 30 June 1992.

14. As indicated earlier, the respondent included the whole of the amounts disallowed as a deduction in the returns of Rydell Unit Trust as additional net income assessable to the applicant under section 97 of the Act. The two unit holders in the unit trust were discretionary trusts, Russell Glenn Family Trust (“GFT”) and Rex Over Family Trust (“OFT”). The applicant, Rydell Australia, is a beneficiary of both discretionary trusts. In each of the years in question the Rydell Unit Trust accumulated $500 and distributed the balance of income in accordance with units held. The trustee of each of GFT and OFT resolved in each of the years to accumulate $500 and distribute specific amounts of income to individual beneficiaries with the balance of income to Rydell Australia.

15. This is another case where the correct application of section 97 of the Act needs consideration. Most recently, the way in which the section operates was canvassed in depth by Sundberg J in Zeta Force Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4681. After consideration of many prior decisions of the Courts, His Honour said (at page 4693):

“The weight of authority thus supports the opinion I have expressed under the heading ‘that share of the net income of the trust estate’, that the proportionate method is that contemplated by s 97.”

Section 97(1) provides, so far as material, that:

“Where a beneficiary of a trust estate who is not under any legal disability is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate —

(a) the assessable income of the beneficiary shall include—

(i) ...that share of the net income of the trust estate...”

At page 4686 His Honour said:

“The words ‘income of the trust estate’ in the opening part of s 97(1) refer to distributable income, that is to say income ascertained by the trustee according to appropriate accounting principles and the trust instrument. That the words have this meaning is confirmed by the use elsewhere in Div 6 of the contrasting expression ‘net income of the trust estate’. The beneficiary’s ‘share’ is his share of the distributable income.

Having identified the share of the distributable income to which the beneficiary is presently entitled, s 97(1) requires one to ascertain ‘that share of the net income of the trust estate’. That share is included in the beneficiary’s assessable income. The expression ‘net income of the trust estate’ in par (a)(i) has the meaning given it by s 95(1)—taxable income as opposed to distributable income. The words ‘that share’ in par (a)(i) refer back to the word ‘share’ in the expression ‘a share of the income of the trust estate’, and indicate that the same share is to be applied to an income amount calculated according to a different formula (taxable income as opposed to distributable income). Since the income amount may differ according to which formula is applied, the natural meaning to give to ‘share’ where it appears for the second time is ‘proportion’ rather that ‘part’ or ‘portion’. When Parliament wanted to convey the latter meaning, as it did in ss 99 and 99A, it used the word ‘part’.

The contrast between the expressions ‘share of the income of the trust estate’ and ‘that share of the net income of the trust estate’ shows that the draftsman has sought to relate the concept of present entitlement to distributable income, and not to taxable income, which is, after all, an artificial tax amount. Once the share of the distributable income to which the beneficiary is presently entitled is worked out, the notion of present entitlement has served its purpose, and the beneficiary is to be taxed on that share (or proportion) of the taxable income of the trust estate.”

It is clear, therefore, that the starting point in arriving at the assessable income of a beneficiary is the trust instrument, accounts of the trust and the resolution of the trustee so as to determine the share of the income of the trust estate to which the beneficiary is presently entitled. That proportionate share is then applied to the net income of the trust estate under section 95 of the Act. It is only that proportionate share over which the trustee has any control, not the quantum of assessable income. It is necessary therefore, to ascertain the share of distributable income for trust law purposes, if any, to which the applicant was entitled in each of the relevant years. This share is then to be applied to the net income under section 95.

16. It is not possible for me to calculate with any certainty the correct assessable income of the applicant in the two years in question as all of the relevant details are not contained in the information provided to the Tribunal. In the submission filed subsequent to the hearing, the respondent accepted the proportionate method as set out in the Zeta Force (supra) decision but appeared to assume that the relevant shares of income in the two discretionary trusts were those set out in the returns of income lodged. This is clearly not the case. For instance, it does seem clear that the applicant was not entitled to any share of the distributable income of either GFT or OFT in the year ended 30 June 1992. Consequently, it can have no share in the net income for taxation purposes in that year. The subsequent submission for the applicant included schedules analysing the distributions in each year. However, for the 1992 year the schedule showed $100,248 accumulated income retained by the Rydell Unit Trust. A perusal of the accounts of the trust indicates that the bulk of this amount represented capital profits and there is no indication that anything in excess of $500 of income otherwise available to beneficiaries was retained by the trustee.

17. In the circumstances, it is appropriate to remit these applications to the respondent with a direction to amend the relevant assessments of the applicant for the years ended 30 June 1991 and 1992 in accordance with this decision and reasons. It is appropriate that the parties have liberty to apply to the Tribunal in the event of any dispute in relation to the calculation of assessable income.
