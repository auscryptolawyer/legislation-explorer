REASONS FOR DECISION



9 December 1999
J. Block, Senior Member




  1. The objection decision in this matter is the disallowance by the Respondent of an objection dated 23 May 1997 against an assessment issued on 21 April 1997 in respect of the year ended 30 June 1996 (“Relevant Year”).
  2. Mr B R Pape of counsel, instructed by Abbott Tout Solicitors, appeared for the Applicant, while Messrs C J Bevan and A Iuliano of counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor, appeared for the Respondent.
  3. (a) The Tribunal received the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 together with the exhibits referred to in sub-clause (c) below.

(b) The matter was heard confidentially over 3 days, and being 1 September 1999, and 27 and 28 October 1999. The transcript of each hearing day commences with page 1; accordingly references to the transcript (“TS”) are prefaced by “1” in the case of the hearing on 1 September 1999, and “27” or “28”, as appropriate, in the case of the subsequent hearings. The amount of $122,874.75 consists of two components and being $100,890.75 paid to the Applicant in cash in the Relevant Year (and which is referred to as the “relevant amount”) and $21,957 which was referred to during the hearings as the ‘timing difference’ (“timing difference amount”). (2) Clauses 37.8, 37.9 and 37.10 are entitled “Retiring Allowances”, “Commutation” and “After-Tax Value Payment” respectively. Those three clauses (collectively referred to in these Reasons as the “retirement allowance provisions”) are related and are of such importance that they are set out in full in these Reasons as follows:



“37.8 Retiring Allowances

37.8.1 As and from the Commencement Date retirement payments and payments (if any) made in respect of funding present or future liabilities for retirement allowances (including entitlements to Consultancy Arrangements) for each Profit Centre will be borne by that Profit Centre.

37.8.2 The retirement provisions (including entitlements to Consultancy arrangements) of the Partners of the Constituent Firms existing as at the Commencement Date (or such other convenient date determined by the CEO) shall -

37.9 Commutation

37.9.1 With the approval of an Ordinary Resolution a Retired Partner may at any time after retirement commute the entitlement to a retiring allowance pursuant to Clause 37.8 to a capital sum of such amount as is determined by the resolution or, failing such determination, by the CEO.

37.9.2 It is the current intention of the Partners that such capital sum will be equal to the then present value of the After-Tax Value of the unpaid share of profit, such present After-Tax Value being computed at bank overdraft rate at the time at which the Partner seeks to commute the entitlement as determined by the CEO with yearly rests, and the amount of the unpaid share of profit being estimated.

37.10 After-Tax Value Payment

If a Retired Partner -

(a) is prevented by law or by the terms of his or her employment or rules of his or her profession from receiving a share of profits of the Firm, and

(b) in consequence thereof any payment under Clause 37.8 would constitute assessable income of the Firm or of its successors in business or of the continuing Partners,

then in lieu of such payment the Retired Partner shall receive an amount determined in accordance with Clause 37.9.2.”

  1. The Applicant’s case in accordance with his evidence before the Tribunal can be summarised in brief terms as follows:

(a) The old firm was, prior to the merger agreement, a highly regarded and profitable firm of solicitors practising in Sydney.



(b) The S firm had a small presence in Sydney, but desired a much larger presence. Accordingly, the partners in the old firm were able to and did indeed exact an amount, referred to by the Applicant variously as goodwill, premium and incentive, as a condition of entry into the merger agreement. I propose to use one term only and being “premium”, and which includes all of such terms.



(c) The premium was calculated by reference to the WIP of the old firm as recorded in its financial statements as at 30 June 1990. That amount is the Frozen WIP; it amounted in aggregate to $4,634,516 and of which the Applicant’s share, taking into account certain alterations in the structure of the old firm in the year ending June 1990, was $162,149.



(d) The Frozen WIP was simply a yardstick or measure selected for the relevant purpose. The new firm did not take over or assume the WIP belonging to the old firm and which remained the property of the partners in the old firm. As the work in question was completed, bills were rendered by the new firm which accounted to the old firm for its share, calculated by reference to the proportion of work performed before and after the merger.



(e) The Applicant’s case, put in simplistic terms, is that the payments in the Relevant Year on account of his Frozen WIP entitlement, albeit made pursuant to the merger agreement, constituted payments on account of a premium properly to be categorised as capital and not income, and exempt from tax because it was referable to his old firm partnership, and which commenced on 1 July 1985, and thus prior to 1 September 1985.

(c) (1) As at 30 June 1990, (and as appears from Note 9) the partners in the old firm had capital of $10,000. That amount was augmented by loan accounts of $528,300. WIP was reflected as $4,634,516 (and this is the amount of the Frozen WIP); current accounts were in debit in an amount of $1,405,417.



(2) At the end of June 1991 (and as appears from Note 12) WIP was reflected at $4,584,564. The partners had overdrawn their drawing accounts to the extent of $2,905,279. Taking into account an operating loss of $713,922 and overdrawn current accounts of $233,176, the total of Partners’ Funds was $1,360,487.



(3) In the year ending June 1992, and as appears from Note 12, there was a further operating loss of $369,928, reducing partners funds to $274,564.



(4) The 1991 figures in the two notes do not match, but the Tribunal does not consider that anything turns on this. The evidence before the Tribunal leads it to consider that in respect of the year ending June 1991, Note 12 is more likely to have been correct.



(d) Coleman testified that the old firm reflected WIP as income for accounting but not for tax purposes (27TS at pages 84-85). The accounting records would appear to indicate that in effect the partners, having raised a substantial WIP figure, then drew against it. This then is the manner in which the Frozen WIP of $4,634,516 was dealt with, and I accept that it was not transferred to or taken over by the new firm.



(e) It is against this accounting background that it may be noted that at some time prior to the merger (and in the late 1980’s) some of the partners in the old firm borrowed moneys from the Bank, and which were on-lent to the old firm; those amounts were $169,800 in the case of the Applicant, $245,000 in the case of Wilson and $290,740 in the case of Frank; (see Exhibit A5).



(f) The Applicant’s evidence was that in the absence of an undertaking by the new firm to pay the Frozen WIP to the partners of the old firm in accordance with the retirement allowance provisions of the merger agreement, the merger agreement would not have been entered into, and that in other words the entry into this undertaking by the new firm was of crucial importance.



  1. Wilson’s evidence was that at the time of the merger, the old firm was a respected but not a large firm of solicitors, and that it could grow in importance only by way of a merger. He agreed that the Frozen WIP was an important element in the negotiations but said that it was only one of a number of important factors. See in this context the following evidence of Wilson (27TS, page 92):
“All that happened was it was to be payable to the individual partners upon their retirement? --- That’s so.

See also in this context 27TS, page 95 (line 14).



  1. (a) Frank’s evidence corresponded with that of Wilson. He agreed that the Frozen WIP was one of a number of critical elements in the negotiations although he said that there were other and more critical factors. His evidence was that the merger arose from a combination of factors, one of which was the fact that both the old firm and the S firm needed a presence in the State in which the State firm practised. The two firms together (ie the old firm and the S firm) selected the particular State firm for the purpose of the merger.

(b) Frank’s evidence was that there were existing pension entitlements in respect of the S firm, and that while it would have been preferable to establish entitlements in respect of the new firm on a consistent basis overall, there was not sufficient time, and it was in these circumstances that it was accepted that the Frozen WIP would be paid to the partners in the old firm in accordance with the retirement allowance provisions. He said that although the schedule referred to in the retirement allowance provisions had not been finalised as at the date of execution of the merger agreement or the commencement date of the merger, it was finalised shortly thereafter and attached. Frank also testified that he was entitled to include his “equity in WIP” in his statement of assets and liabilities (28TS, page 30 at lines 16-21).



  1. (a) The title of clause 37.8 of the merger agreement is “Retiring Allowances”; however, clause 1.2 provides that headings do not affect interpretation.

(b) Note 2 to clause 37.8.2 relates to old firm retirement provisions and which includes pension and equity in WIP.



(c) Clause 37.8.6 provides that when a retiring allowance is payable in relation to a partner who practised in more than one Profit Centre, the chief executive officer may “apportion the shares of Firm’s Profit making up the retiring allowance ... in such manner as the CEO considers appropriate to reflect the time spent in each Profit Centre”.



(d) Clause 37.9.1 provides that in certain circumstances a retired partner can commute a retirement allowance to a capital sum. (As Mr Bevan pointed out, the clause uses the words “capital sum” and not “lump sum” and that the word capital would be otiose if the sum were in fact capital in any event; the same comment is apt in respect of clause 37.9.2.)



(e) Clause 37.9.2 then goes on to note that the capital sum will be equal to the after-tax value of the unpaid share of profit. (Mr Pape criticised the manner in which this clause is worded, but his criticism does not alter the fact that the clause is concerned with income rather than capital concepts.)



(f) Clause 37.10 provides that where a retired partner cannot receive a share of profits and so that the amount referable to that share constitutes income of continuing partners, the retired partner receives a commuted amount calculated in accordance with clause 37.9.2.



  1. (a) The detailed schedule referable to each partner was, as noted previously, duly prepared. It sets out that in respect of the Applicant he had an entitlement to:

(l) Pages 238 and following of Exhibit R2 relate to a letter by the Applicant to the Managing Partner of the new firm dated 18 April 1997; I reproduce the provisions of that letter as regards “Tax Treatment of Payments Representing ‘Frozen Equity in WIP’” at pages 239 and 240 as follows:



“Clause 37.8.2 of the [new firm] partnership agreement refers to the preservation of retirement benefits of partners of the constituent firms at the time of the merger.

The memorandum from Frank (dated 5 July 1991) stated the “frozen equity in WIP” entitlements of the [old firm] partners and my balance was $162,149, the amount which I have been paid by the [new firm].

The “frozen equity in WIP” entitlement of the [old firm’s] partners was based on their respective equity in the WIP balance at 30 June 1990.

It is apparent that the entitlement of a former [old firm] partner on retirement to a payment in respect of “frozen equity in WIP” bears no direct link with WIP accounting of the [new firm] partnership at all. Indeed, as indicated in the [old firm] accounts for 1991 and 1992 years, the balance of [old firm] WIP had reduced to nil by 30 June 1992. Therefore, there can be no nexus between the “frozen equity in WIP” entitlement and WIP that belonged to the [old firm] partners at the time of the [new firm] merger.

Rather, the entitlement to “frozen equity in WIP” represents a retirement entitlement specifically acknowledged by the [new firm] partnership (under clause 37.8.2 of the partnership agreement) as owing to certain former [old firm] partners, but which cannot be directly linked to any amount actually brought to account as WIP by the [new firm] partnership.

As such, the payment to me in respect of “frozen equity in WIP” is really deferred payment of a capital sum recognised as being payable to the [old firm] partners in consequence of the merger to form the [new firm] partnership, consistent with the terms of the 28 November 1992 [new firm] resolution which confirmed my entitlement.

The conclusion is also supported by the terms of the [new firm] partnership agreement, ie clause 37.3 and also clause 10.5(c) and (d) which specifically states that “no Partner has any entitlement” to Work in Progress.

It follows that as my entitlement to payment in respect of the “frozen equity in WIP” arose from the merger to form [the new firm], and given that there is no accounting nexus between the “frozen equity in WIP” entitlement and any amount recorded in the [new firm] accounts as WIP, then the payment to me cannot comprise a distribution of net income for income tax purposes.

I also noted that in IT2551, the Commissioner of Taxation examined the correct income tax treatment of payments made to a retiring partner on account of work in progress. Broadly, the Commissioner draws a distinction between payments that represent the profit component of work in progress, (which will be included in the retiring partner’s assessable income) and payments form work in progress where no detailed calculation of the profit component is made (which will be treated as a non-assessable capital receipt).

The Commissioner also notes that in some cases the partnership agreement may provide for profit distributions to be made to a retired partner on account of WIP at the retirement date, as the profit from that WIP is actually realised; and such profit distributions will also be treated as assessable income.

In terms of the IT2551 principles, as noted above, my entitlement to “frozen equity in WIP” did not arise by reference to the WIP balance in the [new firm’s] accounts at the time of my retirement, but instead was provided for in the terms of the merger agreement. Therefore, the payment cannot represent the profit component of the [new firm] WIP balance at the date of my retirement from the [new firm] partnership, and therefore will not be included in my assessable income under IT2551 principles.

Indeed, IT2551 notes the general legal principle (expressed in Halsbury’s Laws of England, 4th Ed, volume 35, paragraph 189) that an outgoing partner is not entitled to share in the future profits of the partnership, in the absence of a specific agreement between the partners to the contrary. As I have already noted, the [new firm] partnership agreement (clause 37.3 and clause 10.5(d)) specifically provides that no partner has a specific entitlement in respect of the [new firm’s] WIP on retirement from the partnership.”


That letter was significant in that although it characterised the relevant payments as deferred payments of a capital sum, it did not state that it was a premium. Rather the Applicant appears to have emphasised that those amounts were not taxable because they were not in fact payments in respect of WIP entitlements.

With this I agree. But there is a question whether these two cases and other authorities support the Court of Appeal’s view that it is legitimate to take into account the common beliefs of the parties as developed and manifested during their antecedent negotiations.

The broad purpose of the parol evidence rule is to exclude extrinsic evidence (except as to surrounding circumstances), including direct statements of intention (except in cases of latent ambiguity) and antecedent negotiations, to subtract from, add to, vary or contradict the language of a written instrument (Goss v Lord Nugent) (59). Although the traditional expositions of the rule did not in terms deny resort to extrinsic evidence for the purpose of interpreting the written instrument, it has often been regarded as prohibiting the use of extrinsic evidence for this purpose. No doubt this was due to the theory which came to prevail in English legal thinking in the first half of this century that the words of a contract are ordinarily to be given their plain and ordinary meaning. Recourse to extrinsic evidence is then superfluous. At best it confirms what has been definitely established by other means; at worst it tends ineffectively to modify what has been so established.

On the other hand, it has frequently been acknowledged that there is more to the construction of the words of written instruments than merely assigning to them their plain and ordinary meaning - see, for example, the remarks of Knox C.J. in Life Insurance Co. of Australia Ltd v Phillips (60). This has led to a recognition that evidence of surrounding circumstances is admissible in aid of the construction of a contract. So Lord Wilberforce in L Schuler A.G. v Wickman Machine Tool Sales Ltd. (61) was able to state the broad thrust of the rule in this way:

‘The general rule is that extrinsic evidence is not admissible for the construction of a written contract; the parties’ intentions must be ascertained, on legal principles of construction, from the words they have used. It is one and the same principle which excludes evidence of statements, or actions, during negotiations, at the time of the contract, or subsequent to the contract, any of which to the lay mind might at first sight seem to be proper to receive.’

His Lordship noted that evidence of surrounding circumstances is an exception to the rule, but he had no occasion to discuss its scope for there is not, as it is here, a critical question.

However, as Lord Wilberforce had earlier pointed out in his speech in Prenn, a speech in which four other members of the House of Lords concurred, the English rule forbidding recourse to extrinsic evidence is not as strict as some have thought. The issue in Prenn was whether the word “profits” meant the separate profits of R.T.T., a company controlled by the appellant, or the consolidated profits of the group of companies consisting of R.T.T. and its subsidiaries. It was held that, although evidence of prior negotiations and of the parties’ intentions, and a fortiori the intentions of one of the parties ought not to be received, evidence restricted to the factual background known to the parties at or before the date of the contract, including evidence of the “genesis” and objectively of the “aim” of the transaction, was admissible. Considered in the light of this evidence “profits” meant “consolidated profits”.

Lord Wilberforce said(62):

‘The time has long passed when agreements, even those under seal, were isolated from the matrix of facts in which they were set and interpreted purely on internal linguistic considerations. There is no need to appeal here to any modern, anti-literal, tendencies, for Lord Blackburn’s well-known judgment in River Wear Commissioners v Adamson (63) provides ample warrant for a liberal approach. We must, as he said, inquire beyond the language and see what the circumstances were with reference to which the words were used, and the object appearing from those circumstances, which the person using them had in view. Moreover, at any rate since 1959 (Macdonald v Longbottom (64)) it has been clear enough that evidence of mutually known facts may be admitted to identify the meaning of a descriptive term.’

His Lordship went on to assert that the well-known decision of Cardozo J. in Utica City National Bank v Gunn (65) “followed precisely the English line”(66). There extrinsic evidence of the circumstances in which a guarantee was executed and of its object was received for the purpose of giving the words “loans and discounts” the looser meaning of “renewals”. Lord Wilberforce quoted with evident approval the comment of Cardozo J. (67) that surrounding circumstances may “stamp upon a contract a popular or looser meaning” than the strict legal meaning, certainly when to adopt the latter would make the transaction futile.

...

It is apparent that the principle on which the Judicial Committee acted in Simpson is that where words in a contract are susceptible of more than one meaning extrinsic evidence is admissible to show the facts which the negotiating parties had in their mind.

...

Lord Wilberforce returned to the same theme in Reardon Smith (75). In a speech concurred in by a majority of the members of the House of Lords he acknowledged that it is legitimate “to have regard to ... ‘the surrounding circumstances’”(76). He went on to say (77):

‘In a commercial contract it is certainly right that the court should know the commercial purpose of the contract and this in turn presupposes knowledge of the genesis of the transaction, the background, the context, the market in which the parties are operating.’

After discussing Utica, Prenn (78) and Wickman (79), his Lordship continued (80):

‘It is often said that, in order to be admissible in aid of construction, these extrinsic facts must be within the knowledge of both parties to the contract, but this requirement should not be stated in too narrow a sense. When one speaks of the intention of the parties to the contract, one is speaking objectively - the parties cannot themselves give direct evidence of what their intention was - and what must be ascertained is what is to be taken as the intention which reasonable people would have had if placed in the situation of the parties. Similarly when one is speaking of aim, or object, or commercial purpose, one is speaking objectively of what reasonable persons would have in mind in the situation of the parties.’

...

The true rule is that evidence of surrounding circumstances is admissible to assist in the interpretation of the contract if the language is ambiguous or susceptible of more than one meaning. But it is not admissible to contradict the language of the contract when it has a plain meaning. Generally speaking facts existing when the contract was made will not be receivable as part of the surrounding circumstances as an aid to construction, unless they were known to both parties, although, as we have seen, if the facts are notorious knowledge of them will be presumed.

It is here that a difficulty arises with respect to the evidence of prior negotiations. Obviously the prior negotiations will tend to establish objective background facts which were known to both parties and the subject matter of the contract. To the extent to which they have this tendency they are admissible.”

(a) The Respondent submitted that the amounts in question were received as a series of periodical receipts in the nature of a pension and as such constituted income according to ordinary concepts.



(b) In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, the High Court approved the periodicity principle. In essence, the case establishes that a series of payments constitute income when they are made for the purpose of being proceeds upon which the taxpayer depends for regular income (See also: Parsons, Income Taxation in Australia at page 72). Dixon CJ and Williams J stated (at page 557):



“Because the [payment] was an expected periodical payment ... and because it formed part of the receipts upon which he depended for the regular expenditure upon himself and his dependants and was paid to him for that purpose, it appears to us to have the character of income, and therefore to form part of the gross income ...”


Also, Fullagar J remarked (at 567):



“It seems to me that the appellant’s receipts from Macdonald, Hamilton & Co must be regarded as having the character of income. They were regular periodical payments - a matter which has been regarded in the cases as having some importance in determining whether particular receipts possess the character of income or capital in the hands of the recipient, see eg Seymour v Reed (1927) AC 554, at 570 and Atkinson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 64; (1951) 84 CLR 298. This consideration, while not unimportant, is not decisive. What is, to my mind, decisive is that the expressed object and the actual effect of the payments made was to make an addition to the earnings, the undoubted income, of the respondent. What the employing firm decided to do, and what it really did, in relation to the respondent and others in the same position, was “to make up the difference between their present rate of wages and the amount they will receive”. What is paid is not salary or remuneration, and it is not paid in respect of or in relation to any employment of the recipient. But it is intended to be, and is in fact, a substitute for - the equivalent pro tanto of - the salary or wages which would have been earned and paid if the enlistment had not taken place. As such, it must be income, even though it is paid voluntarily and there is not even a moral obligation to continue making the payments. It acquires the character of that for which it is substituted and that to which it is added.”


(c) Where receipts come to a taxpayer not as a gift, but as a matter of right, actual dependence need not be an aspect of the periodicity principle (Parsons, Income Taxation in Australia at page 73). Thus, payments under maintenance orders, annuities and pensions are income under this principle despite an absence of dependence or purpose. This “gloss” on Dixon is in accordance with subsequent judgments which have found such payments to be income under ordinary concepts: see Keily v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 83 ATC 4248, where the aged person’s pension was held to be income.



(d) This objective approach is consistent with earlier case law to the effect that periodic receipts can be in the nature of income; see Egerton-Warburton v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 40; (1934) 51 CLR 568.

  1. The compensation receipts principle overlaps to some extent with the periodic receipts principle. In this context:

(a) The Respondent submitted that the relevant payments constitute income by virtue of the compensation receipts principle as detailed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1980-1981) 147 CLR 578. The Respondent contended that where payments are made to compensate for a loss of income (eg a share of the net income of a partnership) and such payments are made periodically, they bear the character of income according to ordinary concepts.



(b) In Smith’s case, the taxpayer, a medical practitioner employed by a hospital, was injured in an accident. He maintained a personal disability insurance policy and received payments referable to the period of his incapacity. It was held that the sum so received was assessable and that premiums paid in respect of the policy were deductible. Gibbs CJ and Stephen, Mason and Wilson JJ stated that the policy was an insurance against the loss of an ability and that the taxpayer’s capacity or ability to earn was a capital asset. However, their Honours said that this was insufficient to establish that the moneys paid under the policy were paid in substitution for income and, therefore, took the place of a revenue receipt. Their Honours went on to say (at 583):



“If the ability to earn is the tree, and income the fruit thereof, a policy of insurance against impairment of the fruit-bearing capacity of the tree may well take the form of providing the fruit until such time as the tree recovers its proper role. The degree of correspondence, if any, between the moneys payable under the policy and the actual pecuniary loss of revenue suffered by the insured is a relevant factor, but it is not necessary to look for an indemnity measured with any precision against the loss. Any fruit is better than none, whether or not it represents adequate compensation for the loss.

...

In our opinion the conclusion is inescapable that the purpose of the policy is to diminish the adverse economic consequences of injury by accident. It was to provide a monthly indemnity against the income loss arising from the inability to earn. The revenue character of the benefits is so clearly stamped upon them during the period of two years during which the insured is totally disabled from earning that the remote possibility of him enjoying some windfall thereafter by reason of his securing gainful employment of the kind prescribed is of no consequence.”


(c) The decision of the Full Federal Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Inkster 89 ATC 5142 illustrates the application of Smith. ... The calculation of such payments by reference to, and as part of, a weekly income and regular receipt thereof may be sufficient to attract the character of income to the payments. (See FCT v Slaven at ATC pp.4084-4085; ALR p.92)...

Periodicity of payment alone may not be determinative of the question of whether the payments are in the nature of income but such a circumstance is important and additional circumstances may make it clear that the periodical payments do have such a character. Although the payments made to the respondent were not in substitution for wages lost, the payments were intended to be a supplement to whatever income the respondent enjoyed and, furthermore, each payment as it was made was intended to operate as a weekly amelioration of any realisation of his impaired capacity to earn a weekly income.”


  1. I turn next to consider the Reuter principle:

(a) The Respondent submitted (Respondent’s Outline of Submissions at page 8) that the relevant payments to the Applicant made pursuant to the merger agreement are income on the basis of the principle enunciated in Reuter v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4037 (and affirmed by the Full Federal Court at 93 ATC 5030). The Respondent expressed this principle in the following manner:



“Where payments ... are received which bear a sufficient relationship, not being a merely temporal relationship, to the recipient’s former contractual entitlement as a partner to share in the net income of the partnership that he has recently retired from ... because the payments are so closely (ie contractually) associated with that recently relinquished entitlement to share in that net partnership income, that payment or those payments are the product of that recently relinquished contractual entitlement to share in that net income and, as such, is or are income under ordinary concepts.”


(b) In Reuter it was held that although the payment to the taxpayer was not contractually made for his services and did not, in law, reduce the remuneration payable to him, there was a sufficient relationship, not being a merely temporal relationship, between the payment and the taxpayer's services to give the payment the character of income. The payment was associated with the services the taxpayer performed such that the payment was a product of his services. Accordingly the payment to the taxpayer was, in his hands, income under ordinary concepts.



(c) Thus, in the context of this submission, the question is whether the principle in Reuter applies so as to impute to the relevant payments to the Applicant the character of income. The facts in this case are distinguishable from that of Reuter. In that case, the payments were made in relation to the relinquishment of contractual entitlements. The payments presently in question arise not from the relinquishment of contractual entitlements, but rather pursuant to the Applicant’s contractual entitlement in accordance with the retirement allowance provisions.



(d) But this does not mean that the Reuter principle does not apply. The Full Court’s decision in Reuter illustrates the true scope of the principle. The Full Court stated (at 5036):



“The consideration for the payment described in the Deed of Covenant is only part of a matrix of relevant events providing the context in which the Deed was executed and in which the character of the payment must be found. The record in the Deed that the payment made to Reuter had connection with the covenants to be provided by Reuter in the Deed, did not exclude connection with other events and was not conclusive of the nature of the payment received by Reuter. The issue to be decided was whether, in fact, the payment was a ‘product’ of the taxpayer's services having regard to all relevant material.”


(e) The payments in question do indeed, in my view, relate to past services. Those past services included the services of the Applicant as a partner in the old firm; it was that historic Frozen WIP (perhaps correct in June 1990 but almost certainly not thereafter) which was used as the calculation measure, and notwithstanding that another measuring device might properly have been selected. The payments in question also relate to past services in the new firm; they were made under the retirement allowance provisions which governed the rights and duties of the partners in the new firm inter se. The question might perhaps be tested thus: would the Applicant have received the payments in question from the new firm if he had not become a partner in the new firm? The answer must be no. The Applicant’s evidence (as set out previously) was that the inclusion of the provisions preserving his Frozen WIP entitlement (characterised by him in his oral evidence as a premium but in his Supreme Court summons as a pension) was crucial in relation to the merger negotiations.



(f) In my view the payments constituting the relevant amount could be categorised as income under any of the three bases referred to in clauses 23, 24 and 25 above. In any event and even apart from such bases, payments in respect of pension are inherently payments properly characterised as being on income account.



  1. I turn now to deal with the Additional Submissions; dealing with them in this fashion may unavoidably involve a degree of repetition.

(a) The Applicant contends that if he had received his Frozen WIP in one lump sum, it would have been a capital receipt. The Applicant cites Wesleyan & General Assurance Society v IRC [1946] 2 AllER 749 and IRC v Ramsay (1935) 20 TaxCas 79 in support of his contention. I do not accept that this is correct; both cases are distinguishable on their facts insofar as the payments here in question were claimed and received pursuant to the retirement allowance provisions.



(b) The Applicant contends that the compensation receipts principle was not put to him during the hearing. The Applicant accepted that the retirement allowance provisions payments were treated by him in the same manner as he treated other income. That admission is sufficient for this purpose. See in this respect Dixon’s case at page 557.

(c) The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s Additional Submissions that the payments in question must be characterised in the hands of the Applicant recipient, and not by reference to the paying old firm. In respect of the old firm, the issue relates to section 51(1) of ITAA; in respect of the Applicant, the issue is one related to section 25(1) of ITAA (see Federal Coke Co Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4255 at 4265).

(d) In respect of paragraph B of the Applicant’s Additional Submissions the Tribunal cannot accept that the proper characterisation is that of a premium which implicitly recognised goodwill. The evidence before the Tribunal does not allow for such a characterisation. But even if the payment could be categorised as a premium, it would still be taxable as income having regard to the decision in Montgomery’s case.

(e) The fact that the amounts borrowed may have been “roughly in the same order of magnitude” as the Frozen WIP entitlements is not surprising in the view of the Tribunal, having regard to the fact that they were connected to some extent with drawings against WIP, although it was not clear whether the borrowings preceded the drawings either wholly or in part. To draw against WIP may be legally permissible but it is by no means a conservative option. And the fact that the Bank required security in the form of a “direction to pay” is equally unsurprising.

(f) Clause 5 of the Applicant’s Additional Submissions contains an admission (in the alternative) that the payments are pension payments, but should nevertheless be characterised as capital. The Applicant denies that the payments are in fact in respect of pension but does not deny that they are referable to past services. The evidence before the Tribunal is clearly indicative of payments of pension.

(g) The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s Additional Submissions as to the fact that the retirement allowance provisions are indicative of the intent of the new firm partners that they receive retiring allowances calculated (originally), in the case of the old firm partners, by reference to Frozen WIP. Those allowances must of necessity be referable to past services and whether those past services were in fact rendered to the old firm or the new firm or both is not to the point. Even if the Frozen WIP is a concept referable to the old firm, the services to the old firm are nonetheless relevant. As the Respondent points out, the taxpayer in Reuter’s case rendered services to Tryart Pty; the fact that the taxpayer then received his compensation from BML did not have the effect that the receipts were not ordinary concepts income.

(h) The Tribunal agrees that a proper consideration of the retirement allowance provisions clearly indicates a contractual obligation to provide retiring partners with retiring allowances (and see for example the opening words of clause 37.8.2 of the merger agreement).

(i) Clauses 40.1, 40.2 and 40.3 of the merger agreement (which were not canvassed at the hearings) do not, in the view of the Tribunal, carry the meaning contended for by the Applicant in clause 6 of the Applicant’s Additional Submissions. On the contrary they are clearly designed to preserve the rights of a deceased partner to “receive the same share of profits” (and see clause 40.2 of the merger agreement); clause 40.2 is subject to clause 40.4, which provides for a commutation in a manner similar to that provided by clause 37.10.

(j) As set out in the Respondent’s Additional Submissions it was intended that the retirement allowances for Sydney partners would be paid out of the Sydney Profit Centre. Clause 37.8.6 provides for an apportionment only where a partner practises in more than one Profit Centre. There were 26 old firm partners and there were 16 S firm Sydney partners at the date of the merger. The result is that as set out in the Respondent’s Additional Submissions the old firm partners would receive their allowances to a large extent for themselves. In fact as Exhibit R2 (pages 217 – 224) makes clear, the Applicant’s allowance was paid from the Sydney Profit Centre.

All of this leads inevitably to the conclusion that a characterisation of premium must assume that the old firm partners were obliged to pay 62% of their own premium. This alone indicates how unlikely such a characterisation must be.

(k) The Respondent’s Additional Submissions contain a number of TS references which need not, in my view, be reproduced in these Reasons.

  1. In summary:

(a) There was no evidence before the Tribunal supporting the Applicant’s contention that the payments were made in respect of a premium, but even if there were, the premium would nevertheless be income pursuant to Montgomery’s case.



(b) On the evidence before it the Tribunal must conclude that it was the intention of the parties to the merger agreement that the payments would be received by each recipient as income, and being payments out of profits or of profits.



(c) On a proper construction of the retirement allowance provisions, the payments are properly characterised as income.



(d) A pension is inherently paid in relation to past services; the payments in question which were made to the Applicant were correctly characterised as pension, and including by the Applicant himself.



  1. In all the circumstances, the objection decision must be affirmed but only in respect of the relevant amount in the sum of $100,890.75.
