Re Ogilvy and Mather Pty Ltd v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1990] FCA 344 (6 September 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: OGILVY AND MATHER PTY LTD

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. V G72 of 1990

FED No. 485

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Sweeney(1), Ryan(1) and Hill(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - losses and outgoings - reservation by advertising agent of advertising space and time subject to "non-cancellation periods" - non-cancellation period commencing in one year of income and advertisement published in following year - whether liability to pay for advertising imposed by Media Council rules on advertising agency incurred in first year of income - whether taxpayer had completely subjected himself to outgoing in that year - agency - whether an advertising agency is a true agent for its client advertisers - payments received by agent by way of reimbursement of outgoing paid on behalf of principal - whether income in hands of agent or mere reimbursement of capital outlaid by him - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) s.51(1).

HEARING

MELBOURNE

6:9:1990

Counsel and Solicitors Mr N H M Forsyth QC and

for Appellant: and Mr E J Power instructed by

Messrs Minter Ellison

Counsel and Solicitors Mr J Fajgenbaum QC and

for Respondent: Mr C Maxwell instructed by

Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

That the appeal be dismissed with costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

This is an appeal from a judgment of a single Judge of this Court who set aside decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the A.A.T.") and confirmed the disallowance by the Commissioner of Taxation of objections by the appellant ("Ogilvy and Mather") against assessments to income tax for each of the years ended 31 December 1984 and 31 December 1985.

2. Ogilvy and Mather carries on the business of an advertising agency. In the course of that business, it reserves time on radio stations and television channels for the transmission on behalf of its clients of advertisements or "commercials". Similarly, Ogilvy and Mather reserves space in newspapers and periodicals for the publication of advertisements promoting the interests of its clients. Many proprietors of radio stations, television channels, newspapers and periodicals, who are collectively described as "media proprietors", are members of the Media Council of Australia ("the Media Council") or have otherwise agreed to be bound by rules promulgated by the Media Council.

3. Those rules provide for the accreditation of advertising agencies and stipulate that only accredited agencies are entitled to receive commissions in respect of advertisements transmitted, telecast or published by media proprietors who are members of the Media Council or bound by its rules. Rule 18 is in these terms:

"18. EACH accredited agent shall accept responsibility for the

payment of his or its clients' accounts in respect of all

orders received from or through the accredited agent.

Signature to an advertising order shall be by the

accredited agent and signature 'for and on behalf of' his

client will not be accepted."

4. Terms of payment for advertising by accredited agents are stipulated by Rule 24(a) which provides:

"24.(a) AN accredited agent will be liable for payment of all

advertising placed by him or it, and such payment

will be due from him or it for all advertising

published, broadcast or telecast (as the case may be)

in any month not later than the thirtieth day of the

month following that in which such advertising is

published. If payment is not made on or before the

fifteenth day of the second month following that in

which the advertising was published, broadcast or

telecast (as the case may be) no commission shall be

allowed by the Media Proprietor to the accredited agent."

5. Failure to pay for advertising in accordance with those terms constitutes a ground, under Rule 14(a)(iii), for suspension or cancellation of the agent's accreditation.

6. At all relevant times Ogilvy and Mather has been an accredited agent.

7. The learned primary judge characterized as "unsatisfactory" the evidence disclosing the relations between the client on whose behalf an advertisement is commissioned and the media proprietor to whom the advertisement is entrusted for publication. However, despite that characterization, his Honour concluded that "upon the making of that contract by the accredited advertising agent on behalf of the client, two separate contractual obligations to pay that agreed sum come into existence: one on the agent and the other on the client".

8. This Court was taken in some detail to the evidence before the A.A.T. bearing on aspects of Ogilvy and Mather's performance of its obligations to media proprietors, and the evidence of the extent to which its clients complied with the requirements imposed by the contracts between them and Ogilvy and Mather. It is clear that those contracts were framed with a view to ensuring, as far as practicable, that the agency was put in funds by the client before it had to discharge its obligation to pay the media proprietor for the publication of the relevant advertisement. For example, one contractual document in use in April 1984 included these stipulations:

"The financial terms provide that the agency will finance its

own services, but not the advertising of its clients.

Therefore, we look to the client for prompt payment of monthly

accounts in sufficient time to enable the agency to then pay

media and/or suppliers with the client's money for his services

or supplies. Strict thirty day payments are required.

. . .

Two fundamental principles on which our financial relationship

is based are:

a. That the agency will finance its own services, but not the

advertising of its clients, and

b. That the agency is held by media as solely liable for

payment; indeed, if we make payment after the due date for

any reason at all, media automatically withdraw our commission.

Therefore, it is essential that we receive payment in time to

pay media. Queries will arise from time to time and because we

look upon our relationship as a long-term one, these queries

will be resolved and the necessary adjustments made to the

following month's account."

9. The extent to which clients complied with those stipulations is unclear from the evidence. However, it did emerge from passages to which this Court was taken that Ogilvy and Mather concentrated its efforts on collecting, within time, charges due from the largest or most frequent advertisers amongst its clients. Some unidentified proportion of its clients did not pay accounts rendered to them by the date upon which Ogilvy and Mather was obliged to pay the media proprietor for time or space booked for those clients.

10. The learned primary judge made these findings in respect of the treatment by Ogilvy and Mather for accounting purposes of the costs of obtaining advertising time and space from media proprietors:

"The evidence was that in the respondent's books of account

entries, in respect of an advertisement for the publication of

which the respondent had placed an order on behalf of a client,

would be made at the end of the month in which publication had

occurred. The ledger account of the publisher would be credited

with the difference between the price of the advertisement and

the commission which was to become payable by the publisher

would be debited as income of the respondent. Payment to the

publisher would thereafter be made by the respondent of the

balance of the price after deduction of the commission, and

payment of the price to the respondent would be made by the

client. Any balance sheet of the respondent drawn up before

those payments were made would disclose as an asset and a

liability respectively the price unpaid by the client to the

respondent and the balance of the price unpaid by the

respondent to the publisher, but the price would form no part

of the debit and credit entries by comparison of which the

respondent's profit is calculated. Only the commission would

appear in the respondent's trading account."

11. His Honour also adverted to the recognition of "non-cancellation periods" in this passage in which he identified the issue between Ogilvy and Mather and the Commissioner:

"The evidence was taken by the respondent to have established

that publishers of advertisements with whom the respondent

deals contract for the publication of an advertisement upon

terms that withdrawal of the order for advertisement within a

specified number of days before the agreed date of publication

does not relieve from the obligation to pay the price. These

periods before publication, some as long as 70 days and some as

short as 3 days, during which withdrawal of the order does not

affect the obligation to pay the agreed price, were described

in evidence as 'non-cancellation periods'. The price payable

for publication of each advertisement, ordered by the

respondent on behalf of a client, the publication date of which

fell after the end of the year of income (in the respondent's

case 31 December) and the non-cancellation period in respect of

which commenced before the end of that year of income was

claimed by the respondent to be an outgoing incurred by the

respondent in that year of income in carrying on its business.

In recognition of the circumstance that during the next year of

income an amount equal to that outgoing would be received by

the respondent from the client, either in reimbursement of the

price paid by the respondent to the publisher or in provision,

before payment by the respondent, of the amount of the price

payable to the publisher, the respondent reduced in each year

of income the aggregate of the amounts said to be such

outgoings by the aggregate of the amounts said to have been

such outgoings in the preceding year, and claimed only the

balance as a deduction. Thus the outgoings, as the respondent

contended they were, of the years 1982, 1983, 1984 and 1985 -

1982 $21,215

1983 $771,414

1984 $1,270,467

1985 $1,079,114

- were the subjects of the following claimed deductions:

1983 ($771,414 - $21,215) $750,199

1984 ($1,270,467 - $771,414) $499,053

The respondent included as assessable income of the year ended

31 December 1985 an amount equal to the aggregate of the

outgoings claimed to have been incurred in the preceding year,

$1,270,467, and claimed to deduct $1,079,114 as outgoings

incurred in 1985. The applicant Commissioner disallowed the

deductions claimed in respect of the year ended 31 December

1984, aggregating $1,270,467, and deducted from assessable

income the amount of $771,414 treated by the respondent as

assessable income in its return. He took the same course in

respect of the amounts of $1,079,114 and $1,270,467 treated by

the respondent as deductible and as income respectively in the

return for the year ended 31 December 1985. The respondent's

objection in respect of each of those two years was disallowed.

Upon reference to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal the

Commissioner's decisions were set aside."

12. The A.A.T. concluded that when a non-cancellation period commenced Ogilvy and Mather was under a contractual obligation to pay the media proprietor the price agreed to be paid for the publication of the advertisement. Accordingly, so the A.A.T. held, that obligation was an outgoing incurred at that time within the meaning of s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ("the Act") notwithstanding that the time for payment would not arrive until later, and, in certain circumstances, in the following year of income. The learned primary Judge disagreed with that view. He assumed that if the client advertiser communicated after the commencement of the non-cancellation period a desire that his advertisement not be published, or if the media proprietor communicated an election to "charge for the air time booked" an "indebtedness of advertiser to publisher in an amount equal to the price payable for publication of the advertisement would arise". However, his Honour went on to say:

"But in each case the obligation to pay would not exist until

the communication occurred. Nor would any unconditional

obligation to pay the price exist at the time the "non-cancellation

period" commenced or at any time thereafter until

publication of the advertisement. If the advertisement were not

published and no other circumstance occurred by reason whereof

an obligation arose to pay the publisher an amount equal to the

price, no unconditional obligation to pay the price or an

amount equal to the price would ever arise, in my opinion."

His Honour then concluded:

"In my opinion the making of a contract consisting of a promise

to pay a price in consideration, and upon performance, of the

other party's promise to render the first party a service at or

by a specified time satisfies the requirement of s.51(1) that

an outgoing in the amount of the price should have been

incurred. (In this case satisfaction of that requirement was,

no doubt rightly, regarded as deferred until the commencement

of the 'non-cancellation period', because the contracts in

question, or many of them, were regarded as including terms

which empowered each party to rescind the contract by notice to

the other before the commencement of that period.) The taxpayer

ought in my opinion to be regarded as having 'completely

subjected himself' to the outgoing by making such a contract,

notwithstanding that his promise will not become legally

enforceable against him unless and until the service has been

rendered."

13. The opinion was then expressed that the conception of dependent promises considered in Greig and Davis, The Law of Contract Ch.20 is not apt "to provide the discriminatory criterion of the incurring of an outgoing pursuant to a contract for the purposes of s.51(1)". Rather, his Honour opined:

"the more appropriate conception is of a liability 'incurred'

when the contract is made, albeit a liability contingent upon

performance by the other party of his promise to render the

service. The appropriateness of that conception is the more

apparent in a commercial context, such as is under present

consideration, where it is virtually certain that the other

party's promise will be performed".

14. However, the learned primary Judge was persuaded to allow the Commissioner's appeal on the ground that, in the circumstances, no outgoing could be said to have been incurred by Ogilvy and Mather which was represented by the liability said to have arisen on the commencement of the non-cancellation period. In the following passage, his Honour described the relevant circumstances and indicated the reasoning which, he considered, required him to allow the appeal:

"The evidence did not justify, and the Tribunal did not make, a

finding that in any substantial proportion of cases payment of

the price by the respondent to the publisher preceded receipt

of the price by the respondent from the client. What was

declared in the passage last quoted (being the reference to

"fundamental principles" contained in the extract from the

contract between Ogilvy and Mather and the media proprietors

which is reproduced at p 4 of these reasons) to be

'fundamental' and 'essential' was what ordinarily happened.

Accordingly, at the time when the contract for publication of

an advertisement was made, at which time there was made also

the contract between publisher and accredited agent under which

the agent promised to pay the price, the expectation of the

respondent was that when in performance of his promise he

should pay the price he would have previously received from his

principal payment of that amount. The same statement may be

made in respect of the time at which the 'non-cancellation

period' commenced. While the liability of the agent, like the

liability of his client the advertiser, to pay the price may,

for the reasons I have stated, be said to have been incurred

when the 'non-cancellation period' commenced, no loss or

outgoing can in my opinion be said to have been by the agent

then incurred within the meaning of s.51(1). The expression

'losses and outgoings' in that section in my opinion requires

that the payment in contemplation should at least involve a

diminution, even if only momentarily, of the payer's funds by

the payment. The incurring of a liability to make a payment

which is expected to involve no such a diminution does not in

my opinion satisfy the requirement for deductibility expressed

in s.51(1). That the word 'outgoings' does not comprehend a

payment which involves no expense to the payer was laid down by

the Court of Appeal in Dependable Upholstery Ltd. v. Brasted

(1932) 1 KB 291."

15. Similar reasoning, his Honour indicated, also compelled the conclusion that Ogilvy and Mather had not sustained "losses" within the meaning of s.51(1) represented by the liability said to have arisen when the non-cancellation period commenced. On that aspect, his Honour observed:

"It is not in my opinion a legitimate application of s.51(1) to

accept as an allowable deduction the amount of a pecuniary

liability to which a taxpayer has subjected himself by reason

only of the circumstance that it cannot certainly be known

until a later time after the end of the income year that the

liability will not result in a loss or outgoing. All that could

have been said, if so much, of any of the claimed deductions in

this case at the time when the deduction is claimed to have

become allowable, or at the end of the income year in respect

of which the deduction was claimed, was that it might later

appear by reason of events later occurring that the pecuniary

liability was within the statutory description. It must have

been said of the liability at the end of the income year that

it was not probable, and the taxpayer did not expect, that a

loss or an outgoing would result from the liability. Nor was

there evidence before the Tribunal to justify a finding that

any of the amounts constituting the aggregate claimed to be

deductible had in the event answered the statutory description

'losses and outgoings'."

16. Having regard to the conclusion which he reached in the passages to which we have just referred, his Honour found it unnecessary to express an opinion on a further submission on behalf of the Commissioner. That submission was to the effect that any payments made to the publishers by Ogilvy and Mather were made as agent for the respective advertisers who were its principals and in circumstances in which Ogilvy and Mather had an immediate right to reimbursement by those principals. Accordingly, so it was submitted, those payments by Ogilvy and Mather were not losses or outgoings within s.51(1) of the Act unless and until the principal's indebtedness could be characterized as a bad debt. If the principal simultaneously incurred a separate contractual liability to pay the publisher the same debt, it was even clearer, according to the same argument of the Commissioner, that Ogilvy and Mather did not incur, in respect of that debt, a liability or outgoing deductible under s.51(1).

17. The other question left open by the learned primary judge was whether cancellation by the advertiser within the "non-cancellation period" resulted in a continuing obligation on the advertiser to pay the agreed price, or constituted a breach of the contract between advertiser and publisher sounding in unliquidated damages. On that question his Honour observed:

"There was evidence as to what in fact happens when an

advertiser cancels his advertisement within the 'non-cancellation

period'. But it was not at all clear that what in

fact happens is what the contracts made for publication of the

advertisement ordain. The evidence of the terms of the

advertiser's contract with the publisher being unsatisfactory,

and the Tribunal having expressed no conclusion as to the

contractual consequences, as between advertiser and publisher,

of cancellation by the advertiser within that period, I prefer

to express no opinion on that submission on behalf of the applicant."

18. The respondent Commissioner sought to uphold the order of the learned primary judge confirming the disallowance by the Commissioner of Ogilvy and Mather's objections against the assessments but filed a notice of contention that his Honour had erred in law in holding that:

"A. The making of a contract consisting of a promise to pay a

price in consideration, and upon performance, of the

other party's promise to render the first party a service

at or by a specified time satisfies the requirement of

s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 that an

outgoing in the amount of the price should have been incurred;

B. A taxpayer ought to be regarded as having 'completely

subjected himself' to the outgoing by making such a

contract, notwithstanding that his promise will not

become legally enforceable against him unless and until

the service has been rendered."

19. Counsel for the taxpayer challenged the conclusion of fact drawn by his Honour in the passage quoted at p 8 of these reasons that what ordinarily happened was that Ogilvy and Mather had been put in funds by the client advertiser to pay for the relevant advertising before it paid itself the corresponding charges due to the publisher. It is true that the A.A.T. made no specific finding on this question. Nevertheless, the need to achieve a final determination of issues without proliferating hearings before the A.A.T. and this Court can justify the Court in itself identifying relevant facts from the evidence before the A.A.T. where those facts are largely undisputed. See e.g. Statham v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 4,070 at 4,075 which was followed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Emmakell Pty. Ltd. (1990) 90 ATC 4319. The learned primary judge seems to have taken a similar approach in the present case in coming to the conclusion just noted as to what ordinarily happened between Ogilvy and Mather and its advertiser clients in respect of payment of charges for space or time booked on behalf of those clients. However, in our view, it is unnecessary to determine for purposes of this appeal whether facts found by the A.A.T. or otherwise sufficiently undisputed warranted the conclusion or inference drawn by his Honour.

20. We consider the critical question to be whether Ogilvy and Mather incurred a liability to the publishers of advertisements, and if so, whether and when that liability became an "outgoing" within the meaning of s.51(1) of the Act. That sub-section provides:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are

incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are

necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose

of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable

deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or

outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic

nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or

production of exempt income."

21. In the joint judgment of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Pty. Ltd. [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492 it was observed, at 506:

"What losses and outgoings arising in the course of business

are to be deducted is a matter which must be governed by

s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Under its provisions

all losses and outgoings may be deducted to the extent to which

they are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the

purpose of gaining or producing such income, provided, of

course, they are not of a capital nature or otherwise excluded.

The word 'outgoing' might suggest that there must be an actual

disbursement. But partly because such an interpretation would

produce very strange and anomalous results, and partly because

of the use of the word 'incurred', the provision has been

interpreted to cover outgoings to which the taxpayer is

definitively committed in the year of income although there has

been no actual disbursement."

22. Their Honours went on to refer to a broad working rule deduced by Lord Cooper in James Spencer and Co v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1950) SLT 266 at 268 and continued:

"This passage must be qualified in its application under the

Commonwealth Act. For under our law the facts must satisfy the

expression 'losses and outgoings incurred'. These words perhaps

are but little more precise than the word 'established' or the

expression used above 'definitely committed'. But they do not

admit of the deduction of charges unless, in the course of

gaining or producing the assessable income or carrying on the

business, the taxpayer has completely subjected himself to

them. It may be going too far to say that he must have come

under an immediate obligation enforceable at law whether

payable presently or at a future time. It is probably going too

far to say that the obligation must be indefeasible. But it is

certainly true that it is not a matter depending upon 'proper

commercial and accountancy practice rather than jurisprudence'.

Commercial and accountancy practice may assist in ascertaining

the true nature and incidence of the item as a step towards

determining whether it answers the test laid down by s.51(1)

but it cannot be substituted for the test.

To repeat what has been said before in relation to an analogous

provision in the Act of 1922-1934: 'To come within that

provision there must be a loss or outgoing actually incurred.

"Incurred" does not mean only defrayed, discharged, or borne,

but rather it includes encountered, run into, or fallen upon.

it is unsafe to attempt exhaustive definitions of a conception

intended to have such a various or multifarious application.

But it does not include a loss or expenditure which is no more

than impending, threatened, or expected.' : New Zealand Flax

Investments Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1938) 61

CLR 179, at p 207. Nothing that was decided in W. Nevill and

Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1937) 56 CLR

290 was intended to imply that a liability to pay an

ascertained sum is never incurred until the sum becomes due and

payable. The question in that case was whether a sum which a

company had agreed to pay one of two joint managing directors

to induce him to retire was an outgoing on account of capital

or was deductible. Portion of the sum was to be met by monthly

payments over a period extending beyond the year of income and

secured by promissory notes. It was decided that the outgoing

was not on account of capital and was deductible but so much of

it as was payable outside the year of income belonged to the

ensuing accounting period. Probably on this minor point, to

which the parties do not appear to have attached importance,

the judges were influenced to some extent by some of the

considerations affecting their decision on the major question

and looked upon the monthly payments not so much as deferred

instalments of an accrued liability in a lump sum but as an

attempt to spread over a period of trading an outgoing parallel

with the salary that had been saved. But whatever be the

rationale of the decision of the point, clearly enough it is

not based on a view that no outgoing could be incurred until

actual payment was made. It is one thing, however, to say that

it is not necessary, for the purposes of s.51(1), that an

actual disbursement should have taken place. It is another

thing to say that in the present case the taxpayer had incurred

a loss or outgoing in the year of income in respect of the pay

of its men during the annual leave to be taken in the ensuing

accounting period by employees whose service had not as yet

qualified them for annual leave. In respect of those employees

there was no debitum in praesenti solvendum in futuro. There

was not an accrued obligation, whether absolute or defeasible.

There was at best an inchoate liability in process of accrual

but subject to a variety of contingencies. It may be true, that

regarding the labour employed as a whole, the accrual of an

amount of the other claimed had, by 30th June 1947, become

predictable with certainty. But that is not the test. If it be

regarded nevertheless as an evidentiary consideration having

some weight then it cannot be divorced from the further

consideration that the source of the accruing liability, the

award, imposes it as an obligation to pay wages for a period of

time in the future during which the employee must be given

leave. That means that it is imposed in the form of a ensuing

year. In short the deduction claimed of 578 pounds 10s. 2d. does not

represent an expenditure associated with the production of

income before 30th June 1947 for which a liability had been

completely incurred before the date."

23. The passage from the New Zealand Flax case which is quoted in the above extract was considered in Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 CLR 616 where Barwick C.J. observed, at 623:

In my opinion, the language of Dixon J. in New Zealand Flax

Investments Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra)

needs to be carefully perused and applied. Granted that

exhaustive definition of what may be denoted by the word

'incurred' in s.51(1) may not be possible, there can be no

warrant for treating a liability which has not 'come home' in

the year of income, in the sense of a pecuniary obligation

which has become due, as having been incurred in that year. Sir

John Latham's language in Emu Bay Railway Co. Ltd. v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 28; (1944) 71 CLR 596, at p 606 clearly

enough indicates that to satisfy the word 'incurred' in s.51(1)

the liability must be 'presently incurred and due thought not

yet discharged'. The 'liability' of which Sir John speaks is of

necessity a pecuniary liability and the word 'presently' refers

to the year of income in respect of which a deduction is

claimed. it may not disqualify the liability as a deduction

that, though due, it may be paid in a later year. That part of

Sir Owen Dixon's statement in New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd.

v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) which presently

needs emphasis is that the word 'incurred' in s.51(1) 'does not

include a loss or expenditure which is no more than pending,

threatened or expected': and I would for myself add 'no matter

how certain it is in the year of income that that loss or

expenditure will occur in the future'."

24. To similar effect is this passage from the judgment of Gibbs J. (as he then was) in the same case at 627:

"The argument was that during the year of income in question

the taxpayer was 'definitively committed' to the payments in

respect of the leave, and was under a present accrued liability

to make the payments. The principle to be applied in deciding

whether a loss or outgoing was 'incurred' is clear enough. It

is not necessary that there should have been any actual

disbursement: New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd. v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation (supra); Emu Bay Railway Co. Ltd. v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra); Federal Commissioner

of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Ltd. [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492, at

pp 506-507; and see King v. Commissioner of Inland Revenue

(1974) 2 NZLR 190, at pp 194-195. Indeed, it was suggested

in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Ltd.

that it is not necessary that there should be an immediate

obligation enforceable at law whether payable presently or at a

future time, or that the obligation should be indefeasible. It

is not now necessary to consider whether those suggestions

should be accepted as correct. But what is clearly necessary is

that there should be a presently existing liability. In Federal

Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Ltd., this was

expressed by saying that the provisions of s.51(1) cover

'outgoings to which the taxpayer is definitively committed in

the year of income although there has been no actual

disbursement', and that those provisions 'do not admit of the

deduction of charges unless . . . the taxpayer has completely

subjected himself to them'. In other words, s.51(1) does not

cover 'a loss of expenditure which is no more than impending,

threatened or expected': New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd. v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra).

If these principles are applied to the present case, the

question is whether the taxpayer was under a present liability

to make payment to its employees in respect of leave. The

answer is that it was not. The employees were entitled to

leave, but they were not entitled to payment. The entitlement

to payment would not arise until the employees took leave (or

died or left the employment). The event on which the

entitlement of the employees to payment depended had not

occurred. There was a certainty that a liability to make

payments in respect of leave would arise in the future, but it

had not arisen. The present is not a case in which there was an

immediate obligation to make payment in the future, or a

defeasible obligation to pay, or a present obligation which as

a matter of law was unenforceable - there was no accrued

obligation to make any payment at all. There was no loss or

outgoing 'incurred' within s.51(1)."

25. Nilsen's Case was distinguished by a Full Court of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd [1984] FCA 240; (1984) 2 FCR 483 where Toohey J. pointed out, at 49:

"Nilsen's case stresses the need for a presently existing

liability before there can be an outgoing incurred within s.51.

But the case is distinguishable from the matter now before this

court. It was concerned with the entitlement of employees to

long service and annual leave and the obligation of the

taxpayer to pay employees during the period of such leave. The

employees were not entitled to the payment of money in the

sense in which, as mentioned, the employees in Lo's case were

entitled to payment. In Nilsen's case there was no accruing

liability on the part of the employer, simply an obligation to

pay wages when employees became entitled to and took leave. In

the case now under appeal the taxpayer's obligation to pay

interest under deferred debentures arose when the debentures

were issued though subsequent events would determine the

precise amount of interest be paid."

26. In our view, the correct analysis of the contracts between Ogilvy and Mather and the media proprietors or publishers is that a liability did not attach to Ogilvy and Mather until the relevant advertisement had been published or the time or space had been made available for its publication on the agreed date. The effect of the commencement of a "non-cancellation period" was to preclude Ogilvy and Mather from unilaterally avoiding the obligation to pay for the advertisement. However, that is not to say that the liability was incurred, in the sense that Ogilvy and Mather was "definitively committed" to discharge it, from the moment when the non-cancellation period commenced. As we have already suggested, it was publication of the advertisement which definitively committed the agency to the liability, even though payment was not due under rule 24(a), reproduced at p 2 of these reasons, until the thirtieth day of the month following that in which the advertising was published, broadcast or telecast. In the language used in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Ltd. (supra) it was not until publication that there arose a debitum in praesenti, solvendum in futuro, i.e. by the thirtieth day of the next succeeding month.

27. Because the fee which is to become payable to the publisher of the advertisement is ascertainable by reference to a "rate card" or other existing schedule of charges, no difficulty arises in the present context of the kind encountered in Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 32 FLR 210. In that case Newton J. held, at 223, that for the purposes of s.51 of the Act a taxpayer can completely subject itself to a liability, notwithstanding that the quantum of the liability cannot be precisely ascertained provided that it is capable of reasonable estimation. His Honour went on, at 224, to observe:

"The authorities also show, in my opinion, that a taxpayer may

completely subject itself to a liability, notwithstanding that

the liability is defeasible: see Flood's case (supra). It may

incidentally be remarked that all, or almost all, unpaid

liabilities are in a sense defeasible, because they could in

the future be forgiven by the creditor, or cancelled by Act of

Parliament, or barred by any applicable statute of limitations.

If in one year of income an unpaid liability is allowed as a

deduction under s.51 at an estimated amount, then if the

estimate later proves incorrect, the necessary adjustments can

be made in the year of income when the liability is actually

paid, either by way of addition to the assessable income of

that year (if the estimate was too high), or by way of a

further deduction under s.51 in calculating the taxable income

of that year (if the estimate was too low) . . ."

28. In our opinion that analysis accommodates the possibility which was adverted to by the A.A.T. in its reasons for decision in the present case when it noted that:

"It may well be that in some circumstances (after the

non-cancellation period has commenced) the debt is forgiven or may

be subject to what was referred to as a 'drop and charge', the

latter involving some renegotiation between the media and the

agency which may - and no doubt often does - result in the

advertisement still being screened, albeit in a less popular

slot . . . If, as happens, a booking is forgiven in the

subsequent year, the appropriate adjustment will no doubt be

reflected in the agency's accounts."

29. However, that possibility does not avoid the need to resolve the primary question of whether a liability has been incurred. Nor should it be allowed to confuse the analysis which is undertaken in the course of answering that primary question. As Newton J. indicated in the passage just quoted, a liability is no less incurred at a given point because the taxpayer may subsequently be relieved from it by waiver or some other supervening event. On the other hand, a presumptively inchoate liability is not brought within the application of s.51(1) by the possibility that, if and when it has been incurred, the creditor may waive it or the taxpayer may be otherwise relieved from discharging it. As Menhennitt J. indicated in respect of a liability of an insurer in R.A.C.V. Insurance Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1974) ATC 4169 at 4176:

"In relation to liability insurance the insurance company is

bound to indemnify its insured against his liability to a third

person. Once events have occurred out of which a liability to

indemnify an insured arises, it appears to me that within the

meaning of sec. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act a loss

or outgoing has been incurred. Events have occurred which have

subjected it to a liability to indemnify its insured against

his liability to a third person and the extent of that

liability is capable of reasonable estimate. Where there is no

real question of the liability of the insured to the third

party and the only question is one of estimating damages, the

fact that the quantum of the loss or outgoing is a matter of

estimate and that the amount may have to be adjusted in the

light of later events does not stand in the way of it being a

loss or outgoing (see New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd. v. F.C.

of T. [1938] HCA 60; 61 CLR 179 at p 199 and Texas Co. (Australasia) Ltd.

v. F.C. of T. [1940] HCA 9; 63 CLR 382 at p 465 to 466) and in a case

where the liability of the insured to the third party is in

issue but the amount which is likely to be payable can be

reasonably estimated, it is still I think true to say that

within the meaning of sec.51(1) a loss or outgoing has been

incurred by the insurance company."

30. On the analysis which we favour in the present case, the event which must occur to subject Ogilvy and Mather to a liability to the publisher is the publication of the advertisement or the making available on the agreed date of space or time for its publication. If, before that date, the publisher agrees to find another advertiser or to reschedule the advertisement, a different liability may then be incurred by Ogilvy and Mather. However, in the absence of such agreement the agency becomes subjected to a liability for the original fee upon publication or upon the publisher's being ready, willing and able to publish the advertisement or commercial on the originally agreed date.

31. It was submitted on behalf of the taxpayer that, whether or not Ogilvy and Mather was the agent of its advertiser clients, it was primarily and not secondarily liable to the publishers for payment of their charges. We agree with that contention. It is true that in a review of a determination of the Trade Practices Commission in what is known as the Media Council Case (1978) ATPR 17,591, the Trade Practices Tribunal comprised by Deane J. and Messrs Shipton and Walker traced the evolution of advertising agents and observed at 17,616:

"The relationship between advertising agent and advertiser

developed into the relationship of agent and principal in the

strict sense. At the heart of the relationship is the

purchasing of advertising space and time. As between

advertising agent and advertiser, the agent purchases space for

and on behalf of his principal. As between advertising agent

and principal, the liability to pay for the space so purchased

is that of the principal."

32. However, in dealing shortly afterwards with the relationship between advertising agent and media proprietor, the Tribunal concluded in a passage in which the added emphasis is ours:

"Under the Accreditation System, it is clear that the

advertising agent is not an agent for the particular media

proprietor. He contracts with the media proprietor in his own

name and accepts personal liability in respect of

advertisements lodged. Both advertising agent and media

proprietor are, however, fully aware that, except in the

comparatively rare case where the advertising agent is placing

an advertisement promoting his own services, the advertising

agent is acting as agent for the advertiser whose advertising

he is placing."

33. We consider that those two passages can be reconciled by resort to an observation to be found in Bowstead on Agency, 15th Edn. p 427 in these terms:

"'In all cases the parties can by the express contract provide

that the agent shall be the person liable, either concurrently

with or to the exclusion of the principal.' The question

whether an agent who has made a contract on behalf of his

principal is to be deemed to have contracted personally, and if

so the extent of his liability, depends on the intention of the

parties to be deduced from the nature and terms of the

particular contract and the surrounding circumstances,

including any binding custom."

34. The evidence in the present case strongly suggests that it was the agency to which the publisher looked, primarily if not exclusively, for payment of the advertising charges. However, for the reasons which we have outlined, the agent's obligation to pay those charges did not attach at the commencement of the non-cancellation period. At that time, in our view, the agent had not completely subjected itself to the liability in the sense that "payment was a matter of commercial certainty and was not subject to any contingency which would be regarded as such in the world of ordinary business affairs"; (Commercial Union Assurance Co. of Australia Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 32 FLR 32 at 43). Here payment depended on more than the mere effluxion of time from the commencement of the non-cancellation period.

35. Because, for the reasons indicated, we have in substance upheld the arguments adumbrated in the Commissioner's notice of contention, it is unnecessary for us to consider whether the facts of the present case justified the conclusion reached by the learned primary judge that the discharge of the agent's liability to the publisher would not involve a diminution, even momentarily, of the agent's funds. However, we take leave to doubt, with respect, whether Dependable Upholstery Ltd v Brasted (1932) 1 KB 291 illuminates the meaning of "outgoings" in s.51(1) of the Act.

36. We also find it unnecessary to reach a conclusion on the other, question left open below, of whether cancellation by an advertiser during the non-cancellation period left the advertiser subject to a continuing obligation to pay the publisher's agreed charges, or merely exposed the advertiser to an action for unliquidated damages for breach of contract. The proper focus for present purposes is on the liability of the agent to the publisher. As we have concluded that, at the commencement of the non-cancellation period, that liability did not amount to a loss or outgoing within the meaning of s.51(1), the appeal must be dismissed with costs.

between the 1985 and 1984 balance ($191,323)

was thus added back as part of the company's

assessable income. Hence, and unusually, the

applicant says that it has not been assessed

enough in that year. Setting out the accounts

year by year:

The Decision of the Tribunal

20. The Tribunal held that the appellant had accepted a liability for payment to the media. It said:

"The question whether, in the circumstances,

the media could also look to the advertiser for

its charges is therefore beside the point."

The Tribunal proceeded therefore to consider the issue whether the agency had incurred the relevant amounts claimed without considering the question whether the appellant in placing the advertising acted as agent for the advertiser and made no finding on that question. As to the possibility of there being a cancellation after the expiration of the non-cancellation period the Tribunal said:

"This does not mean that the advertiser is

prevented from cancelling - obviously he can

request that the advertisement be simply not

run - what it does mean is that a post-70 days

cancellation carries its own potential

penalties. The evidence for the applicant goes

further, and witnesses were called who asserted

that once the threshold into the 'non-cancellable'

period is crossed, the media looks

to the agency for payment, subject to certain

practices or conventions within the industry."

The Tribunal made no finding as to whether the practices or conventions within the industry to which it referred had been established or what their effect was.

21. The Tribunal after discussing a number of the relevant cases concluded that the arrival of the non-cancellation period had the effect of creating a present or current indebtedness payable in the future and that accordingly a "loss" was incurred in respect of amounts payable to the media once that non-cancellation period had arrived. The Tribunal commented:

"There and then the agency had crossed the

Rubicon and what may happen on the other side

is not to the point. It may well be that in

some circumstances the debt is forgiven, or may

be subject to what was referred to as a 'drop

and charge', the latter involving some re-negotiation

between the media and agency which

may - and no doubt often does - result in the

advertisement still being screened, albeit in a

less popular slot. Be that as it may, it can

have no effect on the outcome of this

application. Much time was thus needlessly

taken up with witnesses, called to depose as to

the practice in the industry with respect to

'non-cancellable' bookings and, not unusually,

there was substantial disagreement. Their

testimony on the consequences of bookings

cancelled beyond the 'non-cancellable' period -

ranging, as it did, from being an obligation

'almost always enforced against the agency' to

'hardly ever' - is in my view, irrelevant. If,

as happens, a booking is forgiven in the

subsequent year, the appropriate adjustment

will no doubt be reflected in the agency's accounts."

22. Accordingly the Tribunal allowed the deduction to the appellant claimed in the 1984 year and included in the assessable income of the appellant for the 1985 year the sum of $191,323.

The Decision Below

23. Jenkinson J was of the view that the appellant in forming contracts with the Media Proprietors for the placement of advertising did so as agent for its client advertisers. Thus his Honour found that there was constituted a contract between the media proprietor and the client advertiser for the transmission of the advertisements notwithstanding what his Honour described as the "curious provision which is the second sentence of Rule 18" of the Media Council Accreditation Rules. In his Honour's view, on the making of the contract by the advertising agency on behalf of the advertiser client, two separate contractual obligations to pay the agreed sum came into existence, one binding the agent and the other the client. His Honour described the evidence of the terms of contracts for publication of advertisements as being unsatisfactory. His Honour said that there was no evidence to justify a conclusion that a price was payable to the publisher by the client if the advertisement was not published and no event justifying or excusing the failure to publish the advertisement had occurred. His Honour said:

"The contracts provided, the evidence

indicated, that the price was payable by the

client in consideration of publication of the

advertisement. The promise to publish the

advertisement, qualified though the evidence

showed it to be, and the promise to pay the

price are dependent promises. Performance of

the former is to precede performance of the

latter and may accordingly be said to be a

condition precedent to the liability of the

client to pay the price. The evidence about

the contracts showed that in certain events

there would be imposed on the client by the

contract an obligation to pay the publisher an

amount equal to the price notwithstanding the

advertisement was not published. But it would

not be known at the time when the 'non-cancellation

period' commenced whether such an

event would occur. . . . If the advertisement

were not published and no other circumstance

occurred by reason whereof an obligation arose

to pay the publisher an amount equal to the

price, no unconditional obligation to pay the

price or an amount equal to the price would

ever arise, in my opinion."

24. His Honour was of the view, however, that once the non-cancellation period had arrived and the parties to the media contract were unable to cancel, the appellant had completely subjected himself to the outgoing notwithstanding that his promise to make payment to the media would not become legally enforceable against him unless and until the advertisement was in fact published. His Honour said:

"In my opinion the more appropriate conception

is that of a liability 'incurred' when the

contract is made, albeit a liability contingent

upon performance by the other party of his

promise to render the service. The

appropriateness of that conception is the more

apparent in a commercial context, such as is

under present consideration, where it is

virtually certain that the other party's

promise will be performed."

25. His Honour then turned to the question whether in the relevant sense used in s.51(1) of the Act, there had been incurred a "loss or outgoing". Although the Tribunal had made no finding of fact relevant to the issue his Honour said:

"The evidence did not justify, and the Tribunal

did not make, a finding that in any substantial

proportion of cases payment of the price by the

respondent to the publisher preceded receipt of

the price by the respondent from the client.

. . . Accordingly, at the time when the contract

for publication of an advertisement was made,

at which time there was made also the contract

between publisher and accredited agent under

which the agent promised to pay the price, the

expectation of the respondent was that when in

performance of his promise he should pay the

price he would have previously received from

his principal payment of that amount. The same

statement may be made in respect of the time at

which the 'non-cancellation period' commenced."

This being the case his Honour was of the view that for the purposes of s.51(1) no loss or outgoing in the relevant sense was ever incurred. His Honour said:

"The expression 'losses and outgoings' in that

section (i.e. s.51(1)) in my opinion requires

that the payment in contemplation should at

least involve a diminution even if only

momentarily, of the payer's funds by the

payment. The incurring of a liability to make

a payment which is expected to involve no such

diminution does not in my opinion satisfy the

requirement for deductibility expressed in s.51(1)."

In reaching this conclusion his Honour found assistance from the decision of the Court of Appeal in Dependable Upholstery Ltd v Brasted (1932) 1 KB 291.

26. His Honour then continued:

"it is not in my opinion a legitimate

application of s.51(1) to accept as an

allowable deduction the amount of a pecuniary

liability to which a taxpayer has subjected

himself by reason only of the circumstance that

it cannot certainly be known until a later time

after the end of the income year that the

liability will not result in a loss or

outgoing. All that could have been said, if so

much of any of the claimed deductions is claimed

to have become allowable, or at the end of the

income year in respect of which the deduction

was claimed, was that it might later appear by

reason of events later occurring that the

pecuniary liability was within the statutory

description. It must have been said of the

liability at the end of the income year that it

was not probable and the taxpayer did not

expect, that a loss or an outgoing would result

from the liability. Nor was there evidence

before the Tribunal to justify a finding that

any of the amounts constituting the aggregate

claim to be deductible had in the event answered

the statutory description 'losses and outgoings.'"

The Statutory Provision

27. Section 51(1) of the Act provides as follows:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to

which they are incurred in gaining or producing

the assessable income, or are necessarily

incurred in carrying on a business for the

purpose of gaining or producing such income,

shall be allowable deductions except to the

extent to which they are losses or outgoings of

capital, or of a capital, private or domestic

nature, or are incurred in relation to the

gaining or production of exempt income."

Was there a Loss or Outgoing?

28. The appellant sought to attack the conclusion reached by his Honour that there was no loss or outgoing on two bases. First it was submitted that the evidence, contrary to his Honour's view disclosed that the payments made by the appellant to the media were not matched up with funds received. It was said rather that the evidence revealed that the clients of the appellant, or at least a majority of them in number, although not necessarily in value, paid late.

29. This was a matter upon which no finding was made by the Tribunal at all. The significance of no finding having been made must be seen in the context of the role of this Court in appeals from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal.

30. Section 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) confers upon a party to a proceeding before the Tribunal a right to appeal to this Court on a question of law from any decision of the Tribunal in that proceeding. As Brennan J said in Waterford v The Commonwealth [1987] HCA 25; (1986-7) 163 CLR 54 at 77:

"The error of law which an appellant must rely

on to succeed must arise on the facts as the

AAT has found them to be or it must vitiate the

findings made or it must have led the AAT to omit

to make a finding it was legally required to make."

31. It is the obligation of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to set out its findings on material questions of fact and to refer to the evidence or other material on which those findings are based: s.43(2B) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act. The provisions of s.44 delineate the boundaries of the proceedings in this Court. Thus it will only be in a case where the party appealing can demonstrate that there is an error of law which vitiates the finding of the Tribunal that this Court can have jurisdiction: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Brixius (1987) 87 ATC 4963 at 4968. The result is that, as Brixius itself discloses, much time will often be spent in determining whether an issue which arises is truly one of law rather than of fact, a distinction not always logically sound and one that tends to lead to arid debate at the expense of a determination by the Court of the real issues between the parties.

32. Section 43(2B) in requiring the Tribunal to give its findings on material questions of fact inevitably leaves to the Tribunal at first instance the decision as to what facts are material. So in the present case as the Tribunal presumably did not regard the question of whether or not clients of the appellant honoured their obligation to put the appellant in funds as having any materiality, it made no finding on that matter. His Honour however apparently regarded that question as being material and embarked himself upon a consideration of the evidence and made findings upon that evidence which are now challenged before us. It is not clear whether submissions were made to the Tribunal that it should make findings of fact in respect of this issue. Where factual matters are in dispute and the parties request the Tribunal to make findings of fact about them, particularly where the parties or one of them see the factual issue as material, there is much to be said for the view that the Tribunal should proceed to make findings of fact as requested, so that when the matter comes before this Court, (circumscribed as it is by the requirement to be confined to a question of law which of its very nature must arise from findings of fact made by the Tribunal), the issue of law can be properly debated and resolved.

33. There is a question of difficulty, in the absence of such findings whether this Court can itself determine what the relevant facts are upon which an issue of law depends. It is true that in Statham and Anor v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 4070 the Full Court of this Court did review for itself the facts. But that was a case as the Court itself made clear at 4075 where "The facts are largely undisputed and the Tribunal did make some findings of fact on material matters". That case was followed in Emmakell v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 90 ATC 4319 at 4326-7 but again in a case where no dispute of fact arose.

34. It is not the role of this Court on an appeal under s.44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act to find facts. An appellant may, of course, challenge a decision of the Tribunal on the basis that there was no evidence to justify the Tribunal's findings of fact: cf Lombardo v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 79 ATC 4542, for such an issue is an appeal on a question of law. A party may also succeed in setting aside a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on the basis that the Tribunal has failed to find the material facts upon which the validity of an assessment depends: East Finchley Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 5280. But where no relevant finding of fact is made and the facts are disputed, it is not for this Court to supplement any deficiency in the performance by the Tribunal of its functions.

35. Thus in Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4834 the Full Court of this Court, having found that the decision of a member of the Tribunal, to the extent that he had found for the Commissioner on a matter not argued by either party, had involved the denial of natural justice refused to deal with other issues involved below on the basis that relevant material findings of fact had not been made and referred the matter back to the Tribunal to make the requisite findings.

36. In my view, if it be material whether the advertiser clients of the appellant put it in funds before the appellant was required to make its payments to the media then this is a matter which should depend upon findings of fact made by the Tribunal and not upon findings of fact made by this Court, which findings of fact do not fall within the boundaries of the issue tendered to this Court by s.44.

37. However in the present case I am of the view that it is quite immaterial to the outcome of this case whether the appellant's clients did to a substantial extent or indeed always put the appellant in funds before the appellant made its payments to the Media Proprietors.

38. In my view his Honour erred in holding that there was no outgoing in the present case where the appellant was first put in funds by its client before making payment.

39. It is well established that for an outgoing to be incurred for the purposes of s.51(1) it does not have to be paid in the year of income: New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179. It is not necessary that the amount to be allowed as a deduction under s.51(1) be a loss. A claim for an outgoing incurred is an alternative to a claim for a loss incurred.

40. The word "outgoing" is an ordinary English word. It contemplates no more than that there is to be something which flows out from the taxpayer. Ordinarily, although not necessarily invariably, that will involve a monetary payment for which the taxpayer is liable. Thus the expression "outgoing incurred" may be equated with the words "liability incurred".

41. The fallacy in his Honour's judgment, with respect, can be tested by considering an independent contractor, (not an agent), who contracts with another to perform work pursuant to a contract that he has entered into with his client. The terms of the contract with the client may require the contractor to be put in funds prior to meeting the obligations to the subcontractor. It may be invariable that the client puts the contractor in funds prior to the contractor becoming liable to make his payment to the subcontractor. There may be no loss suffered by the contractor as a result, in that he would be able to use the funds paid to him (they of course become his own funds) to meet the obligation he has incurred to the subcontractor. But nevertheless the contractor has incurred the outgoing because he is required to use his own funds (albeit augmented by moneys paid to him by the client) in meeting the obligation to the subcontractor. In such a case there is an "outgoing" for the purposes of s.51(1) and if incurred in the year of income that outgoing will be an allowable deduction if otherwise satisfying the requirements of s.51(1).

42. In my opinion the case of Dependable Upholstery Ltd v Brasted (1932) 1 KB 291 is of no assistance in the resolution of the question for it depended solely on the meaning of the word "outgoing" in the context in which it appeared.

43. That case concerned a covenant in a lease whereby a sub-lessor covenanted to indemnify the sub-lessees against "all rates, taxes and outgoings whatsoever which now or hereafter may become payable in respect of the demised premises". As a result of local government rating legislation, the rates payable in the period were reduced and the sub-lessee claimed to recover the amount of the reduction from the sub-lessor who in turn joined the head lessor as a third party, there being a corresponding covenant in the head lease. The question turned upon the construction of the covenant of indemnity. The rating legislation provided that a landlord was liable to pay to his tenant or allow by way of deduction from his rent the amount of the reduction in rates. This provision was equally applicable to a head landlord as to a sub-lessor. Thus the situation was that the sub-lessor was entitled to receive from the head lessor an amount equal to the reduction in rates but was required to pass that reduction on to his sub-lessee. It was held that the amount which the sub-lessor was obliged to pay to the sub-lessee under the rating legislation was not an outgoing. In these circumstances Scrutton L.J. said at 299:

"Is that an outgoing? The question may be put

thus; it is supposed that 100 pounds will have to be

paid for rates on the premises. The landlord

says to the tenant 'I will relieve you of that

burden if you will pay an additional 100 pounds

rent'. Then for the benefit of the tenant, a

statute is passed by which instead of 100 pounds the

rates become 25 pounds and the Legislature secures

the tenant by saying to him: 'You computed

your rent at 200 pounds on the assumption that your

landlord was going to relieve you of an

obligation to pay 100 pounds rates; now that you are

only obliged to pay 25 pounds for rates your rent

must be reduced by 75 pounds.' In that state of

things the repayment of 75 pounds by the landlord to

the tenant is not an outgoing; it is a

balancing transaction. I do not think that

the word 'outgoing' is an apt word to express a

payment made in these circumstances."

44. The context of the case is so totally removed from the context of the use of the word "outgoing" in s.51(1) of the Act as to be of no assistance.

45. It was not suggested, and, indeed, there was no finding of fact to justify a conclusion, that moneys paid by advertiser clients to the appellant were held upon trust so as not to form part of the moneys of the appellant but to be used to meet obligations of the advertiser rather than the appellant cf Barclays Bank Ltd v Quistclose Investments Ltd (1970) AC 567. In such a case no doubt it would be right to say that no outgoing had been incurred.

Whether the Appellant was an Agent for the Advertiser Client

46. It will be recalled that his Honour's judgment proceeded upon the basis that the appellant in contracting with Media Proprietors did so as agent for its client advertisers. As already indicated this was not a matter which the Tribunal found it necessary to consider.

47. The appellant submitted that it was not relevant to determine the question whether the appellant was agent for the advertiser so that the advertiser became liable to the media proprietor as well as the appellant. All that was necessary to determine, so the appellant submitted, was the question whether the appellant incurred a liability.

48. There seems little doubt that whether or not the advertiser was liable as principal the appellant was itself liable to make payment to the media, at least once the advertisement was published. (For the present I will leave aside the question as to whether it was proper to characterise, at the commencement of the non-cancellation period, the obligation of either the appellant or the advertiser (if the advertiser be liable) as a liability.)

49. With respect to the submissions of the appellant in my view this is not an immaterial issue. In the normal case where A contracts as agent for P, the contract entered into is one between P and the third party. The agent is not liable upon it but the liability is that of P. See Bowstead on Agency (19th ed) 351-2. Notwithstanding that A may enter into a contract as agent for P, A may agree to accept personal liability under that contract. There is nothing inconsistent with a person contracting as agent but nevertheless becoming liable personally upon the contract: Halsbury's Laws of England (4th Ed) Vol 1: AGENCY para 826. Nevertheless in such a case the contract is still primarily a contract between the principal and the third party. The obligation of the agent, while a personal obligation, is analogous to a guarantee of payment.

50. In such a case, the principal, who is also bound to make the payment, has incurred a liability and assuming that the liability otherwise satisfies the criterion of s.51(1) is entitled to a deduction under that section. It would be strange if the agent, who has also incurred a liability, would be entitled simultaneously to a deduction of the same amount.

51. The true analysis in such a case, in my opinion, is that the obligation of the agent, although it has been incurred by him, is not an allowable deduction under s.51(1) because it is not an outgoing incurred on revenue account. I leave to one side guarantee obligations incurred by banks and financial institutions who deal in money and credit, where as the High Court pointed out in Avco Financial Services Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1982] HCA 36; (1981-2) 150 CLR 510 at 530 there is a strong similarity between the getting in and turning over of trading stock by a trading company and the borrowing and on-lending of money by a finance company.

52. In the ordinary case of a trading company (not being a bank or finance company) the payment of a guarantee obligation will be on capital, rather than on revenue account, where unrelated to guarantees for the purchase of its trading stock: Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4392 and cf Case V115 88 ATC 733, Case V116 88 ATC 737 and Case V117 88 ATC 741.

53. Another way of looking at the matter is to ask the question whether the amount received by the appellant by an agent from his client in reimbursement of the outgoing would have the character of income. In my view the answer is clearly not. Nor does it matter, that the agent is paid an additional fee to incur the personal indebtedness. The amount received by an agent by way of reimbursement of an outgoing which he has paid on behalf of a principal does not have the character of income, no matter how often that reimbursement may be made. It fills the hole of capital expended and the case is thus the converse of Carapark Holdings Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1967] HCA 5; (1965-6) 115 CLR 653 at 663. It will not be a reward for services rendered; it is a mere reimbursement to the agent in respect of the capital outlaid by him. A person who carries on business as an agent for reward, if not reimbursed by his client, and as a result suffering loss, may, depending upon the circumstances of a particular case, incur a loss on revenue rather than on capital account. The claim of the appellant however is not to deduct a loss incurred but an outgoing. In the present case, if the appellant were required to meet its obligations to the media and were unable to secure reimbursement from its clients, it would incur a loss or outgoing on revenue account. That however is not the basis of the appellant's claim.

54. The problem with which I am faced in the present case is that the Tribunal reached no conclusion as to the existence of an agency relationship and the existence of such a relationship appears to have been assumed by his Honour. However the evidence upon which the existence of such a relationship could be shown was uncontroverted. It consisted solely of the contractual arrangements said by the appellant to exist between itself and its clients as constituted by the agreements which I have already described.

55. While it is true that the evidence relating to the agreements entered into with media proprietors was unsatisfactory and while it is true that there were no findings made as to matters of industry practice which might affect the construction of those agreements, I do not think that the question of agency is to be determined by reference to the agreements, which the appellant entered into with the media. Rather the question whether the appellant was authorised to incur expenditure as agent on behalf of a principal, its advertiser, must be determined by virtue of the contractual relationships entered into between the appellant and its own clients. The evidence relating to those relationships is not in dispute. Further the Tribunal, at least impliedly, in its reasons proceeded on the basis that the standard "letter of agreement" constituted the contractual relationship between the advertiser and the appellant.

56. The relationship of principal and agent will exist in cases where there is conferred upon one person (the agent) the authority or capacity to create legal relations between a person occupying the position of principal and third parties: International Harvester Co of Aust Pty Ltd v Carrigan's Hazeldene Pastoral Co [1958] HCA 16; (1958) 100 CLR 644 at 652.

57. The appellant's terms of business as at April 1984, while not stating in so many terms that the appellant is the agent for its clients in placing advertisements with media, is clearly consistent with such a view. It recognises, that ultimately, the advertising is the advertising of the client of the appellant and not of the appellant itself. It distinguishes the appellant's own business from that of the advertising of its clients. It speaks in terms of indemnification for expenses incurred by the agency, not only in placing the advertisements but also for matters such as might involve breaches of federal or state laws. In my view two conclusions follow from an examination of the agreement. Either it can be said that the appellant is in law the agent of the advertiser, or alternatively, if there be other evidence material that was not adduced, the appellant has not satisfied the burden of proof on the balance of probabilities that it is not such an agent (cf s.190(b) of the Act).

58. A conclusion that the appellant is the agent for its client advertiser is consistent with the conclusion reached by the Trade Practices Tribunal constituted by Deane J, President J F Shipton and J N Walker, in its review of the determination of the Trade Practices Commission determination (the Media Council case) to which reference has already been made. Extracts from the Tribunal's decision were admitted into evidence. I would not regard the admission of this evidence as necessarily involving a concession on the part of the appellant that the relationship is that of principal and agent, since the decision was given in 1978 and nothing that appears from the decision indicates one way or the other whether the contractual arrangements that then existed between advertising agencies and their clients was the same as that which existed between the appellant and its clients. Nevertheless it seems to me that the conclusion reached by the Tribunal in that case on the facts before it is equally applicable to the present contractual arrangements before us.

59. At p 17,617 of the Tribunal's reasons the Tribunal traced the history of the relationship between the advertising agent and the media proprietor. It seems that an advertising agent was originally a principal who purchased advertising space from a newspaper proprietor. This situation changed to the case where the advertising agent became a freelance representative of as many newspapers as he could and in some cases at least the advertising agent may well have been an agent in the strict sense for the newspaper proprietor. This situation is said by the Tribunal to have ceased by the mid-1930's so that with the advent of the accreditation system the advertising agent was clearly not an agent for the media proprietor. The Tribunal said:

"Under the Accreditation System, it is clear

that the advertising agent is not an agent for

the particular media proprietor. He contracts

with the media proprietor in his own name and

accepts personal liability in respect of

advertisements lodged. Both advertising agent

and media proprietor are, however, fully aware

that, except in the comparatively rare case

where the advertising agent is placing an

advertisement promoting his own services, the

advertising agent is acting as agent for the

advertiser whose advertising he is placing."

60. It was the Tribunal's clear view that the advertising agent was an agent in the strict sense for his advertiser client. The evidence, unsatisfactory as it was, as to the contractual arrangement between the appellant and a media proprietor does not suggest to the contrary. Indeed, to the extent that it provides for the relevant rate to be determined by the volume of advertising of the agency's client, for the space to be at the disposal of the advertiser rather than the agency, and for an indemnification in the event of breach of law from the advertiser it is clearly consistent with an agency relationship. I would not place great weight upon this evidence having regard to the qualifications that exist about it as to reading in terms relevant to industry practice. Certainly I would draw no inference from the fact that a number of the contracts in evidence are expressed in the alternative as to obligations between the media proprietor and the advertising agency or the advertiser. It may very well be that these forms are prepared on the basis that advertisements will be placed not only be advertising agencies but also by persons wishing to advertise their own goods or services. In such a case the use of an alternative is clearly a neutral matter.

61. In the event, I am of the view that even if it be correct to say that the appellant has incurred an outgoing in the relevant sense in the year of income, that outgoing was not an outgoing by it on revenue account but an outgoing on capital account and was not available to it as an allowable deduction under s.51(1) of the Act. For this reason I would disallow the appeal.

Was there an Outgoing incurred by the Appellant in the Year of Income?

62. Having regard to the conclusions which I have reached it is not strictly necessary for me to determine this issue. However in deference to the full argument between the parties, raised by the notice of contention lodged by the Commissioner asserting that no outgoing was relevantly incurred in the year of income, I will deal with it.

63. At the heart of the controversy between the parties was the submission of the Commissioner that in the years of income in question the commencement of the non-cancellation period did not subject the appellant to any obligation to pay a sum of money to the media proprietor. Pursuant to the contracts between the appellant and the media proprietors the appellant, it was said, only became obliged to pay a sum of money to the media proprietor in the event that the relevant advertisement was published or shown. Thus it was said that the obligation to pay an amount of money to the media was conditional upon publication of the advertisement and that accordingly, unless and until publication occurred, there could be no liability and accordingly no outgoing incurred for the purposes of s.51(1).

64. It may readily be conceded that if, prior to the advertisement being shown, the party to the contract with the media had sought to avoid the contract after the commencement of the non-cancellation period there would be an anticipatory breach which if not accepted would give rise only to an obligation in damages. If the breach were not accepted there could only be recovery of the contractual amount if the advertisement had been shown. If the advertisement were not shown recovery would be by way of damages which would be in an amount equal to the price if no substituted advertisement were published.

65. His Honour held below, that the obligation of the appellant was an obligation incurred by it for the purpose of s.51(1) and, if an outgoing in the relevant sense, that it would have been deductible under s.51(1) of the Act.

66. What is involved in the concept of an outgoing incurred for the purposes of s.51(1) has been the subject of considerable discussion in the authorities. The starting point of that discussion is the judgment of Dixon J (as his Honour then was) in New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at 207 where his Honour said:

"To come within that provision there must be a

loss or outgoing actually incurred. 'Incurred'

does not mean only defrayed, discharged or

borne, but rather it includes encountered, run

into or fallen upon. It is unsafe to attempt

exhaustive definitions of a conception intended

to have such a various or multifarious

application. But it does not include a loss or

expenditure which is no more than impending,

threatened, or expected."

67. The facts of that case were complex, involving a scheme for the issue of bonds by the taxpayer whereby the taxpayer covenanted with the holders of the bonds that it would within five years complete the purchase of certain land and cultivate that land for the purpose of growing flax upon it and erecting a mill, and of milling, gathering and selling of that flax. So far as is here relevant, it is sufficient to say that subscribers of the bonds had the option of paying for their bonds either cash down on application or by instalments spread over two and a half years. For four years from the date of issue of the bonds, holders were entitled to interest at the rate of 7 per cent per annum. In its accounts the taxpayer claimed to be entitled to deduct from its profit and loss account interest payable to bond holders, notwithstanding that no amounts had been paid up upon the bonds in question and claimed that the amount of that interest was "actually incurred". The taxpayer also brought to account as income the proceeds obtained from the sale of bonds as received.

68. Dixon J was of the view that upon issue of the bonds "the company came under an obligation to pay 7 per cent per annum to every bond holder who completely paid his subscription" (at 201). His Honour was of the view that it was wrong in law to include the future instalments, unpaid, of bonds sold on the assumption that the proceeds of the bonds were on revenue account. There remained the question whether the provision for future expenditure by the company in fulfilling its obligations to bond holders was deductible. The full provision claimed was clearly not deductible having regard to the decision that not the full amount of the bonds sold was to be taken as being assessable income. As his Honour said:

"At best only that proportion could be allowed

which was considered referable or attributable

to the proceeds of the bonds taken into account

as assessable income."

69. It was in these circumstances that his Honour said at 207:

"In the present case I regard the obligation to

pay interest to bondholders who, within the

four years from the date of issue, paid up the

amount of the bonds, as a definite liability

contingent only on the bondholders meeting

their instalments, that is, in the case of

bonds subscribed for in or before the

respective accounting periods the subject of

assessment. There is no reason why the future

liability should not be treated as incurred, if

otherwise it were proper to throw it against

the revenue items, as it would clearly have

been if the full face value of the bonds were

included in the assessable income. But I find

it difficult to say upon the information before

us whether any of this liability should be

considered as properly attributable to the

years in question."

70. Accordingly his Honour set aside the assessments and remitted them to the Commissioner for reassessment "so as to enable him to include only bond moneys received in the accounting periods and to allow whatever part, if any, of the deductions claimed for future interest . . . appears referable to the accounting periods under assessment". McTiernan J agreed with Dixon J. Rich J gave no separate reasons but ordered with regard to deductions claimed for future interest that the Commissioner should allow such part as was referable to the accounting periods under assessment. Starke J was of the view that the assessment had proceeded on a wrong basis and remitted the matter to the Commissioner for reassessment but did not discuss independently the question of whether the interest amounts were incurred.

71. Jenkinson J relied upon the New Zealand Flax case as authority for the view that the appellant had incurred an outgoing. His Honour said that the case established that the appellant was entitled to a deduction in respect of the future liability to pay interest, notwithstanding that the liability was contingent upon the bondholders making promised future instalment payments of the price of the bonds beyond the income year in which the deduction was claimed. His Honour's understanding of the case was challenged by the Commissioner before us. It was said that properly understood, all that the case decided was that a deduction for interest was allowable in respect of bonds which had been fully paid up in the year of income. With respect this is clearly not the case. The Court was of the view that a deduction was allowable to the appellant of interest payable on bonds notwithstanding that the bonds had not been paid up but that the quantum of that deduction was to be determined having regard to the conclusion that there should be brought into income (if it were income, and this Dixon J doubted) only the amounts or instalments paid or payable within the accounting period in question. Hence the need for the Commissioner on reassessment to determine how much of the deductions claimed for the future interest was "referable to the accounting period under assessment". Thus, although, in the event that a bondholder did not ultimately pay up his bond no amount of interest would have been payable, the High Court was of the opinion that the taxpayer had incurred a liability for an amount of interest in respect of bonds where some amount of the bond moneys had been paid, albeit that the whole amount had not been paid up.

72. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492, the question arose whether the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction for a provision made by it for annual leave prior to the employee's right to take that leave having accrued. It was held that the taxpayer was not so entitled. In the unanimous judgment of the Full Court comprising Dixon C.J., Webb, Fullagar, Kitto and Taylor JJ. the Court, after quoting what had been said in New Zealand Flax, continued at 507:

"Nothing that was decided in W. Nevill and Co Ltd

v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1937) 56

CLR 290 was intended to imply that a liability

to pay an ascertained sum is never incurred

until the sum becomes due and payable. The

question in that case was whether a sum which a

company had agreed to pay one of two joint

managing directors to induce him to retire was

an outgoing on account of capital or was

deductible. Portion of the sum was to be met

by monthly payments over a period extending

beyond the year of income and secured by

promissory notes. It was decided that the

outgoing was not on account of capital and was

deductible but so much of it as was payable

outside the year of income belonged to the

ensuing accounting period. Probably on this

minor point, to which the parties do not appear

to have attached importance, the judges were

influenced to some extent by some of the

considerations affecting their decision on the

major question and looked upon the monthly

payments not so much as deferred instalments of

an accrued liability in a lump sum but as an

attempt to spread over a period of trading an

outgoing parallel with the salary that had been

saved. But whatever be the rationale of the

decision of the point, clearly enough it is not

based on a view that no outgoing could be

incurred until actual payment was made. It is

one thing, however, to say that it is not

necessary, for the purposes of s.51(1), that an

actual disbursement should have taken place.

It is another thing to say that in the present

case the taxpayer had incurred a loss or

outgoing in the year of income in respect of

the pay of its men during the annual leave to

be taken in the ensuing accouting period by

employees whose service had not as yet

qualified them for annual leave. In respect of

those employees there was no debitum in

praesenti solvendum in futuro. There was not

an accrued obligation, whether absolute or

defeasible. There was at best an inchoate

liability in process of accrual but subject to

a variety of contingencies."

73. The question of the ability of a taxpayer to deduct a provision for long service leave and annual leave where the employees had satisfied the statutory requirements for entitlement to that leave so that entitlement had become indefeasible was considered by the High Court in Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1980-81) 144 CLR 616. The taxpayer in that case sought to deduct a provision in its accounts for the payments it would be required to make for annual leave and long service leave in the year in which the employee became entitled to such leave and in the case of long service leave in the year in which the employee's entitlement to such leave vested, although none of the employees in fact had taken long service leave in that year.

74. The ratio of the case would seem to be that the obligation of an employer to the employee in respect both of long service leave and annual holiday pay was to actually allow the relevant leave. Thus the employer was under a liability, being the ordinary liability to pay wages to an employee in respect of a period of employment, notwithstanding that the employee's entitlement to leave excused him from working or attending for work during the period. (See the judgment of Mason J at 631). Thus no pecuniary liability arose until the period of leave was entered upon. It was then, and only then, that an accrued liability to pay money arose and an outgoing was deductible under the provisions of s.51(1) of the Act.

75. Barwick C.J., with whose judgment Aickin and Wilson JJ. agreed, approached Flood's case with some caution. His Honour said at 623:

"In my opinion, the language of Dixon J in New

Zealand Flax Investment Ltd v Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at

209 needs to be carefully perused and applied.

Granted that exhaustive definition of what may

be denoted by the word 'incurred' in s.51(1)

may not be possible, there can be no warrant

for treating a liability which has not 'come

home' in the year of income, in the sense of a

pecuniary obligation which has become due, as

having been incurred in that year. Sir John

Latham's language in Emu Bay Railway Co v

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1944) 71 CLR

596 at 606 clearly enough indicates that to

satisfy the word 'incurred' in s.51(1) the

liability must be 'presently incurred and due

though not yet discharged'. The 'liability' of

which Sir John speaks is of necessity a

pecuniary liability and the word 'presently'

refers to the year of income in respect of

which a deduction is claimed. It may not

disqualify the liability as a deduction that

though due, it may be paid in a later year.

That part of Sir Owen Dixon's statement in New

Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal

Commissioner of Taxation which presently needs

emphasis is that the word 'incurred' in s.51(1)

'does not include a loss or expenditure which

is no more than pending, threatened or

expected': and I would for myself add 'no

matter how certain it is in the year of income

that that loss or expenditure will occur in the future'."

76. It may be noted that the subsequent case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd [1984] FCA 240; (1984) 2 FCR 483 concluded that a presently existing liability to pay interest in the future was deductible in the year in which that liability accrued.

77. Mason J, with whose judgment Aickin and Wilson JJ. also agreed in Nilsen Laboratories (supra), approved the words of Deane J in the Federal Court (1979) 41 FLR at 47 as follows:

". . . at the heart of the joint judgment in the

James Flood case lies a recognition of the

competing concepts of the nature of the

liability to make payments in respect of annual

leave, namely, the commercial view of that

liability as a progressive one which should be

treated as part of the cost of labour employed

from day to day and the jurisprudential view

that the liability remains the ordinary

liability to pay wages to an employee in

respect of a period of employment

notwithstanding that the employee's entitlement

to leave excuses him from working, or attending

for work during the period. Logically, the

choice between these competing views is not

affected by whether or not the qualifying

period of service has been completed. In

determining entitlement to a deduction under

s.51(1) of the Act, their Honours accepted the

jurisprudential analysis as the relevant one."

78. Mason J agreed with the comments of the Chief Justice regarding the observations of Dixon J in New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) and referred to the cases of RACV Insurance Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] VicRp 1; (1975) VR 1; Commercial Union Assurance Co of Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4186 and Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4151 without disapproval and as consistent with his Honour's view.

79. Professor Parsons in his work Income Taxation in Australia Law Book Company Ltd 1985 at 632-3 is critical of Nilsen's case so far as that case proceeds on the basis that there is a distinction between a liability to provide paid leave as against a liability to pay on the occurrence of an event that must occur. The learned author suggests that such a distinction is not helpful but is verbal only. Nevertheless the case stands for the principle that the liability in that case was not a pecuniary liability at all in the year in which a deduction was sought. The case, particularly the judgment of Mason J, also seems to make it clear that Flood's case was decided on the same basis.

80. The subsequent cases need only be shortly noted. In both the RACV case and the Commercial Union case the deduction in issue was a provision for insurance claims. In RACV it was a provision for claims incurred but not reported in relation to third party insurance. In Commercial Union what was sought to be deducted was an estimate of claims incurred but not reported which included cases where timely notice had not been given with the result that the insurer would never become legally liable under the policy unless he chose to waive the breach of the policy. In both cases it was held that the provision was deductible. In each case the insurer had, it was held, become completely subjected to liability under the policy, the event insured against having occurred and the insurer having come under a liability to indemnify the insured as provided by the policy. The fact that, in the Commercial Union case, the insurer could have relied upon the failure of insured persons to comply with the condition precedent as to notice was regarded as irrelevant having regard to the long established policy and practice of the taxpayer to pay claims whether or not the condition as to notice was complied with. Emphasis was placed on the passage in Flood's case to which I have earlier referred that it might not be necessary that the obligation in question be enforceable at law for the amount to have been incurred in the relevant sense. Both cases, subject to the qualification as to notice, were cases where the insurance company was completely subjected to the liability which although theoretically ascertainable, had not in practice been determined because claims had not yet been lodged. Nevertheless it was appropriate to allow a deduction for an estimate of the liability.

81. In the case of Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4151, Newton J of the Supreme Court of Victoria held that the taxpayer had incurred royalties payable to the State of Queensland in the year of income pursuant to an agreement with that State, notwithstanding that a determination required to be made by the Minister of Mines upon which the calculation of the royalty depended had not been made and in circumstances where an appeal as to the validity of the Act imposing the royalties was pending in the Privy Council. A submission that, as at the end of the financial year in question, the liability to pay was subject to a variety of contingencies with the result that the amount had not been incurred was rejected. Newton J at 4161 expressed the view that a liability was incurred, notwithstanding that the quantum of the liability could not be precisely ascertained, provided that it was capable of reasonable estimation. In his Honour's view the words "capable of reasonable estimation" comprehended a case where the amount was capable of approximate calculation based on probabilities (see at 4161). His Honour also accepted that a liability was incurred notwithstanding that it was defeasible.

82. What is of importance in the present case was the emphasis place by his Honour upon the fact that there was nothing more which the taxpayer could do which would affect the amount of the royalty. The fact that there remained matters left to be done at that date, e.g. the filing of a royalty return and the making of a determination by the Minister did not produce the consequence that no royalty liability had then been incurred by the taxpayer. A reasonable estimation could still have been made as at the end of the year of income of the amount of the liability.

83. More recently, in Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4392, the Full Court of this Court was called upon to consider the deductibility of an outgoing pursuant to a guarantee given by a parent company in respect of the outstanding income tax of its subsidiaries in liquidation. The Court held that the amount of the guarantee was not incurred until a later year when the income tax liability of the subsidiary was finally determined, the High Court having in that year delivered the judgment in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v St Hubert's Island Pty Ltd (in liquidation) (1977-8) 138 CLR 210. Prior to the delivery of judgment it was said that the liability under the guarantee was but contingent pending or threatened and not incurred.

84. The case turned largely upon the construction of the guarantee in question, that being an undertaking to pay income tax to which the subsidiary might become lawfully liable in the course of winding up. "to the extent to which" the liquidator of the companies failed to so do. Having regard to the right of a taxpayer to request an extension of time for payment of income tax and the right to appeal by a taxpayer dissatisfied with an assessment, there had been no "failure" by the liquidator of the subsidiary. Further, accountancy evidence given in the case reinforced this conclusion, that evidence being that it was only proper to include a contingent liability in the accounts where there is a reasonable probability of the liability maturing into an absolute indebtedness. That, in accordance with the evidence, was not until the decision of the High Court had been delivered, resolving the controversy upon which the liability of the subsidiaries depended.

85. As appears above, the courts have consistently taken the view that it is unsafe to attempt an exhaustive definition of a term such as "incurred" in s.51(1): New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at 207 per Dixon J, Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4392 at 4400.

86. Nevertheless a review of the cases to which I have referred reveals a number of propositions that may now be taken to have been accepted. These, in my view are:-

1. An outgoing may be incurred notwithstanding the

amount is not paid in the year of income provided

the taxpayer has "completely subjected himself" to

that liability: Flood's case (supra) at 507;

2. An outgoing may be incurred notwithstanding that at

the end of the year of income it represents a

present liability then due although payable in the

future: Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd v

Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] FCA 240; (1984) 2 FCR 483;

3. An outgoing not representing a pecuniary liability

of the year of income will not be incurred,

notwithstanding that it is certain that the outgoing

will arise as a pecuniary liability in the future:

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Pty

Ltd (supra) and Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty

Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra);

4. A reasonable estimate of an outgoing payable in the

future where the amount of that outgoing cannot be

ascertained with precision at the end of the year of

income will be incurred where it represents a

pecuniary liability encountered in the year of

income but provided it is capable of reasonable

estimation. The amount will not be incurred in the

year in which the precise quantum of the liability

is ascertained: RACV Insurance Pty Ltd v Federal

Commissioner of Taxation (supra), Commercial Union

Assurance Co of Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner

of Taxation (supra) and Commonwealth Aluminium

Corporation Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra);

5. An outgoing may be incurred in the year of income,

notwithstanding that it is defeasible: Flood's case

(supra) at 506-7;

6. An outgoing may be incurred notwithstanding that

there is no legal liability to make payment at all

at least where commercially that amount is certain

to be paid in the future, provided that it is

otherwise "incurred": Commercial Union case (supra);

7. An outgoing will not be incurred in the year of

income where it is no more than contingent, pending,

threatened or expected no matter how certain it may

be in the year of income that the loss or

expenditure will occur in the future: Flood's case

(supra) at 507-8; Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v Federal

Commissioner of Taxation (supra) at 4400.

87. Although the above propositions would seem to be well-established there is some difficulty in determining what is meant by the phrase "the taxpayer has completely subjected himself to a liability" and for that matter what is meant by the expression "contingent" as used in such cases.

88. For the Commissioner in the present case it was submitted, either that the obligation of the appellant to pay the media proprietors after the non-cancellation period was "contingent" or alternatively that it was not a liability at all. It was said that there was, at best, an inchoate liability in process of accrual but subject to a variety of contingencies, especially publication. It was submitted that "at most it could be said that it had become certain that, in due course when further events occurred the liability to pay the price would arise".

89. It is true that at the expiration of the non-cancellation period the taxpayer had done everything itself necessary to become liable to the media proprietors for payment of advertisements placed. It is also true that if the client ultimately decided the advertisement should not proceed, it or the agency on its behalf could negotiate with the media proprietors and reach some arrangement which may not require payment for the advertisement to be made. That ability, however, is not different from the right of all parties to a contract to renegotiate it; a liability may be incurred notwithstanding that it, in that sense, is defeasible.

90. The strength of the respondent Commissioner's argument is the proposition that under the contractual arrangements with the media proprietors there is no liability to pay the media proprietor unless an advertisement is in fact published. Non-publication of the advertisement could arise for a variety of reasons, including strikes or default on the part of the media proprietor in its contract.

91. Thus it is said that publication of the advertisement is a condition precedent to the liability of the appellant to pay the media proprietors so that all that can be said is that there exists in the year of income the possibility of a liability in the future: cf Emu Bay Railway Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 28; (1944) 71 CLR 596 at 606 per Latham C.J. Whether that is the case must depend upon the terms of the relevant contracts, which incorporate by reference Rule 24(a) of the Media Council's Accreditation Rules.

92. Rule 24(a) purports to create a liability upon placement of the advertisement, that liability being expressed as being for payment "due . . . for all advertising material published, broadcast or telecast" and requires payment to be made "not later than the thirtieth day of the month following that in which such advertising is published". There is perhaps some ambiguity in the way the rule is expressed but it seems to me that the proper construction of the rule is that the liability of the agent is conditional upon publication of the advertisement and that unless and until that publication occurs there is no liability. While, commercially, there is every prospect that the advertisement will be published, that is not the point. the liability still remains merely "threatened" and "impending" rather than "encountered" to use the metaphorical language of the cases.

93. Counsel for the Commissioner sought to press as an analogy the case of a trader who at the end of the year of income, contracts to purchase goods for delivery in the next year with the liability for payment not arising until delivery. In my view it is not helpful to the resolution of the present appeal to consider other factual circumstances which have their own difficulties. It may well be that a contract for the sale of goods may stand in a different position altogether from a contract for services and even if that be not so it would be necessary to consider carefully the wording of the particular contract to determine whether there was any liability incurred in the year of income.

94. Brief comment should be made as to the accounting evidence. The appellant, for reasons connected with its need to comply with the requirements of the Accreditation Rules as to the maintenance of ratios of capital to liabilities did not include in its balance sheet as liabilities at balance date the amounts here said to have been incurred to media proprietors not did it bring into its accounts as assets the right to reimbursement of these amounts. Further, the appellant did not show in its profit and loss account the liabilities here said to have been incurred as a charge against its profits of the year.

95. Once an advertisement appeared, the appellant at the end of that month raised an invoice to its client advertiser (invoices were rendered separately by media in respect of each client). The appellant would receive an invoice from the media proprietor which was paid within the time allowed to obtain commission and in its accounts would show the amount payable for the advertisement net of commission as a liability, bringing then to account the commission as income.

96. Evidence was given by a chartered accountant, a partner in Arthur Andersen and Co (Mr Paton) with experience as an auditor of other advertising agencies. That evidence suggested that it was the general practice of advertising agencies to account in the same way as the appellant did, although Mr Paton encouraged his clients to indicate the dollar value of the "non cancellable media commitment" in their accounts by way of note. Mr Paton pointed out that failure to treat the amounts as liabilities in the accounts was not "material" as it had no effect on income and the debit item would in any event be cancelled out by the credit item of reimbursement.

97. Evidence was also given by Professor Pearson of the Department of Accounting at Monash University who had until October 1988 been the Deputy Chairman of the Accounting Standards Board. The effect of that evidence, as I understand it, was that having regard to the matching principle, the amounts here claimed as incurred would be allocated by him to the next financial year. He would not make entries for the amounts booked with media until invoices had been received. It would be only where it was clear that a bad debt was looming because there was a real risk of the agency not being reimbursed that Professor Pearson would take into account "the liability". He was of the view that the question whether the "amount" media charges should be brought to account depended upon whether the agency was a true agent in contracting with the media proprietors.

98. Accounting evidence cannot be substituted for the test laid down by s.51(1): Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492 at 506-7. Nevertheless as the Full Court of this Court pointed out in Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4392 at 4399:

" . . . the tendency of judicial decision has been

to place increasing reliance upon the concepts

of business and the principles and practices of

commercial accountancy, not only in the

ascertainment of income, but also in the

ascertainment of expenditure: C of T (SA) v

Executor Trustee and Agency Co of South Australia

Ltd [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 at 153; Federal

Commissioner of Taxation v Australian Guarantee

Corporation Ltd (1984) 84 ATC 4642; (1984) 54

ALR 209."

99. Both the appellant's own accounting treatment and the evidence of Mr Paton and Professor Pearson, while not determinative, particularly in a case where the accounting treatment depends upon the operation of the matching principle, are consistent with the view that I have already reached namely that each of the amounts in issue in the present appeal was not incurred until the advertisements to which it related had appeared, when for the first time the liability became one to which the agency was definitively committed. That evidence, especially where the agency is a true agent, is also supportive of the view that the non-cancellable amounts are not properly to be brought into the profit and loss account as a revenue item at all. Rather it is only when there is a loss incurred, by failure of a client to reimburse the expenditure, that a debit item appears in the profit and loss account.

100. In my view the appeal should be dismissed with costs.
