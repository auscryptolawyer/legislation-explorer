CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions – repairs. Kitchen of rented premises refurbished after vacation of tenants. Whether refurbishment extended beyond repairs so as to constitute improvements.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – section 53

Taxation Administration Act 1953 – section 14ZZK



W Thomas & Co Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1965] HCA 54; 115 CLR 58

Morcom and Others v Campbell-Johnson and Others [1955] 3 All ER 264

Case 10/98 98 ATC 171

Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v A.B. Donald Limited (1965) 9 A.I.T.R. 501



REASONS FOR DECISION



28 May 1999
SENIOR MEMBER M D ALLEN




  1. By application lodged with the Tribunal on 16 November 1998 the Applicant sought review of a decision by the Respondent to disallow an objection dated 10 July 1998 against a Notice of Assessment issued on 1 July 1998 in respect of the tax year ended 30 June 1996.
  2. The sole issue before me in this matter was concisely set out in an agreed Statement of Issues tendered by the parties. Paragraph 1 of that statement reads:
“The sole issue in dispute is the nature of expenditure incurred on the installation of new kitchen cupboards in the rental property, whether this expenditure constitutes a revenue expenditure as a repair, or is an outlay of capital by way of improvement.”


  1. The Applicant is the owner of a dwelling house which has since January 1994 been let to tenants, that is to say, it has become an income producing asset in the Applicant’s hands.
  2. After the vacation of the premises by tenants in August 1995, it became necessary to effect repairs to the kitchen of the house. The Applicant was advised of the necessity to undertake these repairs by her letting agents (see Document T15 page 79) and she herself gave evidence of damage caused by tenants, including burn marks on laminated bench tops, missing handles on cupboard doors and chips and scratches to surfaces. In addition, a rat had chewed through a dishwasher hose, leading to flooding which had caused water damage to cupboards including swelling of doors and shelves.
  3. At the same time that refurbishment of the kitchen cupboards was undertaken, the Applicant caused other work to be undertaken to the dwelling house. The total cost of all work was approximately $27000.00 and of this amount, the sum of $5279.00 is in dispute. This sum represents $4779.00 for the new cupboards and bench tops and $500.00 for removal of the old cupboards and installation of the new.
  4. The installation of new cupboards was not the only work undertaken in the kitchen. After the tenants left in August 1995, the Applicant took the opportunity to replace wall tiles, install new floor coverings and white goods, fit new architraves, skirtings and door reveals, together with other structural work.
  5. Section 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) states:
“(1) [Deductable expenditure] Expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income for repairs, not being expenditure of a capital nature, to any premises, or part of premises, plant, machinery, implements, utensils, rolling stock, or articles held, occupied or used by him for the purpose of producing assessable income, or in carrying on a business for that purpose, shall be an allowable deduction.”


  1. Whereas the Applicant’s tax agent referred to Tax Ruling No 97/23 in support of a claim that the installation of the new cupboards constituted a repair, I do not consider that that ruling assists his case. At paragraph 55 the ruling states that the character of a repair does not necessarily change because it is carried out at the same time as an improvement. This, however, begs the question of what is a repair and what is an improvement.
  2. The commencing point of any discussion of repairs vis-as-vis improvements must be the remarks of Windeyer J in W Thomas & Co Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1965] HCA 54; 115 CLR 58 at 72, namely:
“The works in question can all be fairly described as repairs to the building. They were done to make good a deterioration that had occurred by ordinary wear and tear or by the operation of natural causes during the passage of time. I was referred to various definitions of the word ‘repair’ and to a number of cases illustrative of its meaning. I need not discuss them. Cases arising between landlord and tenant are only of indirect assistance. And nothing much is to be gained by comparing the particular facts of other cases with the facts of this case. And understanding the concept of ‘repair’ is not much aided by contrasting that word with other words that in themselves gain only by contrast whatever precision of meaning they have in this field. The words ‘repair’ and ‘improvement’ may for some purposes connote contrasting concepts; but obviously repairing a thing improves the condition it was in immediately before repair. It may sometimes be convenient for some purposes to contrast a ‘repair’ with a ‘replacement’ or a ‘renewal’. But repairs to a whole are often made by the replacement of worn-out parts by new parts. Repair involves a restoration of a thing to a condition it formerly had without changing its character. But in the case of a thing considered from the point of view of its use as distinct from its appearance, it is restoration of efficiency in function rather than exact repetition of form or material that is significant. Whether or not work done upon a thing is aptly described as a repair of that thing is thus a question of fact and degree. But the answer to that question does not of itself decide whether the expenditure on the work is properly to be considered as an outgoing upon capital account or upon revenue account. And that is what must be decided when the question is whether that expenditure is an allowable deduction in the ascertainment of taxable income.”


  1. Again, the dilemma involved in creating a test to distinguish repairs from improvements was referred to by Denning LJ (as he then was) in Morcom and Others v Campbell-Johnson and Others [1955] 3 All ER 264 at 266, namely:
“It seems to me that the test, so far as one can give any test in these matters, is this: If the work which is done is the provision of something new for the benefit of the occupier, that is, properly speaking, an improvement; but if it is only the replacement of something already there, which has become dilapidated or worn out, then, albeit that it is a replacement by its modern equivalent, it comes within the category of repairs and not improvements.”


I note, however, the submissions by Ms Rands, who appeared for the Respondent, that the remarks of Denning LJ should be approached with caution as the Court of Appeal was not dealing with a taxation statute but a case under a Rent Restriction Act. Notwithstanding this caveat there is in the above remarks, like most of what fell from that learned Law Lord, eminent good sense.

  1. Senior Member Beddoe in Case 10/98 98 ATC 171 at 177 referred to Windeyer J’s adoption in W Thomas & Co Pty Ltd supra of the remarks of Woodhouse J in Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v A.B. Donald Limited (1965) 9 A.I.T.R. 501 at 506. The relevant part of Woodhouse J’s judgment for present purposes reads:
“... I take the view that the restoration of defects in an asset cannot be classed as a revenue charge ... unless the work is limited to those defects and does not become enlarged into such a reconstruction that a permanent improvement in quality has been effected.”


  1. Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (as amended) provides that the onus of showing that a taxpayer’s assessment is excessive, lies upon the Applicant.
  2. In this matter the Applicant, following the refurbishment of the kitchen, was able to obtain a higher rent than previous for the said premises. In addition the replacement of the kitchen cupboards was undertaken as part of a refurbishment of the whole kitchen. Whereas I can understand the good sense in replacing the existing dilapidated cupboards with new ones, the test is, as stated by Woodhouse J in Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v A.B. Donald Limited supra whether the work has gone beyond a mere remedying of defects.
  3. In this matter I am not satisfied that the work undertaken did not enlarge itself into an improvement. It may well be that the only practical way of effecting repairs was to replace the whole. I can quite understand that, for example, cupboards or drawers can become warped so that merely to replace a door does not remedy the underlying defect. Similarly, to replace water damage shelving may require a complete restructuring of the total fabric. None of these matters were explored in evidence in any particularity so that at the end of the day, I am simply left wondering whether or not it may have been possible to effect repairs to the existing cupboards and bench-tops rather than a total replacement. As stated above, I can understand the commercial and practical good sense in replacing the cupboards and bench-tops but that is not the question I have to answer.
  4. So far as the issue in dispute before me is concerned, I am satisfied that the Applicant has not made out her case. I note that the original objection decision remitted penalties relating to the kitchen cupboards. Other penalties were not remitted and I was informed by both parties that the question of penalties in this matter did not form part of the review before me. The decision under review will therefore be affirmed.
