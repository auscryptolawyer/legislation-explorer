Robert David Boardman and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 211; (1993) 30 ALD 619 (8 July 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ROBERT DAVID BOARDMAN v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S92/291

AAT No. 8832

Number of pages - 12

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 211; (1993) 30 ALD 619

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.H. Burns(1) (Deputy President), B.C. Lock(1) (Member) and D.L. Davies(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 s.37

Superannuation Act, 1922

Superannuation Act, 1976 ss.137(1), 157(1)

Superannuation Act, 1990

HEARING

ADELAIDE, 16 February 1993 and 10 March 1993

8:7:1993

Applicant unrepresented

Counsel for the Respondent: Ms T. Munoz

Solicitors for the Respondent: Review and Legal Services Branch,

Retirement Benefits Office

ORDER

The decision of the Tribunal is that the decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 7 March 1990 under s.137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.

DECISION

B.H. BURNS, B.C. LOCK AND D.L. DAVIES This is an application to review a decision of 15 September 1991 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation. The decision, made under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act, 1976 ('the Act') was that the Commissioner declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 7 March 1990 under s.137(1) of the Act should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section of the Act.

2. At the hearing of this application, the applicant appeared in person and the respondent was represented by Ms Munoz.

3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 (the 'T' documents), together with exhibits tendered by the parties which comprised in the main, forms, booklets and circulars concerning superanuation. The Tribunal heard oral evidence from the applicant who also called Evan Bomford. The respondent called Kenneth Charles Stidworthy and Robert Furner.

4. Section 137 of the Act is as follows:

's.137. (1) A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled

to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance

with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue

of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58 (3), to

have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an

eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner,

that this Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this

section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee,

the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he

ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is

of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice

in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such

further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration

of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him:

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the

prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer;

and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed

period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation

to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned.

(4) Subsection (3) does not apply:

(a) if a person entitled to make an election under this section

dies without making such an election and the election is made by

another person under subsection 157 (2A); or

(b) if a person who has made an election under this section dies,

without giving notice under that subsection, before the end of the

period for the giving of the notice by the person.'

5. The effect of making an election under this section where a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of resignation, is that Division 3 of Part IX of the Act will then apply. This Division provides for various benefits which can be chosen by an employee who resigns, having regard to that employee's particular circumstances. For example, s.135 of the Act provides for a transfer value to be paid. The benefit which Mr Boardman seeks is entitled deferred benefits as provided for by s.136 of the Act. This benefit is more commonly referred to as preservation of superannuation rights ('preservation'). Various time limits are prescribed in Division 3 of the Act, for example, a transfer value is payable if an election is made within 3 months. Pursuant to s.137 of the Act, the deferred benefit which the applicant now seeks is dependent upon an election being made within 21 days of the applicant ceasing to be an eligible employee or within one month before that event. The actual election which the applicant made was contained in a letter dated 7 March 1990, some 11 years after his earlier resignation.

6. Section 157(1) of the Act, however, contains a power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. The section is as follows:

's.157. (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where

an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration

of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the

election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the

circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should

be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be

treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the

election shall have effect accordingly.'

7. The Tribunal makes the following findings of fact which are not in dispute. The applicant who was born on 7 November 1949, became a contributor under the Superannuation Act, 1922 (the superseded Act) on 30 July 1970 (age 20 years), by virtue of his employment with the then Department of Civil Aviation, and at the date of commencement of the Act on 1 July 1976 was an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act. In a letter to the Regional Director for the SA/NT Region of the (then) Department of Transport dated 17 April 1979, he resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service ('CPS') in the following terms:

'...

I hereby submit my resignation from the Public Service, to take

effect from the close of business on 28 April 1979.

I request that all moneys owing to me at the time of resignation

to be made available to me, in cash, at Adelaide airport, on payday

10-5-1979.'

The applicant completed a form S2A, entitled - 'Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights.' (Although dated 27 May 1979 by the applicant, it was agreed between the parties that the form was actually signed in early May 1979 or, alternatively, that the date should read 27 April 1979.) In so doing, the applicant applied for a refund of accumulated contributions by deleting from the form S2A two other options viz: (1) election for the preservation of superannuation rights and (2) election for a payment of a lump sum benefit. (A 'lump sum benefit' is not a resignation benefit and its meaning is fully discussed later in these reasons.)

A payment of accumulated contributions amounting to a gross payment of $2,717.29 was authorised to be made on 21 May 1979. Mr Boardman ceased to be an eligible employee on 28 April 1989, when he was aged 29 years.

8. The applicant again became an eligible employee for purposes of the Act when he rejoined the Department of Transport on 31 March 1980 (age 30 years). He had been out of the CPS for a period of 11 months and as a consequence, had made no contributions to the superannuation scheme established under the Act during this period.

9. The circumstances surrounding the signing of the form S2A go to the heart of the application for review, and the evidence before the Tribunal dealt with those circumstances at some length. Before turning to the circumstances in question, we consider that it would be useful at this stage to briefly follow the applicant's career path in the Commonwealth Public Service ('CPS') as it was before his resignation, and then from the time he recommenced in the Department of Transport in March 1980.

10. We also make the following findings of fact which are not in dispute. The applicant was first appointed to the CPS as a clerk class 1 in the office of the Public Service Board in Darwin on 2 January 1970. He was soon after successful in being selected for training as an Air Traffic Controller in the then Department of Civil Aviation. He underwent a 2 year course in Perth and continued his training in Melbourne and in Sydney. In 1973 he was transferred to Canberra, sometimes acting as an Air Traffic Controller grade 3. He later accepted promotion to Darwin and remembers the point in time as being a little over a year after Cyclone Tracey had destroyed that town. He was married just before commencing his training and by 1975 he had 2 children, one of whom was seriously afflicted with cerebral palsy. This was not discovered until late in 1975 or early 1976, when the child was 4 months old. Understandably, the illness of his child and the need to work shift work in these circumstances placed pressures on his family life. In September 1977 he sought and was given a transfer to Adelaide so as to have access to treatment for his child at a spastic centre. He was never satisfied with the work he was doing in Adelaide. He described it as 'dreadful' because it involved long range control, outside of radar control.

11. We find that at the time when the applicant was under pressure at home and at work, he made a mistake performing his air traffic control duties in that he misidentified an aircraft. Although, it did not result in any serious outcome, he was aware that it could have. He was reported to his superiors and in the end result was taken off higher duty allowance and given Air Traffic Controller Class 1 duties to perform. He felt totally 'wasted', and was overcome by the feeling that 'he had to get out'. As a result, he resigned his position with the view to taking on a new career selling life assurance, including superannuation policies. He did this type of work for 10 months. At the end of this time, he applied for reappointment to the Public Service as a clerk. He was appointed clerk class 1 in the Personnel Section Department of Transport, (previously Department of Civil Aviation) Adelaide. It is ironic that it is the advice given by an officer of this section, at the time of the applicant's resignation which is now the pivotal issue in this application for review.

12. The applicant went about his duties with proper diligence and discovered that he had a talent for personnel type work and for dealing with people in personnel related matters, including conditions of service, sick leave, recreation leave, long service leave and superannuation matters. As a result, he received promotions which eventually took him away from the Department of Transport to his current position as the Personnel Officer, Bureau of Meteorology, Adelaide. During these years, he developed an interest and skill in the Commonwealth superannuation scheme ('CSS') established under the Act. In January 1989 this interest led to the preparation and publication by the applicant and a colleague, Evan Bomford, of a booklet entitled 'The Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme and you, the facts, the benefits and the costs' (T48/122-131). The publication and distribution of the booklet was sponsored by CPS Financial Services Pty Ltd and, although it contained a disclaimer as to its complete correctness and currency, it was generally well received by those with any knowledge of the superannuation scheme established under the Act.

13. The booklet contained a section on Resignation Benefits and in that section it outlined the four options available to an eligible employee on resignation. Option 3 - 'Leave it in the Scheme' dealt with the preservation of superannuation rights.

14. The Superannuation Act, 1990, introduced the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme (PSS). The applicant and Mr Bomford again worked together to compile and publish another booklet entitled 'Commonwealth Superannuation - your comprehensive guide to Commonwealth Superannuation' (T47/106-121). This booklet was sponsored by the State Bank of South Australia and while its date of publication is not clear, the words in the introduction suggest it was some time in 1990. The PSS came into operation on 1 July 1990 and although we are not directly concerned with this scheme, it is relevant in regard to the extensive knowledge which the applicant has of superannuation matters.

15. Although Mr Bomford and the applicant were collaborators in the compilation of these booklets, they had previously been competitors on at least two occasions for jobs in the Public Service dealing with superannuation. On the first occasion, Mr Bomford was the successful applicant but on the second, the applicant was the successful one. This later application was for a position with the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office in Canberra, which of course is the office of the respondent in this matter. The applicant worked in this office from March 1990 to 20 October 1992. The position was that of national adviser and involved travelling around the country, presenting seminars to staff on the CSS and the PSS schemes. The interviews for these positions were conducted in Canberra, February 1990, and Mr Bomford and the applicant travelled together from Adelaide to Canberra for them.

16. We now turn to the matters in contention. The applicant gave evidence that when he resigned in April 1979, he was given incorrect information as to the options available to him upon resignation, and was given no information at all about the preservation of his superannuation rights. He also gave evidence that although he became aware of the preservation rights during his research and collaboration with Mr Bomford in compiling the first booklet, he did not become aware of s.157 of the Act and its implications until February 1990.

17. On 7 March 1990, Mr Boardman wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office ('RBO') which at this stage was also his employer. He stated:

'...

In May 1979 I resigned from the Department of Transport in Adelaide.

At the time of that resignation, I was provided with a yellow form

(form S2A) and advised that I was entitled to a refund of my

superannuation contributions, plus interest.

The form noted 3 choices, Lump Sum Benefit, Refund or Preservation.

I asked at the time what each meant and was advised that the form

was used for several purposes and that I should cross out Lump Sum

Benefit and Preservation, as they did not apply to resignations.

In good faith, I did as advised, signed the form, and subsequently

received the refund of contributions and interest totalling about

$2,700.

...

I was not at any time told of this provision (preservation) by the

Department of Transport, nor was I supplied with any information

that might have enabled me to find out about this provision. In

retrospect, the Personnel staff at that Department apparently had

very little knowledge of the superannuation provisions as I was

specifically told that preservation did not apply to resignations.'

It is worth noting that although the applicant commenced working in

1980 in the section in which the person who allegedly gave this

misleading information should have been working, he was unable to

identify the person in question.

18. In evidence the applicant said (at pp 17-18):

'... And that was the reason for my resignation at that time, so

it wasn't really voluntary. It was partially forced. So I was

under some stress at the time and I needed to get into something

different, so I started selling life insurance and superannuation.

Even then, I can say that I was intensely interested in the

superannuation industry. My father was a Commonwealth public

servant. I knew that he had good benefits available to him on

retirement so I was aware of what I was giving up in resigning.

So that's why when I was handed that bit of paper, the S2A, I asked

what three items meant on and I was given a very clear indication

that only one option applied to resignation, that the others

applied - well, the assumption was, from what he said, that the

others applied to the other superannuation scheme that they used

them for, when in fact, only one of the others applied to the

problem of account, (sic) and the preservation option which is the

third one on the form, applied to resignations.'

(The phrase 'applied to the problem of account' is transcribed incorrectly; it should read 'applied to the PROVIDENT account'.)

19. Exhibit R6 is the application form for refund which the applicant signed. It is numbered FORM S2A and is headed:

'COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Superannuation Act

APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS

LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR

ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS

Note: This application form should be completed by a

contributor who resigns or a contributor who retires

and who elects to exercise an option to take a Lump

Sum Benefit in lieu of a pension entitlement.

This form should not be completed by a contributor who

is applying for a pension and a refund of accumulated

contributions or supplementary contributions.'

20. Ms Munoz for the respondent questioned the applicant at some length as to exactly where it was that he signed the form. The applicant's evidence was that it was signed at the headquarters of the Department of Transport in Da Costa Building, Grenfell Street, Adelaide. His signature on the S2A (exhibit R6) was, however, witnessed by one 'Peter Thomas JP' and the place was described as 'Adelaide Airport, West Beach, SA'.

21. The respondent challenged the fact that the applicant was misinformed and called Mr Kenneth Charles Stidworthy and Mr Robert Furner, to give evidence before the Tribunal.

22. Mr Stidworthy gave evidence that in 1979, he was senior assessor in charge of the Industrial Assessing Unit in the Department of Transport, Adelaide. He had five class 4 clerks - industrial assessors working for him. They were supported by two class 2-3 clerks who were called distributors and a clerical assistant grade 4 who was responsible for recording details on leave history cards. He told the Tribunal (at p 58 of the transcript), that 'the five industrial assessors were responsible for superannuation matters'. He also told the Tribunal that 'resignations' were processed in this section.

In regard to the procedures which took place within the section when new superannuation circulars were received, Mr Stidworthy gave evidence as follows (p 59 of the transcript):

'Q. Now, were you aware of superannuation circulars issued by the

then Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office?

A. Yes, I used to - the circulars all used to come to me as a

senior assessor and I was responsible for determining whether there

was a need to circulate the information amongst the rest of the

staff or ... inaudible ... just a mechanical type thing which was

for our information to advise us of new procedures. If there was -

information on the circular was of general interest we used to

summarise it and include in our administrative circulars for

circulation thoughout the region.'

23. In respect of Superannuation Circular 1978/S2 (exhibit R2), he told the Tribunal that when it was received, copies of it were distributed to each assessor and he put a handwritten minute on the file 'Superannuation Instructions' (exhibit R3) at folio 209. That minute read as follows:

'MINUTE

MPSI (1)

MPSI (2)

MPSI (3)

MPSI (4)

MPSI (5)

Reference F207 and 208

Superannuation Board Circulars 1978/S2 and S3

Copies of these circulars have already been passed to you.

1978/S2

An Admin circular has been issued advising staff that the procedures

for the preservation of super rights and for them to contact their

assessor if they consider they are eligible. Please discuss with me

if you encounter any problems.

1978/S3

This circular is self explanatory. Orders have been submitted for

the new forms.

(Signed.....K.C. Stidworthy)

MPSS

14/8/78'

Each of the industrial assessors initialled the file as having noted folio 209 on 15/8/78. The Administrative Circular referred to is at folio 211 of R3 and reads as follows:

'ADMINISTRATIVE CIRCULAR

(SA/NT Region)

(Expiry Date : 18.11.78)

No. 52

17 August 1978

PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS

The Australian Government Retirements Benefits Officer has

promulgated the procedures covering the preservation of

superannuation rights on transfer to or from Australian Government

employment.

These procedures allow new employees to increase their

prospective pension entitlements by having their contribution

to the superannuation system of their previous employer transferred

to the Superannuation Fund.

Any officer interested in exercising these rights should contact

their Assessor, Personnel and Establishments Section to determine

their eligibility.

S366/14/1'

The circular contained two other pages of general information to staff but no other references to superannuation matters. Mr Stidworthy told the Tribunal that that paragraph was the only information he distributed about preservation and that he did not attach the full circular (R2). Mr Stidworthy agreed that the Administrative Circular said nothing about resignations. He was of the view that the Circular was 'rather for people who might have been transferring in or out of the Public Service', (transcript p 72). Mr Stidworthy gave evidence that preservation 'was one of the less commonly used and understood benefits. I don't recall ever seeing ... an application for preservation'. He also stated: 'I don't know anybody who took the preservation option' (transcript p 64).

24. Mr Stidworthy was adamant that he had an efficient stable team in those 5 class 4 Industrial Assessors, and would rate their knowledge of superannuation to be as good as any within the CPS in South Australia. He gave evidence that the five were divided alphabetically and that this meant that Mr Boardman's assessor in 1979 would have been Gordon Crozer, a man with years of experience prior to 1979, (transcript p 80). Mr Stidworthy told the Tribunal that although he tried to rotate these industrial assessors between these alphabetical groupings, Mr Crozer always stayed in the same section being 'A-F'. Furthermore, he told the Tribunal that Mr Crozer would be the assessor who in normal working circumstances would have advised the applicant about his superannuation rights. The applicant, however, gave evidence that he knew Mr Crozer and he was not the person who advised him. When questioned further about whether or not anyone in his section could have given the wrong information to the applicant. Mr Stidworthy said, (transcript p 75):

'... I am not saying it didn't or wouldn't occur, however, the

quality of people I had working for me then is such that I think

if someone asked them a question that they didn't know they would

have asked one of their peers or they would make inquiries to find

out further themselves, that's what they were encouraged to do. We

didn't go accounting for every phone call they made whether it was

STD or whatever.

MR LOCK: But would you agree, Mr Stidworthy, that it was quite

possible that one of those assessors thought he knew and did not

see the need to ask anybody?

A. Well, that is not impossible either.

THE D. PRESIDENT: And I think you have already told us that

preservation of benefits on resignation was - well, you had

never heard of one, for a start?

A. I don't recall us processing them.

Q. Would not that indicate that on resignation there was a

pattern of behaviour, namely, that people were, for one reason

or another, taking their money and going?

A. Yes.

Q. Which can indicate not only a preference by people to do that

of three options, but it might also indicate that they thought that

was perhaps the only option that they had, that when you resign that

was the end of super?

A. I don't really know. From my recollection and my knowledge of

people leaving, they were generally leaving to go and do something

else and they generally needed - they had, they relied upon that

money ---

Q. To whatever?

A. --- to go and start that little business they wanted to start,

or move to wherever they were going to move to.'

25. The Tribunal found Mr Stidworthy to be an impressive witness. He was at all times candid in his answers and did his best to remain objective as to the practices of his department and section. We have accepted Mr Stidworthy's evidence except wherever it might be said to be at variance with the evidence given by Mr Boardman. We found Mr Boardman to be a very impressive witness, not only as to his demeanour but as to the accuracy of his account of the events in question. We have no hesitation in accepting Mr Boardman's evidence.

26. The respondent also called Mr Robert Furner who, at the time of the applicant's resignation, was one of the class 4 Industrial Assessors. He impressed the Tribunal as a competent personnel officer but one whose knowledge of the detail associated with the Superannuation Act, particularly the options available to a contributor on resignation, was not as complete as it might have been. He gave a good description of the preservation benefit and said he was aware of the benefit in 1979 and would have provided the same description to anyone who asked him for it in 1979. However, he went on to say that he had never 'struck one case of somebody electing preservation in the whole time I was there', (transcript p 87). He later qualified this statement when he told the Tribunal that he did see one election for preservation for in 1988, and that that person had made up his mind before he came to him.

Given Mr Stidworthy's evidence that generally personnel officers learnt about superannuation by actually handling the individual cases, which evidence was confirmed by Mr Furner when he talked about team meetings and discussions with his colleagues, it seems to the Tribunal that as Mr Furner had only dealt with two cases in his whole career, his knowledge of preservation in 1979 would have been at the least, minimal. The Tribunal accepts, based on the description that Mr Furner gave us, that he is now aware of the preservation provision. We are not satisfied that he had the same awareness of it in 1979.

27. In regard to the issue of the knowledge of personnel clerks on preservation, Mr Evan Bomford was asked when he first became aware of the preservation rights. He gave evidence as follows (transcript pp 172-173):

'Q. Can I ask when you first became aware of Preservation, just

Preservation?

A. Preservation first became an issue for me, I believe, in

January 1986 and that was following the initial superannuation

information session that I ran out at the STO Salisbury. Somebody

asked me about Preservation. I had never heard of if before. I

didn't know what on earth they were talking about. I couldn't

believe there was any such thing, you know. It was my understanding

that when one left, one obtained a refund that that was it, but

because I thought that if I was in a position of providing

information, I should find out, I proceeded to hunt for information.

THE D. PRESIDENT: How did you answer the questions at the seminar?

A. Well, it actually wasn't asked directly at the seminar.

Somebody approached me after the seminar and asked me had I ever

heard of Preservation, and I reluctantly had to admit that I'd

never heard of it, but I would pursue the issue for them.

Q. How did you pursue it?

A. Well, my initial course was to ask everybody I was working

with - and at that time there was - I'm not sure of the exact

numbers but I believe somewhere between about 40 and 55 people

actually working in the whole human resources area out at the STO

at Salisbury and I individually went and asked every one of them if

they knew anything of Preservation or if they'd heard of it and

there was only one person who admitted to either knowing anything

about it or having heard anything and that person referred me to a

registry file and said they believed they'd read something when the

new scheme was introduced in 1976. On asking registry, they at

first denied that there was any such animal. They then searched

their records and found that they did indeed have a file on

Preservation but it had been returned to the registry and not

touched since, ...'

28. We find that DSTO was an establishment with a staff of approximately 3,000 at the relevant time and staff movements including resignations would have been as large, if not larger than that in the Department of Transport. However, there again appears to have been an abysmal lack of knowledge of the preservation provisions among those whose duty it was to inform others of their rights.

29. There was also other evidence before the Tribunal which we have accepted that personnel clerks were not familiar with the form S2A (exhibit R2) which Mr Boardman completed at the time of resignation. This form is divided into three parts. The first part of the form which we have already discussed, contains the applicant's signature and his decision on the alternative available to him. The second part is the departmental report in respect of the contributor and contains the applicant's date of birth, his contributory service, his salary for superannuation purposes and variation to that salary for superannuation purposes. It was prepared by one 'Herb Jaunzarins'. In a letter to the respondent dated 13 July 1992, Mr Jaunzarins, by this time Personnel Officer, Department of Administrative Services, Adelaide, said (T43/99):

'...

I confirm that I was a Staff Clerk with the former Department of

Civil Aviation, and did process applications for superannuation on

resignations.

In many instances it was not possible to formally counsel officers

on resignation, as they were outposted. I cannot recall whether, at

the time of Mr Boardman's resignation, all resignees were routinely

advised of their option to elect for preservation of superannuation

rights. The form clearly spelled out in the header, the provision

for preservation, as did the formal election.

I believe that at the time of Mr Boardman's resignation, many

Personnel staff were not generally conversant with the

preservation provisions, and it was not until several years later

that readily accessible information was available which made staff

more aware of this subject.

I cannot discount the possibility that Mr Boardman was given less

than the best advice with regards to superannuation entitlements

on resignation.

(Signed)

H R Jaunzarins

Personnel Officer 13 July 1992'

30. The applicant told the Tribunal that Mr Jaunzarins was not the person who gave him the information. The Tribunal accepts the evidence of the applicant. We find that he did ask about the options on the form S2A (exhibit R2). We find as a fact, on the balance of probabilities, that the applicant was not given the correct information. We make this finding on the totality of the evidence before the Tribunal, and in particular:

.the evidence of the applicant;

.the evidence of Mr Stidworthy and Mr Furner that the preservation

option was so rarely adopted that one of them had never handled

one at all, and the other had only handled one in the late '80's;

.the evidence of Mr Bomford that in the whole of the 'human

resources' (personnel) section of DSTO (some 45 operators) only

one could refer him to some circular or other;

.the letter from Mr Jaunzarins.

On the totality of the evidence before the Tribunal, we also find that in 1979 the detailed knowledge of preservation, and the benefits which accrued through it, was not generally appreciated in other than the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office, the office of the respondent.

31. We further find that we are not able to identify the person who gave Mr Boardman the incorrect information and cannot identify where that person worked at the relevant time.

32. We turn now to address the question of when and how the applicant became aware of the preservation provisions.

In his evidence, the applicant said that when he came back into the Public Service he was still concerned about the treatment he had received at the hands of those in charge of the Air Traffic Control incident in 1979. He undertook appeal procedures under the Grievance Appeal provisions of the Public Service Act. He told the Tribunal that in the end he felt vindicated and justified by this action, even though it occupied most of his spare time up to 1984. By this time, he had established himself as an administrative officer and had decided not to return to Air Traffic Control work even if it was offered to him. About this time, 'I was getting interested in the superannuation arrangement because I had just found out sometime in the mid '80's about preservation. And I realised that I'd been ... diddled' (T5 p 20).

33. It is clear and we so find that the applicant would have known about 'preservation' by the date of the publication of the first Superannuation Booklet (T48 pp 122-131) in January 1989, because that booklet contained a detailed section on preservation.

We also find that the applicant knew about preservation in June 1988 because an exhibit tendered by the applicant (A2) entitled 'Thinking of resigning? Don't throw away your super]' dated June 1988, contained 'actual examples of the dollar amounts payable and as a deferred benefit on reaching age 55 ...' (T5 p 24). The exhibit contains the following table which contains examples of deferred benefits for a contributor who resigns at age 45, preserves the benefit and applies for the deferred benefit at age 55:

Accumulated basic

in cash pension in cash pension in cash pension

per annum per annum per annum

Benefit at age 55:

Assuming 16%

interest $44,114 $10,204 $132,343 $30,611 $220,572 $51,018

It is worth noting at this stage that this table highlights the fact that the deferred benefit contains two elements viz your own money back plus interest, plus an indexed pension from age 55 years onwards. The same information is detailed in Attachment A to exhibit R2 but it is clear that Mr Stidworthy didn't notice it or if he did, he did not highlight it in the Administrative Circular he promulgated. Mr Furner gave evidence which is accepted, that he had never used the sample in Attachment A to calculate an estimated deferred benefit for a resigning employee.

34. Ms Munoz submitted that the applicant should have been aware of preservation when he received the booklet '"The Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme - New Contributors" on his reappointment to the CPS in March 1980' (R1). That booklet contained the following paragraph re: Resignation Benefits at p 8:

'RESIGNATION BENEFITS

Employees who resign become entitled to a refund of accumulated

contributions. They also have, under the Superannuation Act, the

alternative of electing to preserve their superannuation rights.

These could be deferred benefits, or a transfer value to be paid

to some other eligible scheme. The preserved benefit includes a

Government financed element.'

The Tribunal rejects this submission. The Tribunal is of the view that it is highly unlikely that a person starting a new career in 1980 would be seeking to know his or her resignation benefits. It is more likely that such a person would only look for that information in that booklet if he or she was looking to resign, and in that event, the person would find the information less than complete.

35. Ms Munoz submitted alternatively that the applicant would have been aware of the preservation provisions if he had read the detail attached to the information statements for contributors issued each year to all contributors to the CSS (exhibits R5 and R12). Exhibit R5 contains example copies of the statements issued to contributors in 1987, 1988 and 1989. Exhibit R12 contains a sample copy of the 1988 Information Statement. It is the same in all respects to that contained in R5. Each statement had attached to it a brief report from the Chairman of the Superannuation Fund Investment Trust ('SFIT') and the 1987 and 1988 reports each contained a paragraph about preservation rights.

In 1987 that paragraph read:

'Preservation. Certain contributors who resign may elect to

preserve their benefits in the Fund until retirement and thereby

avoid forgoing the employer contribution of 2.5 times the

employee's contributions accumulated with interest. For further

details of this option contact your personnel officer.'

UNDATED - Presumably October 1987

In 1988 that paragraph read:

'Resignation benefits

If you resign from Commonwealth employment before age 55 you

should consider the value of the Preservation option. Briefly,

if you become entitled to a deferred benefit and so leave your

equity in the Fund your equity will continue to grow at the

rates of interest prescribed from time to time. In addition,

you will have preserved an annual indexed pension financed by

your Commonwealth employer.'

DATED - October 1988

In 1989 no reference was made to Resignation benefits or Preservation.

We are of the view that at the time the applicant and Mr Bomford were collaborating in the publication of a book on the CSS, they would have studied closely everything that was issued by the respondent to contributors and we so find. Thus, the information statements would have alerted them to the existence of a provision providing for preservation of superannuation rights. We are of this view because of the interest the applicant was by this time showing in superannuation. We very much doubt that the information in the 1987 statement would have alerted a less informed contributor and we have serious doubts that the information in 1987/1988 or in exhibit A2 would prompt a contributor to ask: 'What about my resignation 7 years ago?' Just what those rights actually were did not become apparent until exhibit A2 was published in June 1988. We find that Mr Bomford and the applicant first started working together in late 1987 and started to talk about the book in about October 1987. Given the detailed examination of the preservation issue which Mr Bomford had undertaken, it is more probable than not in our view, that the applicant had discussed this issue at this time. We therefore find that the applicant became aware of the preservation provisions in late 1987.

36. We now turn to the question of whether there was any obligation on the applicant to take further action at the time he became aware of presentation. In evidence, Mr Boardman stated as follows, (transcript p 20):

'... And by that time, I was getting very interested in the

superannuation arrangement because I had found out some time in the

mid 80s about preservation. Not late election, but preservation.

And I realised that I'd been - to use my vernacular - diddled. And

I felt very upset about it. And that's where my interest in

superannuation comes from, because I didn't want it to happen to

anybody else. Not knowing about late election means that I just

accepted what had happened, but didn't like it.'

The applicant was not clear as to what he had lost as a consequence of being 'diddled'. The benefits set out in 'A2' do not apply because he became an eligible employee again in March 1980 (s.144(1)(b)). The delegate who reviewed the original decision set out the loss of benefit to the applicant as follows (T2 p 28):

'The benefit to Mr Boardman should his request for late election

be granted is not insignificant. For example, should his 8.7

years contributory service from July 1970 to April 1979 be added to

his period of current contributory service, the following

enhancement of standard age retirement benefits under the CSS would

result:

Pension % of salary Age 55 Age 60 Age 65

With preservation 38.18 46.93 52.50

Without preservation 33.45 44.64 51.40

A similar increase in entitlements would result if Mr Boardman

were to elect to transfer to the Public Sector Superannuation

Scheme.'

37. The Tribunal accordingly finds that the applicant has lost the benefit of 8.7 years of contributory service and that he is seeking to preserve this service.

Even if the applicant had turned to s.137 at this point in time, he would have noted the words: 'A person ... may not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, ...'. There is no reference in the section to any further time being allowed and no reference to s.157.

Although the applicant was at this stage becoming an expert in the CSS, it appears that he may well have been like many other superannuation 'experts' in that 'he (thought) he knew and did not see the need to ask anybody'. As a consequence, he took no further action and made no further inquiries in respect of preservation.

38. After Mr Boardman, in conjunction with Mr Bomford, had published the first Superannuation Booklet (T48), they started research on the second booklet dealing with the PSS (T47). That booklet contained a detailed description of a new scheme for superannuation and purported to compare and contrast it with the existing CSS. Such an undertaking required further study of the Act and the applicant says that it was during the later half of 1989 that he became aware of s.157 of the Act, but was not sure what it meant. He asked personnel people in DSTO Salisbury and no one knew. The Tribunal heard evidence that although the applicant and Mr Bomford were considered to be the superannuation experts in Adelaide, neither understood s.157 of the Act. In early 1990 the applicant and Mr Bomford travelled to Canberra to be interviewed for temporary positions within the office of the respondent. In evidence before the Tribunal, Mr Bomford gave evidence as to when he first became aware of the full implications of s.157, (transcript pp 175-176):

'Q. Can you tell us when you first became aware of the late

election provisions, that section 157?

A. I believe that I first became aware that such a section

existed around about February 1990 after having a conversation

with Bruce Webber at the Retirement Benefits Office before

having an interview.

Q. That is the time when we were both in Canberra for the

A. That's right.

Q. Can you tell us how that came about?

A. Well, we had conspired together to produce our first booklet

and with the introduction of the new scheme we had been discussing

things that we were unsure of and while we were in Canberra we

felt it was convenient to talk to Bruce Webber because he had been

Q. Can you - I do not know whether you can - do you remember my

A. I think that on the - after the interview on the plane on

Did you see any limit in that in the fact that by that time some

10 or 11 years had passed?

THE D. PRESIDENT: I am sorry, what was the question?

MR BOARDMAN: Did you see any immediate limitation on the fact

A. At the time I hadn't acutally read the section but subsequently

During cross-examination on this point, Mr Bomford remained firm that neither he nor the applicant had any idea that s.157 could be used to extend the rigid time frame set down for preservation until Mr Webber explained it to them in February, 1990, (transcript pp 176-177):

'Q. What made you say that "we", meaning - I suppose you were

referring to yourself and Mr Boardman, that you and Mr Boardman

were not aware of section 157. I mean, forget yourself for a while

but what makes you say that Mr Boardman was not aware of section

157 as the time you were talking to him?

A. Because the first that I was aware of it was when I spoke to

Bruce Webber. I was with Rob at the time when Bruce Webber raised

this, and we were actually discussing it - Rob and I were discussing

we were both quite intrigued that there was such a section that

would provide an extension of time for making an election because

we were both of the opinion - well, I am speaking of this from my

for that time frame to be extended.'

39. The Tribunal has no hesitation in accepting the evidence of Mr Bomford and finds that the applicant first became aware of s.157 in 1989 and became aware of its full import in February 1990. The Tribunal further finds that the applicant lodged his election with the respondent on 7 March 1990, and that this was as soon as could have been reasonably expected.

40. It was submitted by Ms Munoz for the respondent that even if the applicant did not know about preservation and its benefits until 1987 when the information statements R5 and R12 were published, from that time on he did know and 'sat on his rights'. That argument was accepted by the Tribunal in Re Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation, AAT Decision No 8609, 19 March 1993. In that decision, the Tribunal was of the view that there existed a combination of circumstances between the time that the applicant rejoined Commonwealth employment and the time he elected for preservation, such that he should have been alerted to make enquiries about his possible entitlement to preservation back in 1979, and that the applicant's failure to make enquiries contributed to the long delay before he finally made his application in 1990. The circumstances which the Tribunal referred to included the fact that when the applicant rejoined Commonwealth employment, he was 53 years of age and was approaching the time when he would be entitled to take early retirement at age 55 under the scheme; the fact that he must have been regretful at the time that he was unable to get the benefit of his 18 years of previous contributions; and that the applicant was at the time receiving information regularly about entitlements of contributors to the scheme, including the entitlement of resignees to elect to preserve their benefits. The Tribunal concluded that any misleading effect of information provided to the applicant, the ambiguous nature of the relevant forms, and the failure by the respondent to provide information in 1979 was offset by the fact that the applicant later received accurate information and was in the position to make further enquiries.

41. Again, in Morgan and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision of DP Gerber, Dr Billings, Mr Fayle, 12 March 1993), where the Tribunal was not satisfied that if the applicant had been fully informed as to his options, he would have elected to preserve his superannuation rights in preference to receiving his lump sum entitlement, the Tribunal expressed the view that the applicant could not succeed in any event. The Tribunal came to this conclusion because the applicant made no enquiries from his personnel officer or at the office of the Commissioner at the time he resigned, an omission made worse by the existence of a superannuation manual. Further, the applicant was a man of considerable education and although he was in New Guinea at the time he resigned, he suffered from none of the disabilities which the majority in Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 found sufficient to treat the election as if it had been made within the period allowed; the lump sum was used to reduce the mortgage on the family home; and the respondent demonstrated a small prejudice to the fund were the application to succeed (in respect of the stability and structure of the fund).

42. In Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision of DP McMahon, Dr Travers, Mr Julian, 26 February 1993), the Tribunal did not grant the extension to elect for preservation of rights. The Tribunal, relying on Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315, stated that the prescribed period of 21 days is the paramount principle. That is, the discretion to extend this time is to be limited by reference to the relatively short time period prescribed, the longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and making a late election, the heavier the onus the applicant bears. The Tribunal was also of the view that there must be an acceptable explanation for the delay. Further, the Tribunal said that in these cases one should determine whether the applicant knew of his or her rights to preserve superannuation benefits. In making this determination, one should ask whether any enquiries were made of the applicant's personnel officer, or at the office of the Commissioner. It is also relevant to take into account whether there was any superannuation manual available and whether the applicant had opportunities to make enquiries. Relevant to this question of making enquiries are the employee's education level, health, any language difficulty or impediment to information sharing, any family or personal circumstances that might impinge upon the employee's opportunity to enquire, the location of the employee and any general publicity available at the time.

43. The above Tribunal also expressed the opinion that one should look at the actions of the applicant and that in doing so, one may ask what happened to the lump sum that was paid to the applicant; one may ask whether the application for refund is the only record of an election of whether separate correspondence also amounts to an election to take accumulated contributions; and that one should also enquire about the actions of the employee when he or she re-entered the public sector and became eligible to renew contributions.

The decision also expressed the view that the length of delay will be material in considering the extent of the prejudice to the respondent and that the longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and exercising a late election, the more difficult it will be to establish the facts and the degree of knowledge available to the applicant on resignation.

44. Public considerations were also listed as something which should be taken into account. The Tribunal included as 'public considerations' any financial prejudice to the fund; the fact that if the discretion is freely exercised in favour of late election, then the stability and structure of the fund could be affected; and the prejudice to those those who fail to make an election because of their perception of time limits under the Act.

In conclusion, the Tribunal stated that at the least it should be 'reluctant to exercise the discretion'.

45. We have had regard to the previous decisions of the Tribunal made in respect of s.157 of the Act, however, we have also had regard to the more authorative decision of Justice Hill of the Federal Court in A'Hearn v Comcare (Unreported Decision dated 16 April 1993, Judgment Number 254/1993). That decision concerned an application for an extension of time under s.29(7) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975. At p 6 Hill J, stated as follows:

'I should say that Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd

was at pains to make clear that he was not seeking to set out

principles of law governing the exercise of the discretion to

extend time. Care must be taken by the Tribunal to ensure that

there is not a slavish adherence to the matters which are referred

to in that judgment, which are listed merely for guidance, in

determining the extensions of time under the Administrative

Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977, a context which differs

slightly from that in the AAT Act. I do not, however, suggest that

the Tribunal necessarily saw the comments of Wilcox J as dictating

matters of law to be considered in the exercise of discretion.'

Later, at p 10, Hill J said:

'... At the end of the day the discretion of a court or tribunal

given power to extend time will be a wide one, to be exercised as

the case may require. As Richmond J ...' delivering the leading

judgement in the court of Appeal in Avery v No 2 Public Service

Appeal Board (1973) 2 NZLR 86 1 at 92, said:

"... In order to determine the justice of any particular case the

Court should I think have regard to the whole history of the matter,

including the conduct of the parties, the nature of the litigation

and the need of the applicant on the one hand for leave to be

granted together with the effect which the granting of leave would

have on other persons involved."'

In regard to the decision of the Tribunal which was appealed against Hill J said, at p 12:

'It seems to me that the Tribunal's reasons for decision suggest

that it has started ... with the view that it is "critical" that

proceedings be instituted within time. That really overstates the

position having regard to the provisions of s.29(7).'

46. On the basis that the discretion under s.157 of the Act is as wide as the discretion provided for in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975, what Hill J had to say in A'Hearn (supra) is very persuasive, if not binding. The discretion contained in s.157, which provides that the discretion should be exercised if 'the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized', should not be fettered by the laying down of principles such as was done in Lander (supra). In Lander, the Tribunal expressed the view that the time limit of 21 days was the 'paramount principle' and that the longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and making a late election, the heavier the onus the applicant bears.

47. We find that we are unable to agree with the views expressed by the Tribunal in the decisions of Secourable (supra) and Morgan (supra). In these decisions it was found that the applicant should not succeed in the application for an extension of time, on the basis that the applicant should have made enquiries as to entitlements to superannuation and thereupon would have discovered that there was a provision allowing for preservation. The Tribunal in these decisions also expressed the view that the failure of the respondent to provide any information at the time of resignation (Secourable), or the fact that the respondent failed to provide adequate information to the applicant (Morgan) was offset by the fact that the applicant should have made further enquiries.

48. This Tribunal is of the view that the nature of the superannuation legislation, and the poor level of knowledge amongst personnel staff as to superannuation rights, in particular as to preservation, which was clearly evidenced throughout these entire proceedings, is such that it is far too onerous to place any sort of 'duty of enquiry' upon an applicant who has not been adequately informed or who, as in this case, has been misinformed as to their rights. Even when Mr Boardman became aware of preservation in 1987, we are of the view that the circumstances of this case are such that there was no duty on him to make further enquiries such as could have led him to the further discovery of late election. From the evidence presented to this Tribunal, it is very likely that any efforts to obtain further information would have been thwarted by the severe lack of knowledge, even by the 'experts' on this issue. We find that the applicant did not at any stage 'sleep on his rights'.

49. We are of the view that employees who resign and are contributors to the CSS, should be given adequate information by personnel officers and that nothing less than an estimate of the benefits available at age 55 calculated in accordance with the example in Attachment A to exhibit R2, and placed in front of the resigning employee would satisfy the criteria that a person be properly informed as to his or her rights.

We find that Mr Boardman was not given these details. He was an eligible employee and contributor under the CSS and its predecessor from 30/7/70 to 28/4/79 (8.7 years). He was not a contributor from 28/4/79 to 6/3/90 (10.5 months). He again became an eligible employee on 7.3.90, at the time he was 29 years of age and had a prospective 35 years service before attaining maximum retiring age.

50. We are of the view that the nature of the discretion which we have to exercise involves determining whether it is desirable in all of the circumstances to allow the applicant to make a late election for preservation of benefits. On the authority of A'Hearn (supra) and Hunter Valley (supra), this involves a determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is granted. On the facts of this case, the applicant suffers a prejudice in the form of losing the benefit of his 8.7 years of prior service if the extension is not given. The desirability of exercising the discretion in the applicant's favour is the alleviation of this loss, especially in these circumstances where the loss is unfair in that it was brought about through no fault on the part of the applicant, and was indeed caused by the misinformation provided by the respondent. The prejudice suffered by the respondent is in the form of the fund not having had the use of the monies for the relevant period.

51. We find that the circumstances of this matter are such that it is desirable that the discretion to grant an extension of time should be exercised in favour of the applicant because the prejudice to the respondent is far outweighed by the unfairness of the loss which would be suffered by the applicant, which loss was caused by the actions of the respondent, should the extension not be granted. The correct and preferable decison is that the applicant's late election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s.137(1) of the Act.

52. The applicant should repay to the respondent the sum of $2,717.29, being the amount of calculated contributions refunded. Certainly, the SFIT has not had the use of the money for the past 13 years but in the long term, however, the applicant is the only loser in that the refund of contributions will be less upon his retirement.

53. The decision of the Tribunal is that the decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 7 March 1990 under s.137(1) of the Act, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.
