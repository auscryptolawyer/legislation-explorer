Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1994] FCA 915; (1994) 94 Atc 4026 (1994) 119 ALR 727, (1994) 8 Anz Insurance Cases 61-206 (1994 (17 February 1994)

GENERAL DIVISION

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - whether deduction allowable under s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for estimation of liability for claims under Accident Compensation Act 1985 (Vic) for employees injured in the year where such claims were either reported but not paid or both unreported and unpaid - whether any amount incurred - whether capable of reasonable estimation

Income Tax - allowable deductions - leasing of luxury cars - whether deduction allowable under s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for difference between depreciated value and sale price of cars - whether deduction prevented by s 59(6) - relation of depreciation provisions and s 51(1) considered.

DECISION

NORTHROP J I have had the advantage of reading the Reasons for Judgment of Hill J. I agree with his Reasons for Judgment and with the orders proposed by him.

LOCKHART J I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment of Hill J with which I agree.

HILL J The appellant, Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Limited, ("the Bank") appeals from the judgment of a judge of this Court relating to the Bank's assessment of income tax in respect of the year of income ended 30 September 1986, being a substituted accounting period for the year of income ending 30 June 1986.

2. Three issues arose for decision in respect of that year of income. The first concerned the ability of the Bank to deduct, under s.92 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), the whole or some part of a claimed partnership loss arising out of an annuity arrangement entered into in April 1986. The second concerned the ability of the Bank to deduct, under s.51(1) of the Act, certain amounts estimated as being the value of Workcare claims in the year of income. The third concerned the ability of the Bank to deduct, under s.51(1) of the Act certain losses said to have been incurred by the Bank in disposing of luxury motor vehicles which it had leased to customers.

3. The first of these matters was ordered to be heard separately and, that matter being decided adversely to the taxpayer, an appeal was brought to the Full Court of this Court. The judgment of the Full Court is reported at (1993) 93 ATC 4370. In respect of a part of the matter concerned in that appeal, special leave has been granted to appeal to the High Court, but that appeal has not yet been heard. The remaining two matters were heard together by another judge and upon their being decided adversely to the Bank an appeal was brought by the Bank to the Full Court of this Court.

4. As each of these remaining matters involves a discrete question, it is convenient to deal separately with each such question.

The Workcare issue

5. The Bank carries on throughout Australia a banking business. It does not carry on any insurance business.

6. As and from 1 September 1985 a new system of Workers' Compensation, known as "Workcare", came into effect in Victoria. The Workcare scheme was governed by the provisions of the Accident Compensation Act 1985 (Vic). It replaced the scheme for Workers' Compensation which had theretofore been in effect. One of the policy reasons for the establishment of the new scheme was the encouragement of rehabilitation of workers injured in the course of their employment. The legislation, so far as concerns the present appeal, did not, however, differ greatly from that in force before September 1985, save that the emphasis under Workcare was more upon weekly compensation benefits rather than lump sum payments.

7. Under the Workcare scheme there was established the Accident Compensation Commission which became the sole workers' compensation insurer in Victoria. Employers, unless exempted, became liable to pay levies to that Commission. Certain employers satisfying particular financial criteria were under the scheme entitled to become registered as "self-insurers". The consequences of such registration were the exemption of the employer from payment of levies and a direct liability upon that employer to compensate its employees injured in the course of employment. It will be necessary to refer later in more detail to the scheme of the Accident Compensation Act.

8. With effect as and from 1 September 1985, that is to say shortly before the end of the preceding year of income, the Bank became registered as a self-insurer. In the year of income, employees of the Bank were injured in the course of their employment in circumstances entitling them to compensation. Some employees so injured claimed before the end of the year of income and their claims were dealt with by the end of that year. It is not in dispute that any claims so made and dealt with gave rise to an allowable deduction to the Bank in the year of income. However, other employees injured did not have their claims dealt with by the end of the year of income. These claims fell into two categories. First, injured employees might report the injury but their claims might remain unresolved and unpaid as at the end of the year of income. In such case the claims might, at the end of the year, be claims reported but not paid. Further, there might be employees who, although injured, had not yet reported the circumstances giving rise to that injury. The claims of this category of employees may be referred to as claims incurred but not yet reported.

9. Evidence was adduced at the hearing of the way in which the estimate of the value of claims in each category was made. Upon becoming registered as a self-insurer, the Bank employed an accident compensation officer, Mr Jubb, who, prior to taking up that position, had 20 years previous experience in workers' compensation insurance with various insurance companies. Also employed by the Bank was a Mr Coull, the Manager - Workers' Compensation, who was responsible for handling the workers' compensation claims of the employees of the Bank throughout Australia.

10. Once a claim was received by the Bank it was examined by Mr Jubb who considered the relevant facts as known and made an estimate of the Bank's liability. At the end of the year, Mr Jubb and Mr Coull selected the larger claims reported in the period 1 September 1985 to 31 August 1986 and considered the relevant information from such claim files including the assessment of the likelihood of the employee returning to work, the weekly benefits and the likelihood of a common law action. They then met with an actuary from an independent firm of actuaries and discussed with that actuary estimates of outstanding claims. That meeting took place in late September 1986. Mr Coull provided to the actuary a claims listing for the period 1 September 1985 to 31 August 1986. He also provided the actuary with claims data compiled by Royal Insurance Australia Limited, the applicant's prior workers' compensation insurer, for the period September 1983 to September 1985.

11. The actuary examined the claims reported to 31 August 1986 and provided Mr Coull with an estimate of the Bank's liability in respect of these claims of between $750,000 and $810,000, depending upon whether an allowance for claim expenses was to be made.

12. In a letter from the actuary to Mr Coull, the actuary noted that he had been unable to apply any of the standard statistical techniques to the historical claims run-off data because the historical data was limited and the number and amount of claims processed each year was relatively small and volatile. The actuary noted further that there had been major changes to the workers' compensation system, particularly a change from lump sum amounts to pensions, which were expected "to dramatically alter the claim run-off pattern".

13. In these circumstances the actuary noted that the approach he had adopted was as follows:

"* Estimate total claim numbers (including

number of IBNR claims) based on past

trends. This assumes the timing of claims

reported will not change dramatically

following the introduction of Workcare.

* In the case of the majority of claims

which are relatively small, I have relied

on Bank estimates. However, I have

adjusted these estimates to take account

of claims inflation, investment earnings,

and claims expenses as appropriate over

the period the claims are expected to be

paid. The Company estimates were also

compared roughly to historical trends for

consistency.

* In the case of the remaining potentially

large claims (long term pensioners), I

have valued the `worse case' situation of

the claimant never coming back to work and

reducing this amount to reflect the

probability of the claimant recovering and

possible recoveries from Royal Life. The

probability factor was derived by

discussion of the individual claims with

you."

14. The reference to "IBNR" is a reference to claims incurred but not reported.

15. After setting out the assumptions that he had made, the actuary commented:

"I consider these assumptions to be relatively

conservative, but appropriate given the

uncertainty surrounding the run-off of claims

incurred following the introduction of

Workcare."

16. Mr Coull then considered a claims listing for the period 1 September 1985 to 30 September 1986, adopting as the value of each claim the current estimate of that claim.

17. The estimate of claims incurred but not reported was made by adopting an "uplift allowance" of 25 percent. This figure was adopted as being an industry average for assessing claims incurred but not reported, conveyed to Mr Coull by an account executive with an insurance broker who had made a telephone survey of figures used by other insurance companies. The ultimate estimate made by Mr Coull of each category of claim was as follows:

"Claims reported but not paid $623,675

Claims incurred but not reported $155,919

$779,594"

18. He compared this estimate ($779,594) with that given to him by the actuary and noted that the figures were very similar, although the actuary's estimate related to a period one month less.

19. Mr Coull expressed the view that his estimate had proved to be an extremely conservative one. Of the claims in respect of which a provision was made in the 1986 accounts reported but not paid, as at 10 December 1991 the total amount paid out was $2,178,462, as compared with the provision of $623,675. In respect of claims incurred but not reported, the total paid out as at 10 December 1991 was $238,372, as compared to a total provision in the account of $155,919.

20. The parties are in agreement that the amount of the provision was estimated taking into account not only the Bank's own claims but also the claims of two related or subsidiary companies, ANZ Executors and Esanda. When the subsidiaries are excluded, the amount of the provision reduces to $757,898 and the Bank does not seek to claim the higher amount.

21. Mr Coull was cross-examined at some length, particularly regarding the letter from the actuary to which reference is made above. Asked about certain potentially large claims in respect of which the actuary had indicated difficulty since the success of rehabilitation arrangements was not known, Mr Coull replied that it was "pretty much a lottery" how they would turn out. He agreed that in view of the limitation of the information he had at the time of making the estimation, it was only possible to make an estimate that was inadequate, although he said that in so doing he had made what he considered to be his best estimate. Indeed he said he had never been able correctly to estimate a claim.

22. In re-examination Mr Coull said that he could not think of any method to use other than that which he had adopted and indeed after some further years of experience with Workcare continued to assess in the same way.

23. It was a requirement attached to its status as a self-insurer by the Accident Compensation Act that the Bank provide in its accounts for claims incurred but not paid, whether or not reported. Further it was expected, under guidelines issued by the Victorian Department of Management and Budget, that the Bank would review estimates made by it on a periodic basis, being at intervals not less than six months.

24. Accounting evidence adduced by the Bank was to the effect that in accordance with approved accounting standards but subject to exceptions such as materiality, the Bank was required to provide in its accounts for both categories of claim as "liabilities" and there was no difference in principle between the way in which an insurance company should account for such claims and the way in which a self-insurer, such as the Bank, should account for them. Cross-examination of the accountants who gave evidence was directed to show that the proper accounting treatment required provisions to be made in various circumstances where no income tax deduction was available, particularly that the proper accounting standards required provision in the accounts to be made for annual holiday leave and long service leave accruing.

The Accident Compensation Act - Statutory Scheme

25. The Accident Compensation Act replaced the Workers' Compensation Act 1958 (Vic). In the language of Mason CJ, Deane, Toohey and Gaudron JJ, in the Registrar of the Accident Compensation Tribunal (Vic) v Commissioner of Taxation (1993) 93 ATC 4835 (at 4837), the 1985 Act "established a new regime with respect to accidents and diseases in the workplace."

26. The objects of the Workcare scheme as stated in s.3 of the Accident Compensation Act were:

"(a) to reduce the incidence of accidents and

diseases in the workplace;

(b) to provide suitable systems for the

effective rehabilitation of injured

workers;

(c) to provide suitable and just compensation

to injured workers;

(d) to speedily and efficiently determine

claims for compensation and deliver

compensation to injured workers; and

(e) in this context to reduce the cost to the

Victorian community of accident

compensation."

27. A worker (as defined) suffering injury arising out of or in the course of any employment was entitled to compensation by force of s.82. In the event of the death of the worker, the worker's dependants were also entitled to compensation. The right of the worker, or his dependants to compensation, was ordinarily a right to be paid out of the fund administered by the Accident Compensation Tribunal. However, where the worker was employed by a self-insurer registered under the Accident Compensation Act, liability was imposed upon that employer by force of s.143 which provided relevantly:

"Where a worker or a worker's dependants are

entitled to compensation under this Act and the

worker's employer was, at the time of the injury-

(a) a body corporate that was at that time a

self-insurer; or

(b) a body corporate that was at that time a

subsidiary of a self-insurer -

the first-mentioned body corporate is...

liable to pay the compensation."

28. The compensation to which s.143 referred was compensation provided for in Part IV of the Accident Compensation Act. In the event of death a lump sum became payable to the workers' dependants in accordance with s.92. If the worker was totally incapacitated, weekly payments became payable to the worker during the period of total incapacity for work, in accordance with s.93 of the Act. Where the worker was partially incapacitated, weekly payments became payable during the partial incapacity under s.94. Injury to certain bodily parts brought s.98 into play. That section provided for a lump sum payment to be made to the worker, that lump sum varying as a percentage of a stated figure depending upon the injury. In addition compensation by way of medical, hospital, nursing, rehabilitation and ambulance costs was payable under s.99. A worker receiving weekly payments could apply for redemption of up to 30 percent of those weekly payments in certain circumstances under s.115 of the Act. Weekly payments might be increased or reduced, having regard to the degree of incapacity of the worker and cease in the event of the worker ceasing to be incapacitated.

29. Notice of a claim was required to be given by s.101 of the Accident Compensation Act which provided:

"(1) A person shall not be entitled to recover

compensation under this Act unless notice

of the injury has been given to the

employer.

(2) An employer who receives a claim for

compensation shall be deemed to have been

given notice of the injury."

30. A notice under s.101 could be given either orally or in writing.

The judgment below

31. The learned trial Judge was of the view that the Bank was not entitled to deduct under s.51(1) of the Act the amounts of the provisions made by it in respect of the year of income for the two categories of claims to which reference has been made. In so deciding his Honour placed considerable weight upon the fact that the Bank was not an insurer but was placed under a direct statutory obligation to make payments not out of premiums payable in the year of income, but from its general funds. For this reason his Honour considered the Bank to be in a different position from insurance companies. Further, his Honour said:

"The statutory liabilities imposed upon the

applicant by the ACA (Accident Compensation Act)

are, in my opinion, indistinguishable in

principle from the statutory obligation to pay

long service leave and annual holiday pay which

were considered in such cases as Flood and

Nilsen (cited above)."

32. His Honour further accepted submissions on behalf of the Commissioner that the amount of any provision for claims not yet paid was incapable of being reasonably estimated and for that reason as well could not be the subject of an allowable deduction under s.51(1).

33. It was from this decision of his Honour that the Bank appealed.

The competing submissions

34. The primary submission for the Commissioner was that the amounts in issue had not been "incurred" in the years in which they were claimed. It was submitted that in respect of both categories of claim for which a provision was made, there was no obligation in the year of income to make a monetary payment to the worker and that until such an obligation arose no amount was incurred. Alternatively it was submitted that if, in accordance with Commercial Union Assurance Co of Australia Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 14 ALR 651, a deduction were available for liabilities capable of reasonable estimation, the facts of the present case showed that no reasonable estimate could be made with the consequence that no deduction was available.

35. The written submissions of the Commissioner did not advance the proposition, accepted by the trial Judge, that a deduction was available for workers' compensation claims only to an insurer, but not to a "self-insurer". However, counsel in oral submission did not concede the point, albeit that little in the way of reasoning was advanced in support of such a submission.

36. For the Bank it was submitted that no relevant distinction was to be drawn between the ability of an insurance company to deduct a provision for claims reported but not paid and claims incurred but not reported, and a "self-insurer". Rather, it was submitted, a deduction was available in the year of income in which a liability was incurred, that being when the taxpayer became definitively committed to make payment of a benefit after an injury has been suffered by an employee. It was submitted that the evidence did not show that no reasonable estimate was possible and that, having regard to all the evidence, a reasonable estimation of the Bank's liability had in fact been made in the present case and accordingly the provision made was deductible.

37. It is convenient to consider the competing submissions under three headings:

* Is there a difference between an insurance

company and a self-insurer?

* Was there a liability incurred by the Bank?

* Was that liability capable of reasonable

estimation?

Is there a difference between a bank and a self-insurer?

38. It has long been determined that an insurance company can deduct a provision for claims reported but not paid and claims incurred but not reported: RACV Insurance Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] VicRp 1; (1975) VR 1; Commercial Union (supra). In the former case, it was held that a provision for claims incurred but not reported gave rise to an allowable deduction to RACV Insurance Pty Ltd. The claims in question related to third party insurance. A provision made by the company for claims reported but not paid, which provision related, inter alia, to workers' compensation insurance, was not the subject of consideration because that provision had in fact been allowed. The primary submission made by the Commissioner in that case was that there was no liability to indemnify an insured under a third party policy until it had been determined by settlement or court order that the insured was liable to pay a quantified sum to a third party. In rejecting that submission, Menhennitt J of the Supreme Court of Victoria said (at 8):

"When there has to be considered the question

whether an insurance company has incurred a loss

or outgoing in a particular year that question

comes to be considered in relation to the nature

of the business being carried on. The essence

of insurance business is that, in respect of

each class of risk insured against, the

insurance company aims to satisfy its

liabilities to the policy holders who actually

experience the risk primarily out of the total

of the premiums paid by all the policy holders,

most of whom normally do not experience the

risk. ... Once events have occurred out of

which a liability to indemnify an insured

arises, it appears to me that within the meaning

of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act a

loss or outgoing has been incurred. Events have

occurred which have subjected it to a liability

to indemnify its insured against his liability

to a third person and the extent of that

liability is capable of reasonable estimate. ...

the fact that the quantum of the loss or

outgoing is a matter of estimate and that the

amount may have to be adjusted in the light of

later events does not stand in the way of it

being a loss or outgoing...".

39. In the course of his judgment his Honour made it clear that the comments made applied not merely to third party insurance but to all forms of liability insurance, reserving for another day the question whether, under a policy under which the right of indemnity was subject to a condition such as the giving of notice, the loss or outgoing was not incurred until notice was given.

40. Subsequently in the Commercial Union case the ability of an insurance company to deduct a provision for claims incurred but not reported was considered by Newton J, this time in the context of an insurance company which carried on virtually all forms of insurance business other than life insurance. In allowing the claim Newton J said (at 655):

"Putting it broadly, the true profit of an

insurance business in respect of any year can be

no more than what remains after deducting from

the premiums earned in that year, all the claims

arising out of the insurances in respect of

which those premiums were earned, and also of

course the administration and like expenses

referable to that year; and in the nature of

things many of the claims arising out of the

insurances in respect of which the premiums were

earned will still be unpaid at the end of the

year and will include claims incurred but not

reported. The cost to an insurer of the earned

premiums in any year is the amount of all claims

arising out of the insurances in respect of

which those premiums were earned, plus

administration and like expenses referable to

that year."

41. The fact that some of the policies required notice within a given time as a condition precedent to a liability arising under the policy was held not to affect the matter, the almost invariable practice being to pay claims in full notwithstanding non-compliance by an insured person with the conditions precedent.

42. In both of these cases evidence was given by experts in the insurance industry and by accountants which made it clear that insurance companies had, at least for many years in the past, made provision for claims not reported and that such a provision was appropriate to determine the true profit or loss of the insurance business.

43. While it is true that the close connection between a liability for claims on the one hand and the premium income derived in the year of income on the other, assists in reaching the conclusion that a liability for claims reported but not paid or claims incurred but not reported, arises in respect of the year of income in which the premiums in respect of those policies is derived, there is nothing in the process of reasoning in the insurance cases which requires the conclusion that the principles applied in that case have relevance only to insurance companies. Whether the question is one relating to insurance companies or not, income tax is an annual levy. The Act requires taxable income to be computed by taking the assessable income derived in the year of income and subtracting therefrom the liabilities incurred in the same year. As the judgment of the High Court in Coles Myer Finance Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1993] HCA 29; (1993) 67 ALJR 463 at 471 points out, there is a need to determine, in respect of a loss or outgoing incurred, whether that loss or outgoing is referable to the year of income. Their Honours said:

"In ascertaining what is the taxpayer's net

income or profit for a particular year of

income, it is proper to set against the

taxpayer's gross income or profit for that

period the net losses or outgoings referable to

that period. Under s.51(1) a loss or outgoing

is a deduction only to the extent to which it is

incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income. That provision has been described as:

`a statutory recognition and application of the

accountancy principle which all the accountants

who gave evidence referred to as the matching

principle',

to use the words of Menhennitt J in RACV

Insurance Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation."

44. As the judgment in that case also makes clear, accounting evidence will not be determinative in deciding whether a particular liability has been incurred. The analysis of whether a liability has been incurred is a legal or jurisprudential rather than a mere accounting or commercial analysis (see at 469). But accepting that, it does not follow that such evidence is irrelevant. In Ogilvy and Mather Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 90 ATC 4836 and more recently in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Citibank Ltd (1993) 93 ATC 4691 at 4698-4700, I discussed in some detail the relevance of accounting evidence in the resolution of questions arising under the Act. As I there said (at 4699), in a judgment with which Jenkinson and Einfeld JJ agreed:

"While in the area of s.51(1) of the Act the

Courts have, as was pointed out in Coles Myer

Finance Ltd v FC of T 93 ATC 4214 at 4221,

adopted a legal or jurisprudential analysis

rather than a commercial view, this does not

mean that accounting evidence has been seen to

be irrelevant, the true position being, as

Barwick CJ, Kitto and Taylor JJ said in the

Arthur Murray case [1965] HCA 58; (1965 114 CLR 314 at 320),

speaking of the decision of the High Court in FC

of T v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492:

`the Court there held that, while commercial and

accountancy practice may assist in ascertaining

the true nature and incidence of an item as a

step towards determining whether the item

answers the test laid down in the Act for

allowable deductions, it cannot be substituted

for the test'.

Thus while accounting evidence shows that

accrued holiday leave or long service leave

should be taken up in the accounts as a

liability prior to the leave itself becoming

due, no deduction may be claimed under the Act

for the accrued value of such leave because the

ordinary liability to pay wages to the employee

for holidays and long service leave was not

incurred, for the purposes of s.51(1) of the

Act, until the liability in fact arose: Flood's

case (supra) and Nilsen Development Laboratories

Pty Ltd and Ors v FC of T [1981] HCA 6; (1980-1981) 144 CLR 616.

Nevertheless accounting and business practice

will be relevant in determining the appropriate

treatment under the Act for determining the

deductibility of amounts of insurance claims

incurred but not reported... So too the issue of

whether a liability is capable of reasonable

estimation raised in Commonwealth Aluminium

Corporation Ltd v FC of T 77 ATC 4151 at 4161

will likewise be susceptible to business and

accounting evidence...

...

Accounting evidence may also have particular

significance in determining the timing of a

deduction, that is to say not whether it is

incurred, but whether it is incurred in respect

of a year of income. So much appeared from a

short comment in the judgment of Rich J in New

Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v FC of T (1938) 61

CLR 179 at 193 and Dixon J at 208 as well as in

the orders made by the Court. A similar

approach was taken by the Full Court of this

Court in FC of T v Australian Guarantee

Corporation Ltd 84 ATC 4642, per Toohey J at

4649. The judgment anticipated the test

ultimately applied by the majority of the High

Court in Coles Myer where the determination of

how much of the loss on discounted bills and

promissory notes was referable to the year of

income was clearly to be resolved by adopting

what the majority of the Court (Mason CJ,

Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ) referred

to (at 4223) as the:

`accounting straight line basis over the term of

the relevant note or bill.'"

45. Not surprisingly senior counsel for the Commissioner in cross-examination secured from the accounting experts the admission that the proper accounting treatment of long service leave and annual holiday pay required a deduction to be taken up in the accounts prior to the leave in question becoming due while such a provision was not deductible for income tax purposes. So much has been self-evident for at least the 13 years that have passed since the decision of the High Court in the Nilsen case.

46. The present, however, is an example of a case where accounting evidence may be particularly relevant in determining not whether a liability is incurred but rather whether a liability incurred is referable to a particular year of income. The accounting experts who gave evidence in the present case made it clear that for accounting purposes there should be no distinction between an insurance company and a bank, or for that matter any other trading company. Each must provide in their accounts for claims whether or not reported or paid. To fail so to do would, subject to an issue of materiality, render the accounts such as not to give a full and true view of the profit and loss of the taxpayer.

47. It follows, in my view, that, provided that in the jurisprudential sense the Bank has incurred a liability, a deduction will be available to it for a provision for claims reported but not paid as well as claims incurred but not reported, in the same circumstances and subject to the same conditions as are applicable to an insurance company receiving premiums for insuring against the making of those claims.

Whether the amounts provided were incurred?

48. In Ogilvy and Mather (supra) I set out a number of propositions drawn from the case law as to the meaning of the word "incurred" in s.51(1) of the Act which may now be taken to be accepted. Those propositions, the Commissioner submitted, correctly stated the law. They are, it must be said, in no sense inconsistent with the submissions of the Bank.

49. There is no point in repeating those propositions here. Suffice it to say that for a loss or outgoing to be incurred it must be more than "impending, threatened or expected". Rather, the taxpayer must have been definitively committed in the year of income to that loss or outgoing: New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at 206-207 per Dixon J; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492 at 506-507. The loss or outgoing must represent a present liability, albeit not immediately payable but payable in the future, and whether or not defeasible: Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 CLR 616 at 627-628 per Gibbs J; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd [1984] FCA 240; (1984) 2 FCR 483 at 486-487 per Toohey J.

50. The insurance cases, to which reference has already been made, as well as the decision of the Supreme Court of Victoria in Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation v Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4151, make it quite clear that it is irrelevant that the quantum of a loss or outgoing can not be precisely ascertained for the liability to be incurred in the year of income. It is sufficient if that liability be capable of reasonable estimation.

51. At the heart of the Commissioner's case lay a submission that the liability of the Bank under the Accident Compensation Act to its employees was a liability to make weekly payments and was to be treated in the same way as the liability to make weekly payments to employees by way of salary or wages, or for that matter by way of annual holiday pay or pay for long service leave. In my view, that analogy is not a correct one.

52. As the decision of the High Court in Nilsen makes clear, the primary obligation of an employer in respect of annual holiday pay or long service leave is to give an employee who has served the requisite amount of time paid leave. It is only when the employee enters upon that leave that a liability arises for the first time upon the employer to make a payment of money to that employee. Thus the liability for long service leave or annual holiday pay will be incurred only as and when the employee enters upon the leave and takes it, or as and when the employee leaves the service of his or her employer, thereby creating for the first time a liability in the employer to make payment to that employee. It is only then that there is a presently existing liability in the sense in which those words are used in the cases. As Gibbs J said (at 628) speaking of annual holiday leave or long service leave:

"The present is not a case in which there was an

immediate obligation to make payment in the

future, or a defeasible obligation to pay, or a

present obligation which as a matter of law was

unenforceable - there was no accrued obligation

to make any payment at all. There was no loss

or outgoing `incurred' within s.51(1)."

53. An employer's obligations under the Accident Compensation Act are quite different. First, there are obligations in certain circumstances to pay lump sums rather than weekly amounts. Counsel for the Commissioner could not advance any reason why liability to pay those lump sums was not deductible in the year in which the obligation to make payment of them arose. To the extent that the obligation arising under the Victorian legislation is one to reimburse an employee against outgoings which the employee may incur for medical expenses etc., the fact that the outgoing has not yet been incurred by the employee does not affect the question.

54. There is, however, no relevant distinction in the present case between the payment of a lump sum on the one hand and the payment of a weekly amount on the other. While it is true that payments of a weekly amount may determine in certain circumstances, such as the exercise by the employee of a right to partial commutation or return by an employee to work, that does not alter the fact that a presently existing liability is imposed upon the employer who is a self-insurer to make the weekly payments. It affects merely the quantum of those payments. Unlike the case in Nilsen there exists from the moment that an employee has suffered an injury in the course of his or her employment, a presently existing liability to make payments in the future. Such a liability, while perhaps in one sense defeasible, is nevertheless a liability incurred within the meaning of s.51(1).

55. Nothing in my view turns upon the provisions of s.101(1) of the Accident Compensation Act. Once a claim for compensation is made, notice is deemed to have been given. The failure thus to give a notice does not disentitle an employee to recover compensation. Put in another way, the giving of a notice is not a necessary pre-condition to the coming into existence of a liability on the part of the Bank to compensate an employee.

56. It follows therefore, subject to the question of whether the amount in question was capable of reasonable estimation, that it was incurred in the year of income.

Was the provision capable of reasonable estimation?

57. It is a corollary of the need to estimate certain outgoings the quantum of which is unknown at the end of the year of income that that estimate may, and presumably more often than not will, turn out to be incorrect. When this is the case an adjustment needs to be made in subsequent years, either to bring into account as assessable income an amount, if the estimate turns out to be too high, or to allow a further deduction should the estimate turn out to be too low: cf The Texas Company (Australasia) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1940] HCA 9; (1939-40) 63 CLR 382 at 465 per Dixon J. Except in cases falling within s.170(9) of the Act where the Commissioner is authorised to amend assessments within a four year time period to give effect to a calculation of the total profit or loss actually derived in a year of income in which an estimated amount of income has initially been calculated, it will be inappropriate to amend the assessment in the year in which the estimate is made to revise that estimate.

58. The concept of "estimate" does not involve arbitrarily seizing upon any figure. What is involved is the formation of a judgment or opinion based upon reason. That judgment or opinion must necessarily be made bona fide but it need not be exact for the process of estimation involves a process of approximation. As Lord Loreburn said in Sun Insurance Office v Clark (1912) AC 443 at 454:

"There is no rule of law as to the proper way of

making an estimate. There is no way of

estimating what is right or wrong in itself. It

is a question of fact and figures whether the

way of making the estimate in any case is the

best way for that case."

59. The task of estimation of a liability in a case such as the present is a commercial one. As the facts of the present case illustrate there is a commercial need to make an estimation quite apart from the requirement so to do for income tax purposes. Not only must the Bank make an estimate for the purposes of its accounts, but also the Bank is required under the Accident Compensation Act to show in its accounts as liabilities claims reported and not paid as well as claims incurred but not reported: ss.146(4) and (5) of the Accident Compensation Act. There is an obligation as well to review these estimates on a periodic basis. This statutory background itself suggests that no matter how difficult it may be, the valuation on these claims is not incapable of estimation.

60. The concept, if it be a separate concept, that the estimate made must be a reasonable one (as distinct from one based upon reason) appears to stem from the judgment of Menhennitt J in RACV Insurance. An earlier case of Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) v Manufacturers' Mutual Insurance Ltd [1931] NSWStRp 45; (1931) 31 SR (NSW) 575, had stressed that a provision for claims made by an insurer, although "uncertain", had to be "susceptible of more or less accurate estimate" (at 585 per Ferguson J).

61. It may well be that the process of reasoning adopted by Menhennitt J in RACV Insurance was no more than to say that because on the evidence the provision in the RACV Insurance case was reasonable it was therefore a real estimate, as distinct, for example, from a guess. Be that as it may, the requirement that a provision be reasonable was accepted by Newton J in the Commercial Union case. The evidence in that case was that the method of calculation adopted was a proper method. This was notwithstanding, in respect of a provision of $5,864,866 it produced an under-estimate by an amount of more than $2,400,000. But the fact that the estimate was wrong did not mean that it was unreasonable.

62. Newton J in Commonwealth Aluminium (supra at 4160-1), after stating the requirement that the quantum of liability must be capable of reasonable estimation said (at 4161):

"In this context I think that the quantum of a

liability is `capable of reasonable estimation',

if it is capable of approximate calculation

based on probabilities: see para.2 of the

definitions of the noun `estimate' in the

Shorter Oxford English Dictionary 3rd ed (1950);

see too JJ Savage and Sons Pty Ltd v Blakney

[1970] HCA 6; (1970) 119 CLR 435 at p.442."

63. No question, however, arose in that case as to the method of calculation of the royalty payment in respect of which, as at the end of the year of income, there was a dispute because by the time the return was lodged the precise amount deductible was in fact known. Although as at the end of the year of income no determination of the royalty could have been made with complete precision, the case was nevertheless one where a reasonable estimation could have been made.

64. The learned judge below accepted the submissions of the Commissioner that the provisions established by the Bank were incapable of reasonable estimation. They were thus unreasonable. No question of credit is involved in this finding and it was not suggested that this Court on appeal was in any different position from the trial judge in considering the issue.

65. With respect to the learned judge below, I am of the opposite view.

66. There was nothing in the evidence to suggest that the determination of the provisions was otherwise than made by reference to a bona fide estimate. The means of calculation was not dissimilar from that adopted in the RACV Insurance case. What made the calculation, no doubt, difficult was that with the adoption of a new scheme for workers' compensation there was not available a long historical experience permitting the actuary to say that the figure chosen had statistical accuracy. But it does not follow from that either that the amount involved was incapable of estimation or that the calculation made was an unreasonable one (if that be a different requirement). In my view the making of an estimate by reference to a case by case analysis of files by a person expert in the field is a reasonable way of making such an estimate and indeed ultimately the only way such an estimate can be made. That was the estimate adopted in the Bank's accounts which were audited and which, so far as appears, was subject to no reservation by the auditors in the certification of those accounts. In my view the present is not a case where it can be said that there can be no process of estimation or that no process of estimation was in fact made because the figure adopted was not reasonably arrived at. Rather, the establishment of provisions in the present case was an exercise capable of approximate calculation based on probabilities.

67. It follows, in my view, that the Bank was entitled to a deduction for the provisions made by it for claims reported but not paid as well as for claims incurred but not yet reported. The deductions must, however, be reduced to reflect the fact that the estimates related not only to claims of the Bank but also to claims of its subsidiaries.

Chattels so leased included computers, other office electronic equipment, other equipment for use in a business, or non-electronic equipment such as furniture, petitioning, etc, as well as cars. The legislative scheme

72. Section 54 of the Act provides for a deduction to be allowed to a taxpayer during the year of income for depreciation of any property being plant or other articles owned by that taxpayer and used by the taxpayer during the year of income for the purposes of producing assessable income. The quantum of depreciation in the year of income was to be determined by reference to an estimate made by the Commissioner of the effective life of the particular unit of property: s.55, and was to be calculated in accordance with s.56, calculation varying depending upon whether a straight-line method of depreciation was adopted or a diminishing value method adopted.

73. In the case of depreciation of luxury motor vehicles, s.57AF imposes what might be described as a cap upon the depreciation available to be allowed to a taxpayer as a deduction. Thus s.57AF(2) provides as follows:

"Where the cost of a unit of property to which

this section applies for the purpose of

calculating the depreciation allowable to a

taxpayer under this Act in relation to a year of

income would, apart from this sub-section,

exceed the motor vehicle depreciation limit in

relation to the year of income (in this sub-

section referred to as the `year of first use')

in which the unit of property was first used by

the taxpayer (whether for the purpose of

producing assessable income or otherwise), then,

for the purpose of calculating the depreciation

allowable under this Act to the taxpayer in

relation to a year of income, the cost of that

unit of property shall be deemed to be the

amount of the motor vehicle depreciation limit

in relation to the year of first use."

74. For the year of income ended 30 June 1980 the applicable motor vehicle depreciation limit was $18,000: s.57AF(3). That limit was indexed and for the year of income ended 30 June 1982, for example, was $19,732.

75. The effect of s.57AF(2) therefore, in the case of a vehicle purchased in the income tax year ended 30 June 1982 was that the depreciation available as an allowable deduction under s.54 of the Act was to be calculated, not by reference to the actual cost of that vehicle, if that exceeded $19,732, but rather by reference to the sum of $19,732.

76. The income tax consequences of a disposal of depreciable plant (excluding any consequences of s.51(1)), are dealt with, for present purposes, under s.59 of the Act. That section provides relevantly:

"59(1) Where any property of a taxpayer, in

respect of which depreciation has been allowed

or is allowable under this or the previous Act,

the year of income, the depreciated value of the

consideration receivable in respect of the

disposal, loss or destruction, shall be an

allowable deduction.

59(2) If that consideration exceeds that

depreciated value, the excess, to the extent of

the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in

assessments for income tax under this Act and

any previous law of the Commonwealth in respect

of depreciation, shall, subject to the

succeeding provisions of this section, be

included in his assessable income of that year."

77. Where the depreciated property is disposed of by way of sale at arm's length, the consideration receivable is normally the sale price less the expenses of sale. The expression "depreciated value" is defined by s.62 of the Act. Sub-section (1) provided:

"In this Division, `depreciated value' of any

unit of property at any time means the cost of

the unit to the person who owns or owned the

property at that time less the total amount of

depreciation (if any) allowed or allowable in

respect of that unit in assessments of the

income of that person, for any period prior to

that time, under this Act or any previous law of

the Commonwealth."

78. The ordinary operation of these provisions can be demonstrated by a simple example. Let it be assumed that a unit of property cost $100 and straight-line depreciation of 20% per annum is to be adopted. If the property was purchased on the first day of a year of income and sold on the last day of the next year of income, depreciation in the two years would have been allowable of $40. In this case the depreciated value would, at the end of the second year of income, be $60. If the item were then sold on the first day of the next year of income at $50, s.59(1) would operate to allow as a deduction in that year the difference between the depreciated value ($60) and the sale proceeds ($50). Assuming no costs of sale, there would thus be an allowable deduction of $10 available to the taxpayer under s.59(1) of the Act.

The competing submissions

91. For the Bank it was submitted that, as a Bank, its assessable income included the profits it made in the ordinary course of its business and that losses it made on the use of its money were allowable deductions under s.51(1). Such losses, the Bank submitted, were revenue and not capital losses. It was conceded, however, that the depreciation provisions of s.59(1) then had application so that the first step of the calculation of any allowable deduction was the determination of the deduction available to the Bank under s.59(1) in a case where the depreciated value exceeded the consideration receivable. It was the Bank's submission that once the deduction under s.59(1) was allowed, the balance of any loss became an allowable deduction under s.51(1). It was not, it was said, sought to obtain two deductions for the same amount. Section 57AF, it was then submitted, had no operation in the computation of a deduction under s.51(1). The limitations imposed by s.57AF were imposed solely in relation to the allowance of a deduction for the purposes of the depreciation provisions.

92. For the Commissioner it was submitted that no deduction was allowable to the Bank under s.51(1) because s.59(1) operated to provide a deduction in respect of the whole of the property and there was accordingly no scope for the allowance of a further deduction under s.51(1). Alternatively, it was submitted that any loss incurred by the Bank on the disposal of luxury motor cars was a loss of a capital nature and therefore not deductible under s.51(1). Faintly it was submitted that on the facts of the case the Bank had suffered no loss but rather had made a profit and accordingly s.51(1) did not arise at all. Finally the Commissioner relied upon s.82 of the Act to prevent the allowance of a double deduction.

The availability of a s.51(1) deduction, absent the depreciation provisions

96. In my view it is now beyond question that a Bank which in the course of its business puts its funds to work in acquiring assets used by it in the course of its business and which thereafter sells those assets at a loss will be entitled to a deduction under s.51(1) to the extent of that loss. The money of a Bank is analogous to its trading stock, it is its circulating capital.

97. At least since 1940, it has been accepted that profits made or losses incurred by a Bank in the course of investing its funds will be assessable or deductible, as the case may be, without the need for there to be shown to be a separate business of buying or selling the investments: Punjab Co-operative Bank Ltd, Amritsar v Income Tax Commissioner Lahore (1940) AC 1055. At 1072-1073, Viscount Maugham said:

"In the ordinary case of a bank, the business

consists in its essence of dealing with money

and credit. Numerous depositors place their

money with the bank, often receiving a small

rate of interest on it. A number of borrowers

receive loans of a large part of these deposited

funds, at somewhat higher rates of interest.

But the banker has always to keep enough cash or

easily realizable securities to meet any

probable demands by the depositors... If, as in

the present case, some of the securities of the

bank are realized in order to meet withdrawals

by depositors, it seems to their Lordships to be

quite clear that this is a normal step in

carrying on the banking business, or, in other

words, that it is an act done in `what is truly

the carrying on' of the banking business."

98. A similar conclusion was reached in Australia on the assessability of profits of a bank on investments made in the ordinary course of the banking business in Commissioner of Taxation v Commercial Banking Co of Sydney Ltd [1927] NSWStRp 22; (1927) 27 SR (NSW) 231, and cf National Bank of Australasia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 11; (1969) 118 CLR 529 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v National Commercial Banking Corporation of Australia Limited (1983) 83 ATC 4715 at 4719.

99. As was pointed out in London Australia Investment Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 50; (1977) 138 CLR 106, these cases are no more than an application of the general principle, enunciated in Californian Copper Syndicate (Limited and Reduced) v Harris (1904) 5 TC 159 at 166:

"....that enhanced values obtained from

realisation or conversion of securities may be

so assessable, where what is done is not merely

a realisation or change of investment, but an

act done in what is truly the carrying on, or

carrying out, of a business."

100. The principle underlying Californian Copper is not limited to profits made on the disposal of investments in the course of the Bank's business. If a bank makes a foreign exchange loss in the course of its banking business that loss will be an allowable deduction under s.51(1) and clearly on revenue and not capital account: cf Avco Financial Services Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1982] HCA 36; (1982) 150 CLR 510 at 518 per Gibbs CJ, and this is so notwithstanding that transactions of borrowing and repaying loans may properly be regarded as transactions on capital account: cf Coles Myer (supra at 470). As is said in the joint judgment of Mason CJ, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ in Coles Myer:

"That is because the gains and losses were

incurred in the course of and as an incident of

making repayments of the borrowed money with

which the taxpayer carried on its business as a

finance company. The losses or outgoings were

incurred in the day-to-day conduct of the

business and for the purpose of carrying it on

as a going concern. Though the borrowed moneys

were capital, it was working or circulating

capital from which the taxpayer derived its

profits by turning the borrowed money to account

at higher rates of interest than those paid to

the taxpayer's lenders. The borrowing, as much

as the lending, was an integral part of the day-

to-day conduct of the taxpayer's profit-earning

business."

101. What is there said is equally applicable to gains or losses made by a bank in the course of putting its funds to work in finance leasing transactions. That this is so emerges clearly from the decision of the High Court in AGC (Advances) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1974-5) 132 CLR 175. In that case the taxpayer, a finance company, sought to deduct under the provisions of s.51(1) various losses or outgoings incurred, inter alia, in financing hire purchase transactions. It was held by Barwick CJ and Mason J that the losses were allowable. Gibbs J dissented, but not on the matter presently relevant. Barwick CJ (at 184), speaking of the ability to deduct unpaid instalments of hire purchase rental under s.51(1), said:

"The appellant was in business in financing

hire-purchase transactions. Following a common

commercial practice it took title to the

chattel, paying out the seller of it and then

hiring it under hire purchase to the

`purchaser'. In this way the cost of the

chattel became part of what may properly be

described as circulating capital. The purchase

of the chattel for the purpose of enabling the

legal formalities of hire purchase to be

observed was not the acquisition of a capital

asset. It was the acquisition of something much

more akin to trading stock. In my opinion upon

the failure to recover the amount paid for the

chattel there was a trading loss made in the

gaining of assessable income, that assessable

income being the hiring charges made by the

hire-purchase agreement under which the chattel

was made available to the hirer."

102. The judgment of Gibbs J proceeded on the basis that the amounts concerned would have been deductible under s.51(1), provided the taxpayer showed that it had not, in the year in which it sought to claim the deduction, ceased to carry on the business it had carried on when the hire-purchase agreement was entered into.

103. The answers to the questions stated to the Court in AGC (Advances) make it clear that a deduction was allowable under s.51(1) in respect of the hire amounts written off in certain years of income. There can be no relevant difference between a finance company and a bank where both exist to turn to account money. Nor can there be any relevant difference between rental amounts under a hire purchase agreement and other profits or losses made in connection with a chattel leasing transaction.

104. Little need be said of the suggestion that no loss was made by the Bank. That suggestion fails to distinguish between losses on the disposal of items on the one hand and income in the nature of rental received in the meantime. As the judgment of the Full Court of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Citibank Ltd (1993) 93 ATC 4691 made clear, rental instalments are income derived by a bank or finance company; they are assessable to the bank or finance company under s.25(1) of the Act. The assessability of any profit or the allowability of deduction for any loss on the disposal of an investment made by the bank, including the acquisition of cars for chattel leasing, will be looked at separately from the income derived from the holding or use of that asset. Thus, for example, if a bank were to purchase an asset and sell it at a loss when the sale price is subtracted from the purchase price after taking into account costs of sale and purchase, that loss will not be converted into a profit by taking into account income by way of rental or dividends or interest received annually in the meantime.

The effect of the regime for depreciation on the allowability of a deduction under s.51(1)

105. The starting point of the Bank's submission was the decision of this Court in Memorex Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 77 ALR 299. That case established, so it was submitted, that the depreciation provisions were not a code. From this it followed, so it was submitted, that the provisions presented no impediment therefore to the allowance of a deduction under s.51(1), to the extent to which that deduction had not already been taken into account in the computation of the deduction under s.59(1) of the Act.

106. Memorex was not a case concerned with the relationship between the depreciation provisions on the one hand and s.51(1) on the other. It was concerned with what was said to be the opposite side of that coin, namely, the relationship between the inclusion of a balancing charge in assessable income under s.59(2) and the bringing into account of a different amount under s.25(1) as assessable income. The case concerned a taxpayer whose business was held to be the leasing of goods and the sale at the end of the lease period of those goods at a profit over and above the original cost. It was not in dispute between the parties, at least, that the leased goods should be dealt with in the first instance by the application of s.59(2). The taxpayer, however, argued that these provisions were a code in relation to all goods which were depreciable. The consequence of the argument was said to be that where s.59(2) applied to include a balancing charge in assessable income, there was no scope for any application of s.25(1), it being impermissible to bring some other amount into assessable income on a basis for which s.59(2) did not provide.

107. The argument advanced for the taxpayer was rejected. The Court found that the case was one where the net profits made by the taxpayer were income in ordinary concepts and thus assessable income. By net profits was meant the difference between the actual cost and sale price (not the difference between the depreciated value and the sale price). Thus the Court found the depreciation provisions did not preclude that profit being brought to account, pursuant to the provisions of s.25(1) of the Act if otherwise it was proper so to do. To the extent that the taxpayer's argument depended upon a submission that the depreciation provisions were specific provisions which should take priority over the general provisions of s.25(1), that argument was rejected. The reference to the depreciation provisions not constituting a code must, therefore, be seen in this context.

108. An example given in the course of his judgment by Pincus J (at 318) may suggest that his Honour took a slightly different view from that of the majority, Davies and Einfeld JJ. In that example, his Honour postulated on an item of plant purchased for $20,000 and in the course of its use depreciated down to $10,000. The article was then sold for $30,000. The taxpayer's claim in Memorex was that in such a case the only amount included in assessable income under s.59(2) was $10,000. The actual profit made on the sale (calculated by subtracting the purchase price from the sale price) was, it was claimed not assessable. The Commissioner's view, which prevailed in that case was, that two amounts were included in assessable income, one under s.59(2) of $10,000 and the other, also $10,000, under s.25. That example has, in my view, some significance in the present case.

109. For the taxpayer it was submitted that in a case to which s.57AF had no application, a taxpayer in the position of the Bank would have been entitled to a deduction for a loss, both under the general provisions of s.51(1) and the depreciation provision, s.59(1). It was then said that as the amount of the deduction in each case would be the same, s.82 of the Act gave the Commission a discretion to choose the more appropriate provision thus ensuring that only one deduction was available. Section 82(1) of the Act provides as follows:

"Where in respect of any amount, a deduction

would but for this section be allowable under

more than one provision of this Act, and whether

it would be so allowable from the assessable

income of the same or different years, the

deduction shall be allowable only under that

provision which in the opinion of the

Commissioner is most appropriate."

110. A mathematical example reveals, however, that this submission is incorrect. Let it be assumed that property is purchased for $20,000, depreciated by $10,000 and then sold for $9,000. The deduction available to the taxpayer, in such a case under s.59(1), is $1,000. However, if the taxpayer's submission be accepted, the taxpayer has made a loss (deductible under s.51(1) if the Commissioner's submissions be accepted) not of $1,000 but of $11,000, being the difference between the purchase price of $20,000 and sale price of $9,000. In this example the taxpayer conceded that only $1,000 was deductible but was unable to explain on what principle (other than equity, a principle which seldom seems to find direct expression in income tax matters) the deduction of $10,000 was to be denied. Indeed the taxpayer conceded that in such a case s.82(1) would have no application to disallow to the taxpayer a deduction for the $10,000 representing the depreciation allowed in past years.

111. In my view counsel for the Bank was correct in conceding that s.82(1) in such a case had no application. The example given is not a case where there is the same amount allowable as a deduction under more than one provision of the Act (whether or not in different years). The problem comes about because the computation of the loss allowable under s.51(1) takes into account the entire cost of the asset, whereas that same cost has been taken into account as a deduction in the computation of depreciation. That suggests that as a matter of interpretation the depreciation provisions leave no room for the operation of s.51(1).

112. Other examples may be imagined (excluding consideration of s.57AF) which produce, if the Bank's submission be correct, quite peculiar results. For example, let it be assumed that an item of plant was purchased at $20,000, depreciated to $10,000 and sold at $11,000. If a deduction be allowable to the Bank under s.51(1), the deduction available would be for $9,000. In such a case no deduction is allowable at all under s.59(1), but there is to be included as assessable income the sum of $1,000 under s.59(2). In the result the taxpayer would, if the Bank's submissions be correct, have an overall deduction of $8,000.

113. Counsel for the Bank also did not attempt to rely upon s.82(2) as bringing about the result of reducing the deduction available under s.51(1) by the amount of depreciation already allowed under the depreciation provisions, although an argument to that effect can be made out. Section 82(2) provides:

"Where the profit arising from the sale of any

property is included in the assessable income of

any person, or where the loss arising from the

sale is an allowable deduction, and any

expenditure incurred by him in connexion with

that property has been allowed or is allowable

as a deduction under this Act or has been

allowed or is allowable as a deduction in

assessments under the previous Act, that

expenditure shall not be deducted in

ascertaining the amount of the profit or loss."

114. Counsel for the Bank referred us to the notes to s.82(2) contained in the explanatory handbook, showing the differences between the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934, issued by authority of the Commonwealth Treasurer on 31 August 1936. That handbook does not stand in the same position as explanatory memoranda to legislation to which regard may be had in interpreting that legislation, at least in the case of ambiguity under s.15AB(2)(e) of the Acts Interpretation Act (1901) (Cth). Publication of the handbook postdated the date of assent to the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 on 2 June 1936. Nevertheless, regard might be had to that publication as indicating the mischief to which s.82(2) was directed, that sub-section having no counterpart in the prior legislation. The explanatory handbook has the following comment:

"Sub-section (2) of section 82 applies to cases

where profit or loss results from the

realization of property acquired for purposes of

profit making. For instance a person may

acquire real property for purposes of re-sale at

a profit. It may be two or three years after

purchase before a satisfactory offer is received

from the property. In the meantime, the

taxpayer may be receiving rent from the property

and paying interest on money borrowed to

purchase the property, and also paying rates and

taxes, and expending money on repairs.

The interest, rates and taxes, and repairs would

be allowable deduction from the rental income,

and when the property was sold, section 82(2)

would apply to prevent the interest, rates and

taxes, and repairs, being again deducted, when

arriving at the profit arising from the re-sale

of the property."

115. While there is no reason to read s.82(2) as limited to that class of profits which would have fallen within s.26(a) as arising from the sale by a taxpayer of property acquired, inter alia, for the purpose of profit-making by sale, the difficulty with applying s.82(2) in the present case is that the deduction granted for depreciation is arguably not a deduction for expenditure incurred by a taxpayer in connection with property sold, but rather allowance is made for depreciation by a calculation which takes into account as the starting point to which the rates of depreciation are to be applied, the depreciated value of the unit of property. The starting point of the calculation "depreciated value" is the purchase price of that property. In these circumstances I doubt the correctness of applying s.82(2). It is, however, in my opinion, unnecessary to reach a final conclusion on that matter.

116. In my view, however, the application of the depreciation provisions leave no room at all for the allowance to a taxpayer of a deduction for a loss calculated as the difference between the purchase price of the depreciated plant and the sale price of that plant. The explanation for this result lies in the fact that the whole of the purchase price for the property is taken into account under s.62 in the determination of "depreciated value". By so providing, the legislation evidences the intention that the same amount should not be taken into account a second time in calculating a loss under s.51(1) of the Act. Put in another way, the legislation requires the conclusion that the depreciation provisions and s.51(1) are mutually exclusive and that the depreciation provisions are to be applied.

117. This view is strengthened when regard is had to the terms of s.57AF of the Act and s.59(6). Section 57AF makes it clear that that section is to apply to every case where depreciation is allowable in respect of a motor vehicle to which s.57AF applies. Then the language of s.59(6) makes it clear that that sub-section is to apply to every case where s.57AF has applied. Thus the deduction under s.59(1) is required to be limited to that available under s.59(6).

118. This interpretation accords with the evident legislative policy. It is scarcely conceivable that the legislature intended that a taxpayer not being a bank or a finance company purchasing a luxury car and selling it in circumstances where s.59(1) would have application, would on the one hand be confined to the deduction available to that taxpayer on the disposal of the car by s.59(6), but that a leasing company entitled to depreciate the same vehicle and limited in so doing by s.59(6) would, through the operation of s.51(1), not be so limited to the deduction under s.59(6). The result would, in my opinion, be capricious. If leasing companies were entitled to a deduction under s.51(1) in addition to a deduction under s.59(1) and passed the benefits of that deduction to their customers in lower leasing charges, the effect would be to negate s.59(6) in the case of leased vehicles while applying it in the case of vehicles owned directly by the taxpayer and depreciated by that taxpayer. The evident policy of s.57AF and the correlative provision of s.59(6) was to discourage the purchase of vehicles costing in excess of a stipulated amount by reducing the benefits of deductions available by way of depreciation or balancing charge in respect of those vehicles. That policy would be severely affected, if not totally negated, by permitting a deduction to financiers under s.51(1) in addition to the deduction allowable under s.59(1) of the Act.

119. In coming to this conclusion I do not find it necessary to have regard to the extrinsic material, being the Treasurer's budget speech of 21 August 1979 and the explanatory memorandum which accompanied the Bill introducing s.57AF, to which reference was made by the learned judge below. To the extent that that material supported the view that finance companies were similarly bound by s.57AF, that view did not come about by s.57AF itself, but by virtue of the construction of the depreciation provisions. It is hard thus to see how this extrinsic material could be of assistance in construing those provisions.

120. It follows, in my view, that on this issue the appeal should be disallowed and the Commissioner should succeed.

121. That argument could take place through a telephone link-up, if necessary.
