Last Updated: 19 May 2009



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - taxpayer is a registered valuer - whether income from business or personal services of taxpayer - whether s.260 or Part IVA of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 applicable.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 Part IVA s.260

Valuation of Land Act 1960 (Vic)

REASONS FOR DECISION



Deputy President B.M. Forrest



In these applications the taxpayer has applied for review of decisions to disallow objections against amended assessments of income tax, for the income years ending 30 June 1984 to 1989 inclusive.



On 20 December 1990 the Commissioner, following an audit, issued amended assessment notices increasing the taxpayer's income by the amounts described as net valuation fees, excised from the assessable income of two family companies hereafter called "B Nominees" and "T Nominees", as follows:



  1. 1984 B Nominees 8295 T Nominees 21567

  1. 1985 " 16533

  2. 1986 " 5665

  3. 1987 " 18860

  4. 1988 " 18942

  5. 1989 " 42874



In including this income as part of the taxpayer's, the Commissioner relied on s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 "the Act" in respect of B Nominees for the year ended 30 June 1984, and on Part IV A of the Act in respect of T Nominees for the years ended 30 June 1985 to 1989.



In 1967, the taxpayer became a registered valuer under the provisions of the Valuation of Land Act 1960 (Vic). From 1967 to 1971 he was a real estate salesman, and from 1971 to 1976 he had a real estate agency business. He then was overseas for two years and from 1978 to 1980 he was employed by a finance company selling property finance.



In 1975, he formed B Nominees which undertook real estate development and share trading activities. A family trust, "Trust No. 1", was also established at the same time with B Nominees as trustee. The trust deed provides inter alia that the class of general beneficiary were to include the children of the taxpayer (specified beneficiaries), their children, grandchildren and spouses and the taxpayer and his spouse. The taxpayer is the appointor during his lifetime with power to remove and appoint trustees.



In 1980, the taxpayer left the finance company and commenced practice as a registered valuer working from home. He registered a business name, "X Professional Services" (X being his surname, preceded by the initial of his spouse and himself) under which valuation work was carried out. B Nominees was proprietor of the business name. The taxpayer gave evidence that B Nominees was used as the vehicle for valuation activities for primarily three reasons: that of limited liability; the cash flow generated by the valuation work provided funds for real estate and share trading, and to preserve the good-will of the business in a corporate structure.



From 1980 income was derived from valuation fees. Although the income years prior to 1984 are not relevant in these proceedings, it is apparent from material tendered on behalf of the taxpayer (Exh. E) that the valuation practice was established by the year ended 30 June 1981. Valuations were performed of industrial, commercial and residential real estate in city and country locations.



In late 1981, the taxpayer acquired another corporate entity, T Nominees, and a second family trust, "Trust No. 2", was established with T Nominees as trustee. The taxpayer said Trust No. 2 was established following advice from his accountant and his solicitor for basically the same reasons as B Nominees and, in addition, to separate his investment activities in one corporate/trust structure from his trading/business activities in the other. Further he stated it was his belief he did not derive any tax benefit in using a company/trust structure and it was his understanding that no tax benefit was derived from the structure put in place in 1981. The trust deed of Trust No. 2 provided that the principal beneficiaries were the children of the taxpayer. The secondary beneficiaries were the descendants, wives and widows, and parents and grandparents of the principal beneficiaries. Additional beneficiaries included B Nominees and aunts and uncles of the principal beneficiaries and secondary beneficiaries and their descendants. The taxpayer is also the appointor during his lifetime.



In June 1983, the business name "X and Associates" (the initial of his spouse not being included) was registered. T Nominees was the proprietor of that name and from late 1983 valuation activities were carried out under that name. Although not made clear in evidence, it appears that the name 'X Professional Services' was allowed to lapse on expiry of registration. For the year ending 30 June 1984, gross income from valuation fees (X Professional Services) of $10,326 was included in the return of B Nominees, and $37,594 (X and Associates) in the return of T Nominees. In the subsequent years in question, all of the valuation income was included in the return of T Nominees.



The taxpayer said he conducted the valuation practice from his residence except for a period which he thought was about one year in 1987/88 when he operated from rented premises. He was not a valuer full time, being also involved as an investor/trader in property, gold, livestock and futures trading activities which were spread across B and T Nominees.



In further evidence the taxpayer said that the role of his spouse in the valuation work where she performed routine administrative and secretarial duties, was commensurate with full-time employment. Neither the taxpayer nor his spouse were paid a salary in any of the relevant income years.



The taxpayer's spouse (hereafter called 'H') gave evidence at the request of the Tribunal. In combination with home duties she typed valuation reports for the taxpayer from 1980 until 1983 when, following a disagreement, he rented an office outside the home and apparently had other secretarial assistance. In 1986, H was employed as a full-time secretary of a business associate of the taxpayer in a futures trading business, but also did some typing for the taxpayer. In February 1987, she obtained other full-time employment but continued to do some typing work for the taxpayer.



In 1989, the taxpayer ceased valuation work and referred some of his clients to another valuer. He did not sell the valuation practice.



The parties made oral and written submissions. Ms Cassidy, who represented the taxpayer, submitted that the valuation income should be seen as business income as part of the interdependent business activities of the corporate trustee and not as personal exertion income of the taxpayer. She submitted the valuation income stemmed from the combined efforts of the taxpayer and H and the use of business assets, including a motor vehicle, office equipment, business name and goodwill. Submissions on behalf of the taxpayer in relation to s.260 were essentially that the section is inapplicable because there is no antecedent transaction and that the use of the corporate/trust structure is an ordinary business and family arrangement. Similar considerations and the absence of a tax benefit were submitted on behalf of the taxpayer against the operation of Part IVA.

In the present case if s.260 has application, it only applies to that part of the valuation income included in the return of B Nominees as trustee of the No. 1 Trust for the year ended 30 June 1984. For part of that year and subsequent years in dispute, s.260 has no application as the valuation income was included in the return of T Nominees as trustee of the No. 2 Trust and it will be recalled that T Nominees and Trust No. 2 were established in late 1981.



Section 260 reads as follows:



"(1) Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or entered into, orally or in writing, whether before or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -



(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;



(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any income tax or make any return;



(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or liability imposed on any person by this Act; or



(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any respect,



be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but without prejudice to such validity as it may have in any other respect or for any other purpose.



(2) This section does not apply to any contract, agreement or arrangement made or entered into after 27 May 1981."



Section 260 has been the subject of much judicial consideration and in the context of income splitting arrangements, see in particular Peate v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 111 CLR 443; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Gulland [1985] HCA 83; 160 CLR 55; Tupicoff v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 4 FCR 505; Bunting v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 24 FCR 283; Davis and Another v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ALR 195 and Rippon v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 23 ATR 209.



In respect of the valuation income included in the return of B Nominees for the 1984 year, it is necessary to determine whether there has been any contract agreement or arrangement made or entered into at any time which has the purpose or effect as described in paras (a) to (d) of s.260.



In Peate, Kitto J said at 469:



"The arrangement in the present case, considered objectively as is thus required, may well seem to be characterized by several purposes and effects, some of them unconnected with taxation, including the protection of individual members of the group against liability for negligence; the making of superannuation provision for employees, including doctors employed to assist the group; the better organization of the group's activities and particularly its methods of accounting; and the making of provision for the doctors' families. (All of these purposes, indeed, the appellant swore were actually contemplated in the formation of the plan.) But the question remains, whether the overt acts that were done under the plan are fairly explicable without an inference being drawn that tax-avoidance is a purpose of the arrangement as a whole. Menzies J. thought they were not, and with respect I agree. The arrangement bears ex facie the stamp of tax-avoidance. An understandable purpose of providing for doctors' families, and doing so quite honestly, is perfectly evident; but what is equally evident is a purpose of doing so by a method which will divert income away from the participating doctors to or for the benefit of their families, to the end that a substantial part of the tax might be avoided which would have been incurred if the income had first been derived by the doctors and then applied by them for the benefit of their families."



The arrangement claimed by the Commissioner to be caught by s.260 was the splitting of income said to have flowed directly or predominately from the rendering of personal services by the taxpayer among beneficiaries of the No. 1 trust.



The arrangement in the present case has to be inferred from the circumstances. In Commissioner of Taxation v Patcorp Investments Limited [1976] HCA 67; 140 CLR 247 at 311 Jacobs J said:



"...the applicability of s.260 involves a degree of factual inquiry and inference and therefore to a considerable extent its applicability is often a question of fact."



In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Newton 96 CLR 578 Williams J at 630 cites with approval the following extract from Bell v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; 87 CLR 548:



"In Jaques v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (4) Isaacs J. said of the word 'arrangement' that in this collocation it is the third in a descending series, and means an arrangement which is in the nature of a bargain but may not legally or formally amount to a contract or agreement. It must be remembered, however, that the section is concerned only with contracts, agreements and arrangements which have an effect in law and accordingly are capable of statutory avoidance. With this in mind, it may be said that the word 'arrangement' is the third in a series which regards comprehensiveness is [sic] an ascending series, as to embrace all kinds of concerted action by which persons may arrange their affairs for a particular purpose or so as to produce a particular effect."



The splitting of the taxpayer's income for the purpose of tax avoidance, need not be the sole purpose of the assessment for the purposes of s.260 as Gibbs CJ said in Gulland at 68:



"If tax avoidance is one of the main purposes of the arrangement in the sense that it is not inessential or merely incidental, that is enough".



See also Davis per Hill J at 227:



"...it is now clearly established by the decision of the High Court in Gulland that the tax avoidance purpose (meaning thereby the purpose that falls within one of the four alphabetical paragraphs of the section) need not be the sole purpose nor, indeed, even the principal purpose. It would seem now to be sufficient to attract the operation of the section that tax avoidance was a purpose at least so long as it was not merely an incidental or unessential purpose: see per Gibbs CJ (CLR at 67) and per Dawson J (CLR at 105)."



In the present case, the taxpayer had not derived income from the application of his professional qualification as a valuer until 1980. Here, a new source of income was created when the tax payer's services produced income from property valuations under a corporate trustee/trust structure which until then had been used for property and share trading. While the taxpayer's circumstances do not amount to an antecedent transaction or situation as those expressions are used in the cases - for a review of the authorities on that topic see the discussion in Bunting per Beaumont, Gummow and Hill JJ - it does not follow that s.260 can have no operation in a case where a person arranges his affairs to split income for the first time which he would have otherwise derived but for the arrangement and upon which he had a liability for tax. In Gulland Gibbs CJ said at 73:



"...there is nothing in s.260 that supports the view that that section can apply only when there has been an antecedent transaction between parties. An arrangement will, for example, be within the section if it alters the incidence of income tax in a case in which the only relevant antecedent circumstance is that the taxpayer is in receipt of income."



On the facts in this matter, concepts of an antecedent transaction or situation are not applicable.



A further question for the Tribunal is whether the arrangement whereby the corporate/trust structure which derived the income from valuation services can be explained by reference to ordinary family and business arrangements. A test frequently applied is that expressed by Lord Denning in Newton & Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 CLR 2 at 8:



"In order to bring the arrangement within the section you must be able to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which it was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax. If you cannot so predicate , but have to acknowledge that the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then the arrangement does not come within the section."



The corporate trustee used by the applicant from when he commenced valuation work in 1980 had been the vehicle utilised by the taxpayer over a number of years for property and share trading and investing. The use of a corporate structure for those activities is common place. There is no legal impediment to the use of a corporate entity and/or business name by a registered valuer. The Valuation of Land Act 1960 (Vic) recognises that a registered valuer may practice on his own account, as a member of a firm or as a director of a company. Nor does the use of a trust necessarily bear the stamp of tax avoidance: see Rippon per Heerey J and on appeal per Lockhart, Beaumont and Foster JJ (unreported judgment, 29 September 1992).



The subjective purposes which the taxpayer contended motivated him to enter the arrangement are largely irrelevant: see Tupicoff per Fisher J at 508 also SP Investments Pty. Ltd. as Trustee for the LM Brennan Trust and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 24 ATR 14 per Lockhart J at 26. A good deal was sought to be made of the claim that the taxpayer would not have engaged in practice as a valuer unless he had the protection of a company with limited liability, because of the high cost of professional indemnity insurance. While there may be sound business reasons for incorporation, it should be said that it is difficult to accept his reason, that by incorporation he would personally be protected from the consequences of the law of negligence given the requirement a valuer personally sign valuation reports.



I accept that the use of a corporate vehicle can readily be explained as an ordinary business or family arrangement. However the question remains whether the income from valuations is properly characterised as business income as contended by the taxpayer or income from personal services.



In my view, the fees generated for valuation services were derived predominantly from the provision of personal services by the applicant. They were a product of his professional judgment, qualifications and skill in making property valuations.



The pertinent facts are that a company and trust structure in existence for many years and used in connection with the taxpayer's other business activities, was used for the purpose of his valuation practice. It was not a creation at the commencement of the valuation practice and while the absence of an antecedent transaction or situation as stated earlier is not conclusive, viewed objectively, the arrangement whereby income generated by the personal services of the taxpayer is diverted into a family trust, in my view suggests the paramount objective of the use of the trust structure is for income splitting purposes by the taxpayer. Section 260 has application because in my view, the arrangement taken as a whole bears the stamp of tax avoidance in that it was a main, not merely incidental, feature of the arrangement.



Part IVA was inserted into the Act in 1981 in place of s.260, to apply to transactions entered into on or after 27 May 1981. "Scheme" is defined in s.177A(1) as follows:



"Scheme" means -

(a) any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise or undertaking, whether express or implied and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings; and



(b) any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct."



It was not in issue that the use of Trust No. 2 was a "scheme" for the purposes of s.177A(1).



In s.177C(1) the concept of "tax benefit" is defined. The criteria for determining whether Part IVA applies to particular schemes are to be found in s.177D. Section 177 F(1) gives the Commissioner a discretion as to whether a determination be made to cancel a tax benefit.



Section 177D provides:



"177D This Part applies to any scheme that has been or is entered into after 27 May 1981, and to any scheme that has been or is carried out or commenced to be carried out after that date (other than a scheme that was entered into on or before that date), whether the scheme has been or is entered into or carried out in Australia or outside Australia or partly in Australia and partly outside Australia, where -



(a) a taxpayer (in this section referred to as the "relevant taxpayer") has obtained, or would but for section 177F obtain, a tax benefit in connection with the scheme; and



(b) having regard to -



(i) the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out;



(ii) the form and substance of the scheme;



(iii) the time at which the scheme was entered into and the length of the period during which the scheme was carried out;



(iv) the result in relation to the operation of this Act that, but for this Part, would be achieved by the scheme;



(v) any change in the financial position of the relevant taxpayer that has resulted, will result, or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;



(vi) any change in the financial position of any person who has, or has had, any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) with the relevant taxpayer, being a change that has resulted, will result or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;



(vii) any other consequence for the relevant taxpayer, or for any person referred to in subparagraph (vi), of the scheme having been entered into or carried out; and



(viii) the nature of any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) between the relevant taxpayer and any person referred to in subparagraph (vi),



it would be concluded that the person, or one of the persons, who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme did so for the purpose of enabling the relevant taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme or of enabling the relevant taxpayer and another taxpayer or other taxpayers each to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme (whether or not that person who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme is the relevant taxpayer or is the other taxpayer or one of the other taxpayers)."



If Part IVA is to apply, it is necessary to conclude that the scheme was entered into for the sole or dominant purpose of enabling the taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit in relation to the scheme: s.177A(5).



The taxpayer received no salary during any of the years in question and received very little distributed income, yet as observed earlier it is clear from the evidence the source of the income generated from the valuation work was the taxpayer's as it depended upon his professional qualifications, skill and business contacts. He had an essential role in the valuation practice and no proper commercial value was placed on his services to B Nominees and later T Nominees. In reality, the taxpayer was a sole practitioner with some secretarial assistance, with the use of a motor car and a minimal amount of equipment. The income of the practice was generated not by income producing assets but predominately from the rendering of his personal services.



There was no sale or transfer of any business assets when the valuation activity was acquired by T Nominees in 1983. Valuation work was carried on by the taxpayer as before but using a separate corporate/trust structure. Items for which depreciation was claimed in the return of B Nominees for the year ended 30 June 1984 were a motor vehicle (disposed of in October 1983); also a typewriter and tape measure which for depreciation purposes remained in B Nominees for subsequent years when the valuation income was included in the return of T Nominees.



In the return of T Nominees for the year ended 30 June 1984, depreciation was claimed for some office furniture (cost $550). In later years, a typewriter and filing cabinet were added to the depreciation schedule. Whether these items were used in connection with valuation or futures trading activity or a combination of both was not indicated but does, I think, reinforce the view I have formed as to the nature of the valuation practice.



The absence of any salary component to the taxpayer, while perhaps understandable in the early days of a practice is, having regard to the fees generated, not explicable in my view, without drawing an inference that a purpose of the arrangement was income splitting to escape the burden of taxation on his personal services income. The effect of that arrangement is demonstrated by the treatment of that income in the years in question by distributing between the trusts, having regard to the losses available from the taxpayer's other activities to offset the distributions. It is difficult to avoid the conclusion that the dominant purpose of the arrangement was to obtain a tax benefit by offsetting taxable income against deductions in the form of losses whether they be incurred in the activities of B Nominees or T Nominees. The effect of the arrangement was to alter the incidence of income tax which otherwise would have been imposed upon the taxpayer by providing a capacity to make distribution through both trusts and as evidenced by what in fact was done.



The reality of the situation when the valuation practice was carried on by T Nominees, is that a new corporate/trust structure was introduced, enabling the income from the practice to pass through the No. 2 Trust as well as the previously existing No. 1 Trust. There was no new enterprise created when it was decided to introduce a new structure into an existing arrangement with the effect of creating an additional channel for the flow of income. The taxpayer retained control of both trusts. Although it was the evidence of the accountant that the applicant and his spouse, who were the directors of both B Nominees and T Nominees made the decisions each year to distribute, I accept the evidence of H which revealed she played no part in those decisions.



There is no basis in fact for the taxpayer's assertion that the role of H was commensurate with full time employment. In the relevant income years she performed some typing work for the taxpayer during the time she was in other full-time employment from 1986. Her evidence disclosed the taxpayer had other secretarial assistance from 1983-86. Although it appears H may be entitled to some salary, the evidence is insufficient to enable a finding be made and accordingly the objection decision in respect of any compensatory adjustment is affirmed.



The taxpayer gave as the reason for the distribution of income between the two trusts that they were inter-dependent and the activities of each to some extent relied on the other. That explanation was not convincing. The claim that the various activities of the taxpayer were said to be interdependent has no bearing or relevance to the question of whether the arrangement of introducing a second trust arrangement was for the purpose of avoiding tax. Nor do I think the taxpayer can take comfort from the fact the Commissioner, in apparently accepting the taxpayer's answers in 1982 (Exh E) to queries raised as to the nature of the valuation income can, apart from anything else, be said to have given his imprimatur to an arrangement implemented subsequently.



I find that on the facts in this matter the steps taken by the taxpayer in setting up Trust No. 2 were carried out for the dominant purpose of obtaining a tax benefit, having particular regard to s.177D(b)(i), (ii), (iv), (v) and (vii).



The final issue is the additional tax imposed by s.226 of the Act. The calculation of additional tax included a "per annum" component of 14.026% for the years ending 30 June 1984 to 1989 inclusive and a "culpability component" of 45% for the years ended 30 June 1986 to 1989 inclusive. In all the circumstances, given the arrangements were made following receipt of professional advice and were reasonably arguable, the culpability component should, in respect of the years for which it is imposed be remitted from 45% to 25%.



For these reasons the Tribunal affirms the objection decision in respect to the 1984 and 1985 years of income and varies the objection decision in respect of the 1986, 1987, 1988 and 1989 years of income by reducing the culpability component of the additional tax payable to 25%.
