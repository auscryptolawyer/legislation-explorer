Gibson Mcmillan Owen and Susan Marie Owen v Commissioner of Taxation [1994] FCA 1011; (1994) 94 Atc 4199 (13 April 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

GIBSON MCMILLAN OWEN and SUSAN MARIE OWEN v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NG916 of 1993

FED No. 175/94

Number of pages - 7

Administrative Law - Income Tax

(1994) 94 ATC 4199

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

HILL J

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - Judicial Review - whether decision-maker addressed her mind to all relevant matters - whether decision maker considered matter afresh or merely sought to review an earlier decision - distinction between a review and a reconsideration.

Income Tax - additional tax payable for unpaid provisional tax - remission of additional tax.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth): s.207(1A)(b) and (c)

Words and Phrases - "reconsider", "review"

HEARING

SYDNEY, 14 March 1994

13:4:1994

Counsel and Solicitors DH Murr instructed by

for Applicants: Taylor Kearney Reed and Owen

Counsel and Solicitors RM Henderson instructed by the

for Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The court orders that:

(1) Application be dismissed.

(2) Applicants pay respondent's costs of the application.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

HILL J Mr and Mrs Owen, the applicants, seek judicial review of a decision made by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner"), the respondent, not to remit the whole of additional tax payable pursuant to s.207(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ("the Act") upon certain provisional tax instalments remaining unpaid after the time when those instalments became due and payable.

2. The decision not to remit the whole of such additional tax but only a part was made pursuant to s.207(1A) of the Act:

"Where additional tax is due and payable by a

person under this section in relation to an

amount of tax and -

(a) the Commissioner is satisfied that -

(i) the circumstances that contributed

to the delay in payment of the tax

were not due to, or caused directly

or indirectly by, an act or omission

of the person; and

(ii) the person has taken reasonable

action to mitigate, or mitigate the

effects of, those circumstances;

(b) the Commissioner is satisfied that -

(i) the circumstances that contributed

to the delay in payment of the tax

were due to, or caused directly or

indirectly by, an act or omission of

the person;

(ii) the person has taken reasonable

action to mitigate, or mitigate the

effects of, those circumstances; and

(iii) having regard to the nature of those

circumstances, it would be fair and

reasonable to remit the additional

tax or part of the additional tax;

or

(c) the Commissioner is satisfied that there

are special circumstances by reason of

which it would be fair and reasonable to

remit the additional tax or part of the

additional tax,

the Commissioner may remit the additional tax or

part of the additional tax."

The Factual Background

3. The applicants had carried on, as partners, the practice of solicitors since 1984. They experienced cash flow problems and defaulted on payment both of income tax assessments and of quarterly provisional tax instalments.

4. On approximately 1 June 1992 the applicants' tax agent, a Mr James, sought clarification of the outstanding provisional tax instalments and income tax assessments relating to the applicants. Whether Mr James did so at the request of the applicants before contact was made with him by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office, or whether the contact was initiated by the Australian Taxation Office is not clear. In any event nothing turns upon it. What followed was a letter sent by facsimile transmission on 2 June 1992 setting out a statement of account of income tax assessments outstanding with a notation that considerable additional tax for late payments would have accrued. It was only as and from 29 January 1993 that notices of instalments of quarterly provisional tax payable notified, in addition to the particular quarterly instalment owing, arrears of instalments of provisional tax and other moneys owing by Mr and Mrs Owen.

5. Once the applicants became aware that there were large sums of provisional tax outstanding, they placed their home upon the market. They also placed an office suite in the city on the market. They sought an extension of time to make payments of provisional tax outstanding.

6. There were difficulties in selling the residential property and in December 1992 the applicants offered to pay the sum of $1,000 per week on account of the outstanding tax assessments and again applied for an extension of time to pay those assessments. That was granted by letter dated 30 December 1992, subject to the proviso that a weekly payment in respect of each of the applicants was made in the amount of $500. Each taxpayer was advised that late payment additional tax continued to accrue.

7. Ultimately the house was sold (the contract was exchanged on 2 March 1993 and completed on 8 April 1993) and on 10 March 1993 the applicants applied for the remission of the whole of the penalty tax imposed. The application referred specifically to s.207(1A)(a), (b) and (c) of the Act.

8. The application stated the primary reason for the delay in payment of tax to be:

"... an unforeseen dramatic reduction in gross

revenue of the firm during the period commencing

1st July 1992."

They referred to their attempts to sell the residence and office suite and their agreement to reduce the outstanding tax liability by payment of a weekly amount. They emphasised that they had minimised their personal commitments and taken all steps within their power to realise their assets and maximise the profitability of their business. They said they were not aware of any other steps they could have taken to reduce the indebtedness and requested the Commissioner to accept:

"that all reasonable action to mitigate the

shortfall in revenue (has been taken), and that

in all the circumstances, it would be reasonable

to remit the additional tax presently assessed,

or assessable on unpaid tax of both partners."

9. On 11 May 1993 the Commissioner agreed to reduce the additional tax payable by 50% in respect of the quarterly provisional tax instalments "post 1991". It was decided, however, that unpaid instalments prior to 1991 would attract the normal additional tax for late payment of 20% to 30 September 1992 and then 16% per annum.

10. The applicants appear to have misinterpreted the Commissioner's letter and formed the view that the Commissioner had not dealt with their application under s.207(1A)(a), (b) and (c) in relation to penalty tax imposed on quarterly provisional tax accounts and requested the Commissioner again, in a letter dated 1 June 1993, to respond to the application. They sought at the same time a statement of reasons for the decision under s.13 of the Administration Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth) ("the ADJR Act").

11. The statement of reasons dated 8 June 1993 noted that the request was considered to fall under s.207(1A)(c) rather than paras.(a) or (b) of that sub-section. The statement pointed out that the quarterly provisional tax had remained outstanding since the fourth instalment for 1989 which was due for payment on 5 June 1989 and had remained unpaid for approximately 4 years. The reason the reduction in penalties was only allowed for the period after 1 July 1992 was because the primary reason for delay in payment was due to the unforeseen dramatic reduction in gross revenue of the practice, such reduction, however, only affecting the applicants' business after 1 July 1992. The cooperation of the applicants and the efforts that they had made to extinguish the debts was also taken into account. The statement said, inter alia:

"We do not consider that your request qualifies

under section 207(1A)(a) or (b) as your efforts

to mitigate the effects of the circumstances

that contributed to the delay in payment were

not taken until some three years after the debts

fell due."

12. The applicants commenced proceedings in this Court under the ADJR Act on 6 July 1993 to review the Commissioner's decision.

13. After that application was instituted, it came to the Commissioner's attention that the calculation of additional tax on unpaid quarterly provisional tax was incorrect because the officer who had made that calculation had not taken into account the effect of Kinny v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 11 NSWLR 657 which held that upon the issue of an assessment in respect of a year of income the liability to pay provisional tax was extinguished, although additional tax payable in respect of the provisional tax unpaid during the period when it was due and payable remained recoverable by the Commissioner. This required a recalculation of additional tax on the quarterly provisional tax instalments, from time to time payable, as well as additional tax on income tax assessments unpaid. The end result was that Mr Owen owed $5,076.08 additional tax for late payment of quarterly provisional tax and Mrs Owen owed $4,050.62 as additional tax for late payment of quarterly provisional tax. These figures contrast with the sums of $11,327.13 previously said to be outstanding to Mr Owen by way of additional tax for late payment of quarterly provisional tax and $8,897.72 outstanding by Mrs Owen, these two calculations already taking into account the 50% reduction previously agreed upon.

14. The Commissioner indicated to the applicants that he was prepared to reconsider the applications for remission of the additional tax outstanding and invited submissions or other material in support of that reconsideration.

15. By letter dated 25 August 1993 the applicants made a fresh application under s.207(1A)(a), (b) and (c) for remission of the additional tax imposed on the quarterly provisional tax amounts outstanding. The response to that application came in a letter dated 24 September 1993. The letter referred to the previous invitation to submit further information and said:

"I note that no further information was supplied

by you, and as such any further review is based

solely on information previously available to

this office. In particular information

contained in your letter of 10 March 1993, and

information contained in the Application for an

Order of Review."

16. The letter noted that the decision-maker's findings were reliant solely on s.207(1A). The letter then proceeded to deal with the three grounds set out in the applicants' initial application for review under the ADJR Act. It said, inter alia:

"When determining such requests the Commissioner

is duty bound to consider all facts that he sees

as being relevant to the case at hand. I can

assure you that our decision on your request for

remission of ATLP was not taken likely.

Consideration was given to all previous

correspondence and the complete history of the

facts of the case were reviewed and given due

consideration."

17. The letter concluded as follows:

"Therefore, having studied the entire history,

of this matter, including all relevant

information that has been previously supplied to

this office by yourself, I see no reason to

change our original decision on your remission

request. I therefore submit that the amounts

shown on the enclosed reconciliations of ATLP

remain outstanding."

The initials ATLP stand for "Additional Tax for Late Payment".

18. The applicants again sought reasons under the ADJR Act in respect of the second decision refusing to remit the whole of the additional tax. The statement of reasons, which was quite brief, said relevantly as follows:

"In response to your request for a 'Statement of

Reasons', as to my decision concerning your

ATLP, I refer you to my letters dated 8 June

1993 and 24 September 1993. I feel that the

contents of these letters fully outline the

reasons for my decision, and therefore no

further information can be supplied at this time."

19. The applicants thereupon commenced the present proceedings in this Court.

20. Two arguments were advanced in support of the application for review. First it was submitted that the decision-maker, in making the application, had considered s.207(1A)(c) but had failed to address her mind to the matters in s.207(1A)(a) or (b), those paragraphs also being the express subject of the applicants' request to the Commissioner. Second it was submitted that the decision-maker had erred in law in the way in which she had approached her decision on remission because she had not considered the matter afresh but had merely sought to review the original decision made on 11 May 1993. No question arose as to the application of s.207(1A)(a).

Failure to consider s.207(1A)(b)

21. For the applicants to succeed on their first submission they must demonstrate, on the balance of probabilities, that the decision-maker considered the request for remission solely under s.207(1A)(c) and did not consider the request pursuant to s.207(1A)(b). It is not in dispute between the parties that the test to be applied under each of those paragraphs differs and that on one view of the matter it would be easier for the applicants to obtain a favourable exercise of discretion under s.207(1A)(b) than it would be under s.207(1A)(c).

22. If regard be had only to the letter of 24 September 1993 communicating the adverse decision to the applicants, the inference could readily be drawn that the only matter to which the decision-maker directed attention was s.207(1A)(c). So much emerges from the language used in that letter which refers to "special circumstances", a test relevant only to s.207(1A)(c) but not relevant to a request made under s.207(1A)(b). It was open to the applicants to leave the evidence in this state and thus leave it to the Commissioner to put on an affidavit by the decision-maker, upon which she could be cross-examined, accepting or denying the inference that she had not considered s.207(1A)(b). However, the applicants chose to tender the letter of 27 October 1993 which, in response to a request for a statement of reasons, referred back to (thereby incorporating by reference) the letters of 8 June 1993 and 24 September 1993. Both the 8 June and 24 September letters make it clear that the request was considered also under s.207(1A)(b) and rejected for reasons there given.

23. In these circumstances, I am of the view that the applicants, on the evidence, have not shown that the decision-maker failed to consider the request by reference to the criteria set out in s.207(1A)(b) and accordingly this submission must fail.

Did the decision-maker address the issue afresh?

24. The gravamen of this submission was that there was a difference between a review of an existing decision, on the one hand, and the making of a fresh decision, on the other. The review of a decision amounted, so it was said, to giving some prima facie value to the initial decision and then determining whether that initial decision was wrong. It was submitted that a fresh decision involved commencing afresh, untrammelled by any existing decision and examining the matter on its merits. The issue thus posed was whether the decision-maker merely engaged in a process of review, or whether she truly considered the request on its merits and made a decision on that basis.

25. There is some material in evidence which lends support to the submission that the decision-maker did not approach the matter afresh. There is also evidence which points in the other direction. The letter from the Attorney-General's Department to the applicants' solicitors advising of the Commissioner's miscalculation based upon a non-application of Kinney's case stated that the Commissioner was prepared to reconsider the remission applications. Reconsideration is something different from review of an existing decision. The letter of 24 September 1993 referred both to reconsideration and to "further review". It then, however, expressed the view that there was no reason to change the original decision. The letter in response to the application under s.13 of the ADJR Act is neutral in referring back to the previous correspondence and in its reference to reasons for decision.

26. On the basis of this material I do not think that the applicants have made out a case that what the decision-maker did was merely review an existing decision for error. The word "review" is capable of various meanings. It may mean a re-examination to find error, on the one hand, or it may merely mean looking at the matter again. These two meanings are to be found in the law in the context of administrative review and judicial review. A tribunal, such as the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, when it comes to review a decision does again that which the decision-maker had done before. It stands in the place of the decision-maker. In so doing the tribunal itself makes a decision, although called a review. Judicial review stands on a different footing for it exists to determine whether there is legal error in the decision to be reviewed.

27. The consideration of the applicants' request in respect of figures vitiated by the non-application of Kinney's case brought about a result that the original decision was set aside and an order to this effect was made by consent of the parties. There was thus no decision, in a legal sense, to review. The task for the decision-maker was to start again and consider all matters and arrive at a decision to remit the whole or only some part of the additional tax payable. Ms Houston, as the decision-maker of the decision under attack, was also the decision-maker at the time of the initial decision on 8 June 1993. There being no further evidence submitted to her it is not surprising that she reviewed, in a loose sense, the basis of her original decision and came to the view that it should stand. In the circumstances of the present case that amounted, in my view, to her making a new decision based on the material which she had been provided and did not amount merely to reviewing for error or giving some prima facie significance to her original decision.

28. It follows, therefore, that the second submission should also fail.

29. In these circumstances I would dismiss the application and order the applicants to pay the respondent's costs of it.
