NT86/5378-5381 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 75; 87 ATC 341 (5 March 1987)

Last Updated: 14 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - applicant leaving Australia in 1967 - touring -arriving in and marrying an Australian he met in Australia' in Canada in 1968 - Canadian Immigration Identification Card issued in Canada in 1968 and position with mining company obtained applied for position with another mining company in Australia term four years with right of election to leave after two years and to remain at company's request after four years - arrived back in Australia in April 1971 stating period of stay as permanent - company in. difficulties and receiver appointed - applicant and wife purchased land (on which residence comoleted in 1978) in 1971 and family still residing here - whether on a "visit" and entitled rebate section 160 ABA,

Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) S160 ABA

Case G64 5 TBRD 385 Case L48 79 ATC 330

He:

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No: NT86/5378-5381

AAT Decision No 3263

Date: 5 March 1987

Mr H P Stevens (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. NT86/5378-5381

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr H P Stevens (Senior Member)

Following the abandonment before the Tribunal of all claims in relation to an application for review of a decision upon an objection to his assessment for the 1971 year of income (such decision is accordingly affirmed) and other claims in respect of later years, the sole question for decision is whether or not the applicant is entitled to a rebate in terms of section 160 ABA in his assessments for the years ended 30 June 1972 to 1974 inclusive.

2. The applicant returned (see later) to Australia in April 1971 and difficulties arose in relation to obtaining the necessary certification for the purposes of the relevant provisions - the Commissioner taking the view his return did "not constitute a 'visit' within the meaning required for the application of" those provisions. It was sometime before any returns of income were lodged and all four returns were forwarded under cover of a letter of 21 November 1975 which, inter alia, said they were lodged "now so as to assist your review of" the applicant's position. Assessments (all dated 13 September 1976) issued on the basis the provisions were inapplicable and objections of 12 November 1976 followed. The relevant portions of the 1972, 1973 and 1974 objections claimed the applicant was "entitled to the rebate provided in s.160 ABA of the Act" and stated, inter alia, the applicant was on a visit and that "the Secretary of the Department of Trade or a person authorised by him in writing to give an appropriate certificate should have certified, and the Treasurer should have been satisfied that the retention of my services in Australia beyond the first year will assist or has assisted in the development of Australian industry".

3. Notices disallowing the relevant grounds issued on 19 June 1978 and requests for reference to a tribunal followed on 18 August 1978. Further correspondence ensued and by letter of 23 June 1980 the applicant's agents were advised certification had taken place. However it was made clear that this was without prejudice to the Commissioner and that its issue had "been made basically to resolve the unfortunate position in which (the applicant) found himself in regard to getting the issues ... properly before a Board of Review". In due course the references were transmitted to the tribunal and were set down for hearing in February 1983. However the applicant was ill and the hearing was adjourned with his representative "to approach the board on his behalf as soon as I know that he has recovered". This hearing "resumed" before the Tribunal on 1 December 1986 as a result of action by the Tribunal rather than any approach to it.

4. Section 160 ABA provides:

(1) Where a taxpayer derives income as director's fees, salary or wages (in this section referred to as "the remuneration") during a visit to Australia during which he acts as a director, manager or other administrative officer of, or is employed as a consultant, technician or operative in, a manufacturing, mercantile or mining business or a business of primary production, and the remuneration is not exempt under sub-paragraph (vii) of paragraph (c) of section 23, that person shall subject to this section and to section 24AA, be entitled to a rebate in his assessment.

(2) The rebate shall be allowed -
  • (a) in respect of the remuneration derived during the first year of the visit; and
  • (b) in respect of the remuneration derived during any of the second, third and fourth years of the visit if the Secretary to the Department of Overseas Trade, or a person authorised in writing by him to give such certificates, certifies, and the Treasurer is satisfied, that the retention of the taxpayer's services in Australia during the relevant year will assist, or has assisted, in the development of Australian industry.

(3) The rebate to which the taxpayer shall be entitled under sub-section (1) shall be an amount equal to the excess (if any) of the amount by which the income tax under this Act which would, but for this section, be payable by him in respect of his income of the year of income is increased by the inclusion of the remuneration in his assessable income over the amount of income tax which he is liable to pay in respect of the remuneration in the country in which he is ordinarily resident, or which he would be liable to pay in respect thereof in that country if it were earned by him in that country.

The above quotation is from the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1973 and contains some amendments made by Acts No 51, 52 and 216 of 1973. However these do not alter the basic terms applicable to all three years of income.

  1. The issue to be determined is whether the applicant was on a visit to Australia and this question is one of fact or degree to be determined after a consideration of the whole of the facts involved. In this regard evidence was given by the applicant (A), his brother-in-law (B) and another person (C).
  2. Dealing now with the factual situation A was born overseas to Australian parents in 1942 and was "repatriated back to Australia" with his mother in 1945 - his nationality was Australian. He went to school in Sydney and did a metallurgical degree as a cadet with BHP - at Newcastle University. He continued to work for the company for around another year whilst he undertook a post-graduate course. A then did a trip around Australia before leaving in 1967 - whilst on this trip he applied in Canberra for entry into Canada (details of application not before Tribunal). He left Australia holding an Australian passport and toured New Zealand before eventually arriving in Canada in 1968 - Canadian Immigration Identification Card issued Vancouver 25 March 1968. A had made no arrangements concerning employment in Canada but he obtained (date unstated) a position at a mine in northern Canada. He also (date unstated but in 1968) married in Canada an Australian girl he had met at Newcastle University and they lived in a company house in the company's mining township.

7. Later A read an advert in a Canadian mining paper concerning a position in Australia with a mining company of which he had learnt through an Australian shareholding in his Canadian employer. He applied, was interviewed in Vancouver by the managing director of the Australian mining company (X) and received a letter of 12 February 1971 in the following terms:-

I have given much thought to the situation and here is a proposal that I hope you will consider very seriously.

  1. We would start you out as Assistant Mill Superintendent at an annual salary of A$18,000. The job of Mill Superintendent would be made available to you 12 months after joining (X) assuming of course normal relations between you and (X) at that time. You will remember my reservations in Vancouver and I trust that you will see our position in this matter.
  2. Other Considerations -
    • (a) An executive house will be made available to you at a minimum weekly rental that will satisfy the Australian Tax authorities. This house will be equipped with drapes and carpeting as well as major electrical appliances namely stove, refrigerator, clothes washer and dryer.
    • (b) A rental car will be made available for business purposes.
    • (c) You qualify for the four year tax break (as an industrial expert) that we discussed in Vancouver.
      • (d) The company will pay a $3,000 (Can) relocation expense for loss on furniture and car and in addition 400 cubic feet of ocean freight for any special furniture and personal effects you may wish to bring to Australia.
      • (e) Air transportation will be provided for you and your family from (Canada) to (closest town) via Sydney, economy class.
      • (f) I believe that you are aware of Australian policies concerning holidays.
      • (g) Medical insurance, group and life insurance, disability etc policies will be established and comparable to plans in effect in major Canadian companies.

An attached five page circular, inter alia, stated:-

TO WHOM IT MAY CONCERN:

(X) is now in the process of constructing a mill near......, New South Wales It is anticipated that a number of experienced people in mining and mill operations will be employed in this operation and they will come from countries other than Australia.

The purpose of this letter is to outline to such prospective employees the general terms and conditions upon which their services would be engaged by our company and further more to provide you with specific information which you will require in the course of moving to Australia. The various matters are dealt with under the separate headings listed below:-

TERMS OF EMPLOYMENT;

It is intended that each employee would serve for a period of four years.

INCOME TAX:

It will be apparent that the four year term is most desirable from the stand-point of the individual.

Application is made initially to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation giving reasons why expatriate has been recruited from overseas to fill a certain position. Details of Australian income and normal dependent deductions are also required.

On approval by Taxation Department, expatriate pays the same tax in Australia as he would have paid in his own country.

Applications for the second, third and fourth years are made to the Department of Trade and these applications require information particularly in respect of training of personnel to ultimately fill position of expatriate.

These taxation benefits only apply for four years.

RETURN TO HOME COUNTRY:

If the employee elects to return to his own country at the completion of his four year term, the cost of return would be for the account of the company, in addition to out-of-pocket expenses incurred for the transferring of 400 cubic feet of personal effects.

If the employee elects to return at the completion of two years of the four year contract, the cost will be shared equally by the company and the employee.

If, at the end of the four year contract, the employee wishes to remain in the employ of the company, at the request of the company, new arrangements may be effected with the company.

8. With some minor adjustment A accepted the offer on 1 March 1971. In reply to a questionaire of 7 March 1975 A by letter of 1 May 1975 advised, inter alia, that "the appointment was for a period of four years and subject to negotiation at the end of that period". In March 1971 his wife was expecting their first child and, after the birth on 3 April 1971 the family left Canada arriving in Australia on 24 April 1971. The Incoming Passenger Card signed by A stated the purpose of the journey to Australia as "Returning to live in Australia" and intended length of stay in Australia as "Permanent". His Canadian Short Individual Income Tax Return indicated, inter alia, he had ceased to be a resident of Canada on 18 April 1971. No real estate had been owned in Canada and upon their departure some books and clothing were left with a friend whilst some $100 X was left in a bank account. His Canadian ex-employer indicated to him there would be employment opportunities if he desired to return whilst A said they had interests in Canada that could be considered also - no concluded arrangement however with either made.

  1. Upon arrival in Australia the family lived in a house provided by X in a town close to mine site. Later in 1971 B and his wife were acquiring a vacant property with a view to building on it. However they had an opportunity to travel overseas and rather than have the sale fall through B who thought "it was a very good investment" interested A in the land. The land was acquired in the name of B and his wife but by memorandum of transfer of 16 February 1972 it was transferred for $1 from them to A and his wife. A bank loan (application not sighted) was obtained to provide the funds for the acquisition. Subsequently a family home was built on this land (see later).
  2. B in evidence deposed as to interesting A and his wife in the "purchase" of the property - although he was vague as to the actual mechanics of the purchase and subsequent transfer to A and his wife. It was sought to have him also depose as to the intentions of A in relation to his return to Australia. However B conceded that although he "understood that the family had emmigrated to Canada ... I may be wrong" whilst he agreed there had been no discussion of A's future. B was also asked whether during the period he and his wife were overseas there had been any documentation indicating any intention of A "to remain in Australia or return to Canada" to which he responded, inter alia, ("there was no documentation". The final question asked in chief was "So, during the period 1971 to 1974 and during the period afterwards, there is nothing that you can specifically draw the Tribunal's attention to at this stage on the question of (A's) intentions to remain in Australia or return to Canada; is that correct" and it is fair to say the answer was there was nothing.

11. Soon after A's arrival and commencement of employment at the mine site various difficulties were experienced in relation to the mining operations and despite attempts to overcome these difficulties, X went into receivership. No evidence was led as to the date of receivership nor was the effect of such' receivership on A's contract canvassed by either advocate. A remained because the receivers were placing the technical success of the project on him and "how on earth could I ... tell them I was going to leave". He also thought there was a very good chance of making X a profit earning company - it did make a profit in 1976 but the "weakening Canadian dollar and the strengthening Australian dollar ... wiped the profit out" (mineral sold world wide in Canadian dollars). In September 1975 the whole family went for a holiday including Canada, Germany, South Africa to see what prospects there were in those countries. There were some prospects but if he had left "it would of had very severe effect on the operation. My work, it was still all there. It was difficult for me to do that". In 1977 A left X and the family came to live in Sydney (B returned at the end of 1974, discussions re designing a residence took place in 1975 and construction on land acquired in 1971 completed late 1978 or early 1979). When asked why he did not return to Canada A said the situation had changed radically during the years 1975-1977 and "opportunities for employment within the industry were shrinking". However he continued as a consultant in relation to a new process on which he was working in collaboration with another company Y - if successful first plant would be built for X and then they would franchise the process and build plants overseas. A and his family are still in Australia.

12. Evidence was led from C - another who came in 1971 from Canada (but from a different previous employer) to work for X to the effect that he frequently discussed the situation of not knowing "whether we would have a job here" ie at X. There were other discussions as to whether having regard to certain trends they would be "able to obtain a job back in Canada". C did try to obtain a position in the same mining industry outside of Australia in 1974, again in 1976 and 1980 but was unsuccessful because there "were not any jobs available" due to "the state of the industry". As a result C did not leave Australia and is still here. A also made enquiries concerning overseas employment and deposed that although he could have obtained employment back in Canada and in South Africa too he couldn't leave X - "the responsibility for the operations ... (was mine) and I felt that I had to see it through".

13. The evidence of both A and C was vague as to any specific dates of discussion. As indicated earlier difficulties were experienced almost from the date of A's arrival whilst X eventually went into receivership. The impression gained was that A was prepared to stay in Australia as long as was necessary to suit his own ends (meaning whatever suited him personally) whilst the contract entered into allowed for extension beyond four years. The other person who could have assisted was A's wife the girl he met in Newcastle and married in Canada and whose family lived in Australia - but she was not called to testify. No explanation was given for such failure and the Tribunal could be pardoned for inferring her evidence would not have assisted A.

14. On behalf of A his representative referred to Case L48 79 ATC 330 as being the only authority on section 160 ABA and said it was necessary to simply apply the words of the Act. The provisions do not say they are to apply only to people who have no connections in Australia and there is no indication that an Australian cannot subsequently visit Australia. He said the facts showed "the taxpayer was, in his own mind, assuming a short term employment prospect and in his own mind, was intending to leave Australia". The fact what was intended did not happen was irrelevant as it arose out of factors not in contemplation at the time the offer was accepted. In relation to sub-section (3) the applicant was "ordinarily resident" in Canada - that being his situation immediately prior to acceptance and nothing done subsequently could change that (assuming he was not returning to take up permanent residence in Australia). Reference was made in this regard to Case G64 5 TBRD 385 per Mr Bock at p 393.

15. In relation to the answer given by A on his incoming passenger card comfort was drawn also from Case L48 wherein Dr Beck said, at pp 336/337, that the taxpayer there concerned was "a man who fills in such forms in a way that he believes will make the path smoothest rather than as evidence that he came with the intention of settling permanently". However it is trite law that a particular finding made in another case cannot be relied upon in the case of a different individual. A finding can only be made on the facts of each individual case. It might also be observed that, in any event, Dr Beck was in the fortunate position of having evidence led from three other witnesses (the taxpayer's wife, brother-in-law and father-in-law) to assist him and as Dr Beck stated such evidence outweighed "the negative effect of the doubtful credibility of D and his response on the passenger cards". In the present case the evidence of the applicant is the only evidence and, in this regard, his explanation as to why he filled the card in as he did was not very convincing.

16. For the Commissioner it was submitted, that the applicant "became a resident of Australia from the date of his arrival in Australia" and that for the section to apply it was necessary to find that he had retained a sufficient connection with Canada that he could be said to be "ordinarily resident there". It was said that a visit in terms of section 160 ABA could continue for up to four years but, for there to be a visit, there had to be the continuing connection with another country to which "he may, ultimately, plan to return". Case L48 was distinguishable because there the taxpayer was a Canadian citizen (married to a Canadian) who appeared to have all his connections in Canada. Here there was an "Australian" married to an Australian (families in Australia) without sufficient continuing connection with Canada. The contract with X provided for renegotiation at the end of four years and there was no evidence of any definite arrangement to return to Canada at the end of two or four years. In relation to a statement that the applicant felt bound to return "because it was his belief that after four years in Australia his VIE status would expire" it was submitted this "was just an economic factor which would have some weight on his particular decision" although as could "be seen from subsequent events it was not of sufficient weight, or significance to in fact make him leave this country". His overall submission was that when the applicant "came to Australia in 1976, he did so not as a visitor but, as indicated on his migration card, as an Australian returning to live in Australia with an intention of residing here permanently".

17. The Tribunal agrees with the submission made on behalf of the applicant ie that it is necessary to simply apply the words of the Act to the facts of the particular case before it. Accordingly as set out in para 5 the issue is one of fact or degree. If it be accepted A so came here but then subsequently decided to remain in Australia it could not be said thereafter that he derived income "during a visit to Australia". In A's favour is his assertion that he thought he had to leave at the end of four years (or else he would not obtain the "tax break") but the contract was not a simple four year contract. It was always envisaged that the actual period of employment could exceed four years and, whilst a "last minute" change of mind to remain would not necessarily disqualify, an earlier change of mind to remain indefinitely thereafter (even though not formally notified until later) would disqualify. Also against A is the acquisition of land soon after arrival and his continued presence here since 1971.

18. Taking all the evidence into account the Tribunal is not satisfied on the ordinary balance of probabilities that A did come to Australia with the intention of remaining here for a period not exceeding four years and then leaving Australia again. Accordingly the Tribunal is unable to find that A derived income "during a visit to Australia".

19. For the above reasons the Tribunal affirms the decisions of the Commissioner upon the applicant's objections to his assessments for the years of income ended 30 June 1971 to 1974 inclusive.
