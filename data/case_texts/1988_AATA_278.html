AT87/238 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 278; (1988) 19 ATR 3911; 88 ATC 923 (15 September 1988)

Last Updated: 3 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax - deductions - whether travel undertaken by school teacher deductible - onus of proof

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss.51(1), 190(b), 223, 227

FC of T v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

Case V74 88 ATC 529

Case R73 84 ATC 507

Case J46 77 ATC 421

Case J47 77 ATC 423

FC of T v Hatchett 71 ATC 4184

FC of T v Kropp 76 ATC 4406

Grapsas v Unger (DFC of T) 86 ATC 4588

Case V81 88 ATC 557

Lunney v Commissioner of Taxation (1957-1958) 100 CLR 478

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

15 September 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. The applicant in this reference is a physical education teacher at a secondary school. In the year of income ending 30 June 1986 ("fiscal 1986") he claimed inter alia a deduction under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") for travel expenses of $1,989. In an assessment issued on 24 October 1986 his claim to deduct these travel expenses was accepted. On 9 December 1986 an amended assessment was issued disallowing $1,537 of the expenses claimed. This assessment also included an amount of $320 as additional tax.
    1. In his evidence the applicant stated that he had been teaching for five years. As a physical education teacher he is required to teach physical education and health education to students in Years 7 to 10. He also teaches a subject called Human Relations to Year 10 students, outdoor education to students in Years 9 and 10, and an advanced physical education elective. The school has some forty to fifty sporting teams in various competitions throughout the year and in consequence the applicant also coaches two teams per term each year. As a member of the physical education staff the applicant organises swimming, athletic and cross-country carnivals as directed. On occasions he is also required to contribute to updating subject curricula.
    2. The applicant's interest in physical education and sport does not stop there. He has also obtained official coaching qualifications through the National Coaching Accreditation Scheme. At present he has level 2 status in soccer coaching, level 1 status in volley-ball coaching and level 0 in soft cross. The top coaching level in each of these sports is level 3. In order to obtain coaching qualifications the applicant had to coach a soccer and volley-ball team for at least one season. Since his school sporting teams did not participate in their competitions for more than six weeks he had to coach teams not associated with his school to satisfy the coaching prerequisites.
  2. Once he had coached the teams for the requisite periods he was allowed to embark on courses leading to the various coaching levels. On the coaching courses the applicant apparently developed his knowledge about many subjects related to teaching physical education. Some of the topics with which the courses dealt were human physiology, anatomy, biomechanics, skills acquisition, and human grace and development. He also obtained information on sports medicine, the prevention of sports injuries and the use of drugs in sport. In addition he learned about coaching philosophy, coaching of juniors, new rules in each sport and the adaptation of rules to younger players. The applicant stated that this knowledge helped him to be a better teacher on a day-to-day basis. He also said that had he not undertaken these courses the only other way of obtaining this information would have been to enrol in the relevant units at a tertiary institution.
  3. The applicant hopes to obtain level 3 status in soccer coaching and level 2 status in volley-ball coaching. To this end, in order to be eligible to enter the relevant coaching courses, where he will acquire more information useful to his teaching, and to obtain these coaching qualifications, he must coach one soccer team for three seasons in addition to the previous two seasons required for level 2 status, and a volley-ball team for two seasons. In fiscal 1986 he coached a soccer and a volley-ball team with these requirements in mind. The soccer team was a second division team in a local competition and the volley-ball team was an under-15 year old state representative team.
  4. The expenses which the applicant sought to claim in the relevant year related to the cost of travel to and from the teams' coaching sessions and games. The Commissioner disallowed the cost of this travel, incurred in fiscal 1986, $595 of which related to the soccer team and $942 of which related to the volley-ball team. In order to meet his soccer coaching commitments, the applicant claimed to have travelled 1920 kilometres to destination C. His volley-ball commitments were said to have required him to travel some 3040 kilometres, being 2800 kilometres to W and 240 kilometres to S. In both cases the applicant claimed thirty-one cents per kilometre. The Commissioner did not as such contest that the applicant had actually travelled the claimed number of kilometres, nor did he contest that thirty-one cents per kilometre was an appropriate amount to claim. As was indicated above, the applicant was required to travel to three different locations to perform his coaching jobs. When asked by the Tribunal whether he travelled from school or from home to get to these places he said: "The [S] one was all from home, they were on weekends. Most of the [W] ones would have been from home - from school. I would have gone from school. There would have been a couple I would have gone from home." It should be noted that the applicant was not paid for his work in either of the coaching jobs.
  5. The Principal of the applicant's school was also called as a witness by the applicant. He gave evidence as to what factors would be taken into account in appointing a senior teacher in physical education, a position the applicant seeks in the future. The Principal, who is involved in selection of staff at his school, and who has been a Principal for ten years, stated that he would look for a person who could demonstrate expertise in physical education and sport, control of children in sport, involvement in sport at the school and in the "wider sphere of sport". He said that he thought that it would be essential for that person to have coaching qualifications, and reiterated what the applicant had said about the need to obtain coaching experience outside the school system. The Principal stressed in his evidence that competition to attain the position of senior teacher was extremely intense.

8. The applicant's 1986 tax return was prepared by his accountants. The tax return included a two page handwritten schedule which itemised various expenses said to be incurred in earning his income. The relevant parts of this schedule for the purpose of this application are set out below:

"TRAVEL EXPENSES - HOLDEN 186 - 6CYL TRANSPORTING STUDENTS TO VARIOUS SPORTING VENUES & TEACHING FACILITIES IN & AROUND

... 852 Km

PARENT/TEACHER EVENINGS - 6 TRIPS

SOCIAL EVENINGS - 3 TRIPS

9 TRIPS x 36k(net) 324 km

COACHING OF 2nd DIVISION SOCCER TEAM - ... LEAGUE - 2 NIGHTS TRAINING & 1 GAME/WEEK DURING SEASONS (FROM FEB TO SEPT.) - GAMES & TRAINING HELD AT [C] - CLAIM AV. RETURN TRIP = 20KM/GAME OR 60KM/WEEK x 32WKS -

C'FWD - (1-2)

ITEM 18 OTHER EXPENDITURE INCURRED

B/FWD (SCHED 1-1) 3096 KM $493

TRAVEL EXPENSES (continued - School Use) COACHING UNDER 15'S VOLLEY BALL ... TEAM - TRAINING ONE NIGHT PER WEEK - TRAVEL TO [W] &

RETURN = 70 KM (Return) x 40WKS = 2800

EXTRA TRAVEL TO [S]

COLLEGE @ 30KM (Return) x 8 Trips = 240 PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT COURSE 'LEVEL 1 VOLLEY BALL COACHING

COURSE' HELD AT ... TAFE 7 DAYS

20 KM x RETURN = 40KM x 7 DAYS =

TOTAL DISTANCE TRAVELLED -

For School & Teaching Purposes therefore 6416 KM x 31 cents per km = $1,989"

The Commissioner claimed that the applicant had made a false statement in his tax return. It was submitted that the reference to "For School and Teaching Purposes" was incorrect because the travel had not been for teaching purposes per se.

  1. The applicant's representative contended that the travelling expenses relating to his coaching commitments were deductible under s. 51(l) of the Act because they were a necessary prerequisite to obtaining coaching qualifications which would directly assist the applicant to earn income in two ways. First, they would assist him in teaching on a day-to-day basis, and secondly they would enhance his career prospects.
  2. In relation to the argument that the coaching qualifications would assist the applicant in his teaching on a day-to-day basis the applicant's representative relied upon F.C. of T. v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60. In his judgment therein Windeyer J. said at 70: "...a taxpayer who gains income by the exercise of his skill in some profession or calling and who incurs expenses in maintaining or increasing his learning, knowledge, experience and ability in that profession or calling necessarily incurs those expenses in carrying on his profession or calling". It was submitted that the applicant is such a taxpayer. He is a professional person and incurred expenses in "maintaining or increasing" his knowledge. The courses, which are part of the process of obtaining the coaching qualifications, are self-development courses directly associated with the nature of the work undertaken by the applicant. The judgment of Kitto J. at 69 in Finn was also referred to: "I am of the same opinion, and even if the relevance of the expenditure to the respondent's prospects of promotion were to be put on one side I should consider that enough would remain in the facts of the case to entitle him to the deduction he has claimed". The applicant's representative placed reliance on Case V74 88 ATC 529, a case decided by this Tribunal. In that case I decided that the cost of a canoe expedition undertaken by a school teacher was deductible because it was "part and parcel" of maintaining the high standards required of her in competently undertaking her work. Case R73 84 ATC 507 was also referred to by the applicant's representative. In that case a so-called master art teacher claimed deductions for expenses incurred on an overseas trip during which she attended an art conference and visited art galleries. Taxation Board of Review No. 1 decided that the expenditure was incidental to her adding to her knowledge and understanding of art education.
  3. Since coaching qualifications are necessary to increase the applicant's chances of promotion to senior teacher, it was submitted that the travel expenses incurred in gaining the qualifications in question should be deductible. Evidence on this matter was provided by both the applicant and his principal. The proposition that expenditure incurred to increase the possibility of promotion would be deductible is given support in Finn amongst other cases. The applicant's representative sought to distinguish Case J46 77 ATC 421 and Case J47 77 ATC 423 from the present case. Both of these cases involved school teachers who claimed expenses related to retention of positions in their State Science Teacher's Association. It was argued by each that holding a position in the association increased his chances of promotion as a teacher. In both cases the No.2 Board of Review decided amongst other things that the connection between promotion and the expenses claimed was too tenuous. It was submitted for the applicant that the correctness of these decisions should be reconsidered. In my opinion Case J46 and Case J47 are distinguishable if only on the basis that in those cases there could be no more than a tenuous connection between the positions held and the promotion prospects of the teachers in question.
  4. The Commissioner submitted that since no extra income was derived by the applicant's non-school coaching work only the first limb of s. 51(l) of the Act was applicable and that therefore the expenses claimed must be referable to his salary income earned as a teacher. It was conceded that to qualify for deductibility the expenses did not have to relate merely to the 1986 fiscal year as long as they resulted in promotion or advancement in the future, as was stated in F.C. of T. v Hatchett 71 ATC 4184. The Commissioner's representative also acknowledged that physical education is an exacting science and that teachers of this subject are professionals who must keep abreast of new developments. It was also said that the Commissioner did not dispute that the coaching courses were important to the applicant's work.
  5. The submission put for the Commissioner however was that the applicant's claim was not allowable. It was not comparable with the facts which arose in F.C. of T. v Kropp 76 ATC 4406. In that case a taxpayer who belonged to a large chartered accounting firm resigned his position and went to Canada to work with an affiliated firm. At the end of his time abroad he was re-employed by the Australian firm with a promotion and an increase in pay. It was held by Waddell J. that the expense of the airfare was deductible because it was part of a plan by the taxpayer to increase his experience and therefore aid his promotion prospects. There was a "perceived connection" between the expenditure and the taxpayer's assessable income so that it could be said that the expenditure was "incurred in gaining the assessable income" in future years in Australia. In the present case it was submitted that the applicant had not applied for a higher position and thus has not been promoted.
  6. It was contended by the Commissioner that Case V74 was distinguishable from the present application because in that case the taxpayer had undertaken an extra-arduous wilderness expedition which was so rigorous that it was inexplicable on any other basis. The Commissioner further submitted that Case J46 and Case J47 are on all fours with the present application. In those cases the taxpayers perceived that the expenditure would help their promotion to higher teacher positions. In fact the Commissioner argued their cases were stronger because the taxpayers actually earned some income from their involvement with their association. here, it was submitted, the applicant undertook the coaching work because he had a love of soccer and volley-ball. No deduction, therefore, should be allowable because the outgoings were of a domestic or private nature.
  7. As to the general merits of the claim, I substantially agree with the submissions made by the applicant's representative as to the deductibility of expenses which related to the increase of the applicant's professional skills in teaching and to the enhancement of his career prospects. Finn's Case supports the notion that a professional will be entitled to a deduction for expenses related to "maintaining or increasing his knowledge". I reiterate everything I said in Case V74. I reject the argument that the applicant in that case succeeded only because the canoe trip was very arduous. Like here, the activity there was undertaken better to enable the teacher to carry out her duties. It allowed her to maintain and increase her knowledge and skills. Finn's Case is also authority for the proposition that expenditure incurred to increase the chances of promotion will be deductible. While this part of the applicant's argument was perhaps not so strong, there was evidence provided by the applicant's principal that the coaching qualifications were necessary for promotion to a higher level. I see no reason to add anything further on this point, for in my opinion the applicant's case fails, in terms of the particular claims made, because the applicant did not discharge the onus of proof required to be satisfied under s.190(b) of the Act.
  8. As has been indicated earlier, the claimed deductions related to expenses travelling to three destinations, C, S, and W to conduct coaching. It was unclear from the return as to whether the travel to each of these destinations was to and from home or school, being his workplace. This aspect of the case was not clarified adequately at the hearing. In fact the only question asked of the applicant on this point was by the Tribunal. The applicant's evidence on this point appears in paragraph 6 of these Reasons for Decision. As was stated there, the travel to and from S was from home. As to travel to and from W the applicant gave a fairly ambiguous answer. At best most of this travel was from school but presumably the travel from the coaching sessions would have been to his home. The Tribunal received no evidence relating to travel to and from S. I do not suggest that this evidence was withheld whether deliberately or otherwise. I am left with the impression that it was not possible to prove the more detailed parts of the claims.
  9. In Lunney v Commissioner of Taxation (1957-1958) 100 CLR 478, and in many cases since, it has been held that the cost of travel between home and work is not deductible. In this application the costs of travel to and from destination C is not deductible as this was from home each time. As to travel to and from destination S, there was no evidence forthcoming and therefore the onus of proof was not discharged. In the case of the costs related to travel to destination W, the evidence was ambiguous and since the applicant did not state how many times he travelled from home it is not possible to apportion the claimed expenditure into deductible and non-deductible components. In addition the travel from W was probably to his home and therefore half of the cost relating to W coaching commitments would not be deductible. All in all I am simply left without adequate proof of the travel that occurred, and although I can deduce the likelihood that some of the travel would qualify for deductibility, given the favourable view which, as expressed above, I took of the applicant and of his attempts to advance himself in his career, I am left without an adequate foundation for arriving at any kind of apportionment.
  10. The next question for determination is the question of additional tax. It was argued by the applicant's representative that, in the event that the Tribunal did not allow the applicant the deductions which he claims, the additional tax of 40 per cent of the tax unpaid should be remitted and only 20 per cent should be payable. The Commissioner's representative in his submissions supported the imposition of the additional tax for which the applicant was assessed. The applicant was assessed as liable to additional tax of double the difference between the tax properly payable by the applicant and the tax payable on the basis that the statements in the return were true. The additional tax was remitted to 40 per cent under s.227 of the Act. Although the return was prepared by the applicant's accountants it was submitted that the applicant was responsible for any misstatement contained in the return. Authority for this proposition is contained in the majority decision of Wilson, Brennan and Dawson JJ. in Grapsas v Unger (D.F.C. of T.) 86 ATC 4588 where it was held that the now repealed s.230, which related to understatement of income, did not apply to tax agents who prepared their clients tax returns. The wording of s. 223(1) (a) is even more clear than the repealed section because it commences with the words "Where a taxpayer ... ".

19. The Commissioner referred to Case V81 88 ATC 557 in which Mr. P.M. Roach, Senior Member, discussed inter alia the effect of s.223 and s.227 of the Act. It was submitted that the error that was made in the present case was one that was referred to in paragraph 66 at 571 by Mr. Roach. It was an error in the description of the claim - the return inaccurately described the claim. Paragraph 70 at 572 which was also referred to says:

"I next observe that the section does not require that the statement should have misled the Commissioner. It is sufficient that the statement be false, whether or not it deceives; or misleading whether or not it misleads. Nor does a statement escape being false or misleading because of a view honestly held by the maker of the statement that it is not so. (Reliance Finance Corporation Pty. Limited v. F.C. of T. 87 ATC 4146)."

20. I thought the applicant an honest and straight forward person. The information set out in his return in the schedule referred to in paragraph 8 of these reasons, however, was misleading and disingenuous. The claims in relation to the coaching are wedged in amongst other claims to deduct expenditure firmly related to teaching activities. More particularly, the impression is then given by the use in the tax return of the words "For School and Teaching Purposes" that the travel expenses the subject of this application related directly to the coaching of school teams as opposed to outside coaching commitments to teams outside the school. A reasonable person, knowing that the applicant was a school teacher, would be unlikely to think anything else.

  1. Although he signed it, the return was not prepared by the applicant but by his accountants and perhaps that is where the problem lies. As was stated in the majority decision in Grapsas and made explicit by the wording of s.223 it is, however, the taxpayer who has an obligation not to make a statement which is false and misleading in a material particular. The applicant, therefore, is responsible for any acts or omissions relating to the contents of his tax return.
  2. I consider that the decision made by the Commissioner to assess the applicant as being liable to 40 per cent additional tax should be affirmed. The applicant's representative referred to various passages in Mr. Roach's decision in Case V81, but I could find nothing in that case that supported the view that the additional tax should be remitted still further to 20 per cent in this case. As was submitted by the Commissioner the misleading statement falls into the category, discussed by Mr. Roach at 571, of misdescription of a claim. The statements made in the applicant's tax return were misleading and they were misleading in a material way. It should always be remembered that the Act provides for a penalty of 200 per cent of the unpaid tax attributable to a false or misleading statement. In this case, the Commissioner has seen fit to reduce that amount to 40 per cent and I am unable to detect any considerations which should justify a further reduction.
  3. For the above reasons the objection decisions under review must be affirmed.
