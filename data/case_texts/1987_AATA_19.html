WT86/2586 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 19; (1987) 18 ATR 3232; 87 ATC 243 (28 January 1987)

Last Updated: 9 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - assessable income - profit from sale of office block - project undertaken by incorporated group of architects -office block erected in joint venture by architects with stockbroking group and . building company - project offered for sale before completion and sold at price substantially in excess of expectations but outside twelve months - taxpayers asserting intention that acquisition and development of the site for long term investment - finding with regard to at least one of joint venturers that that joint venturer's intention was profit making by sale - relevant time for assessing the intention of taxpayer under first limb of sub-section 26(a).

Income Tax - assessable income - one "one-off" transaction of a kind not commonly associated with the carrying on of business of architecture - whether capital profit.

Income Tax - assessable income - profit from sale of office block - consideration of the relevance of intention for second limb of subsection 26(a).

Income Tax - assessable income - full and true disclosure.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1930) SS25(1),26(a),26AAA,226.

FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355, 39 ALR 521; 12 ATR 692; 82 ATC 4031.

McClelland v C of T [1970] HCA 39; (1970) 120 CLR 487; 2 ATR 21; 70 ATC 4115; [1971] 1 WLR 191; [1971] 1 All ER 969.

The Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v FC of T [1950] HCA 16; (1950) 81 CLR 188

XCO Pty Ltd v FC of T [1971] HCA 37; (1971) 124 CLR 343; 2 ATR 353; 71 ATC 4125

North Coast Grazing Pty Ltd v FC of T, 86 ATC 4942

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: WT86/2586

APPLICANT And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

No: WT86/2586

AAT Decision No 3175

Date: 28 January 1987

Place: Perth

Dr P Gerber (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) WT86/2586

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: WT86/2586

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

In this case, the various bit players were given star billing, while the "hero" of this drama was relegated to one line in the chorus - "I particpated in this venture for purposes of long term investment". Be that as it may, once I accept his evidence - as I do - that is really the end of the case.

  1. The issue is whether profit from the sale of an office block at 33 Straw Street, West Penrith, (hereinafter referred to as "The West Penrith Project") is subject to tax pursuant to sec 25(1) and/or either of the two limbs of sec. 26(a).
  2. At all relevant times Mr Roe, the taxpayer, was a director of Mainwaring & Cholmondley ("Cholmondley"), an incorporated group of Penrith architects. All directors in due course also became directors of Marshall Pty Ltd, the corporate vehicle used for the acquisition and development of the West Penrith Project. In quick succession this is what occurred. In early 1979, 33 Straw Street, West Penrith, was a vacant block. Subsequent to the taking of an option in April 1979, it was acquired on 25 June 1979, an office block erected thereon and sold - fully let - shortly after completion in October 1980 for a substantial profit. Indeed the evidence makes it abundantly clear, and I so find, that the project was offered for sale well before completion and much time was spent in the evidence-in-chief and cross-examination on the numerous witnesses in seeking an explanation of this odd feature with respect to a project which, so it was alleged, was acquired and developed for purposes of long term investment.

4. I propose to set out the material facts as I find them after a review of all of the relevant evidence. The first thing to observe is that Mr Roe's testimony must be approached with some caution in the sense that it had all the hallmarks of a three-legged race in which Mr Roe is tied to his fellow director, Mr Cholmondley. To the extent that Roe can be said to have had any intention at all, it was the intention of Cholmondley. It was Cholmondley who was the spiritus rector, and it was his vision, planning and drive which was responsible for this group of architects becoming involved in the project. Mr Roe, although an active architect within the group, played no role whatever in the acquisition, planning, letting and finally selling of the West Penrith Project. To the extent that he deposed that his intention was to participate in the project in order to achieve a long term investment, I accept his evidence subject to the reservation that I am satisfied that he would have "gone along" with anything that Cholmondley put to him as a fellow director of Mainwaring & Cholmondley. It is thus Cholmondley's evidence which is crucial to the outcome of this application.

5. Mr Cholmondley impressed as an honest witness, although I must conclude that time has clouded his recollection of the feverish sales activities of the several estate agents during the various stages of construction. I shall return to this when I come to deal with that evidence. Mr Cholmondley deposed that he was aware of the fact that another firm of Penrith architects -Bing and Associates - had erected and let an office block in West Penrith in conjunction with a firm of stockbrokers consisting at that time of Messrs Westminster and Wade ("Wade"). His own experience with a number of computer-oriented firms had convinced him that there was a market for buildings which incorporated the special facilities required by such companies. Knowing that the site of 33 Straw Street was for sale at what seemed to him a price substantially below the market price, he evinced considerable enthusiasm for a project similar to the Bing-Wade project when the latter approached him on a social occasion to sound him out as to whether he (Cholmondley) would be interested in such a joint venture in association with Wade. After some discussion with the directors of Mainwaring & Cholmondley, Cholmondley, on behalf of his group and himself, agreed to join Wade and his group with the aim of erecting an office building containing the special design features required by computer oriented companies. At this stage nothing was reduced to writing save that the parties adopted a business name - Special Features Group. In quick succession the detailed proposal, incorporating special computer oriented features, was addressed to one hoped for tenant - Hightech Pty Ltd - on 2 March, 1979, offering to lease accommodation designed to meet that company's special needs on a long term lease basis: "The building to be available for installation of computers on arrival early in 1980." It is worth recalling that at this point in time, the Special Features Group, consisting of a couple of stockbrokers and a fist full of architects, was offering an office block not yet built on land which it did not own and over which it held no option. From this point onwards, both groups did some detailed feasibility studies (all of which were put in evidence) and which appeared both viable and potentially profitable investments. All save one involved the sole participation of the Wade-Cholmondley groups. However, Cholmondley and Wade finally concluded that the most attractive option would involve the introduction of a third party capable of producing substantial short term equity capital into the venture. The search, for what Cholmondley felicitously termed a "godfather", soon came up with Billingham Construction Division Pty Ltd ("Billingham") and negotiations began on the prospective terms of the involvement of the three groups. No useful purpose is served in setting out the complex and convoluted pas de deux between Billingham and Mr Wade. Suffice it to say that I find that it was quite clear from the very start of negotiations and remained so at all subsequent times that Billinghams' interest in the venture was on an "in out" basis, that is, its aim was to take the profit and run. I find as a fact that Billingham were professional builders and thus not concerned with the issue whether or not such a profit would constitute assessable income in its hands. Nor did Billingham ever make the slightest attempt to conceal its attitude from its joint venturers and it would be the height of naivete to reach any other conclusion. Mr Wade's attempt to persuade me that his group's intention to retain the building on a long term basis was shared by Aguecheek, the local manager of Billingham ("he felt (this) would coincide with Billingham's intention should they join us") defies credibility and is not borne out by the contemporaneous correspondence between Wade and the principals of Billingham in Melbourne. Suffice it to say that the negotiations between Mr Wade on behalf of his and the Cholmondley groups were finally concluded and reduced to writing, as evidenced by a deed dated 6 November 1979. This deed was made between Marshall Pty Ltd (the successor of Special Features Group) on the one hand and Billingham, the Wade group and the Cholmondley group on the other. Paragraph 6 of this deed recites:

"The estimated cost of carrying out and completing the project is ONE MILLION FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY THOUSAND DOLLARS ($1,450,000) and subject as hereinafter provided, Wade, Cholmondley and Billingham have agreed to provide all monies necessary for the carrying out and completion of the project in the proportions which the number of units respectively held by them in the Trust bear to the total number of issued units in the Trust, that is to say -

Wade - 1/4

Cholmondley - 1/4

Billingham - 1/2. "

(The "Trust" referred to above relates to a complex set of agreements pursuant to which Marshall Pty Ltd agreed to act as trustee of the Marshall Unit Trust erected under a deed executed on 25 June 1979. Billingham subsequently became a unit holder in the said Marshall Unit Trust.) The management of the project was to be under the control of Marshall Pty Ltd and to comprise of one representative of each of the three joint venturers. Marshall Pty Ltd undertook to engage Billingham as the builder for an agreed amount ($940,000.00) who was to be responsible for all the funding of the development.

  1. In due course, the building was completed with the usual hitches and profitably let.
  2. It was at all times obvious to the three joint venturers or reasonably should have been - that their respective purposes were not identical. As already pointed out, Billingham had no tradition of long term investment and at no time made any bones about it. Its purpose was to put up the building and sell it on completion to the nearest buyer, hopefully at a profit. This purpose, for obvious reasons, did not suit the other joint venturers who sought to wrest some concession from Billingham on this point. After protracted negotiations, a "compromise" was attained and set out in a letter from Billingham to Wade dated 5 August 1979 in which the former states, inter alia, that "the intent would be to hold the project until profit on sale could be regarded as a capital profit as far as the joint venturers are concerned. Should Billingham for profit or liquidity reasons desire earlier realisation, the joint venturers undertook to acquire at valuation either directly or by nominence (sic) the Billingham 50%." I am not convinced that this "concession" was worth reducing to writing. It seems to me that an intention to acquire property until the profit on sale is a "capital profit" is not a complete answer to the first limb of sec 26(a). Be that as it may, this is not relevant to this application since I have not been persuaded that this was Cholmondley/Roe's main or dominant purpose at the time of acquisition. It was submitted on behalf of the applicant that, whatever differences of purpose may have been in the respective minds of the joint venturers, this was, somehow, resolved by the incorporation of a "deadlock" provision. This clause is to be found in an earlier deed dated 12 October 1979 which provides that in the event of a deadlock existing between unit holders, any unit holder may serve a notice on all remaining unit holders requesting them to resolve the deadlock and, if that should fail, "require the initial unit holder to buy the units held by (the other unit holders) at the price stated in the deadlock notice". This provision was inserted at the instigation of the Wade/Cholmondley group. The explanation for it was not hard to seek. It was offered succinctly by O'Shea, the (then) representative of Billingham who was questioned about this provision:

"Did Billingham ever give any commitment to Mr Cholmondley or Mr Wade or anyone else connected with this project that it would hold the property on a long term basis, or hold its interest in the property on a long term basis once the building was completed? --- I think that the position was that they would hold it if they could but they wanted the flexibility to sell if they had to.

And that flexibility was encapsulated in a provision which subsequently found its way into an agreemenmt which the parties signed? --- Yes, and I think the trust deed had to be altered to record that."

Pausing here, it is apposite to note that, having carefully observed the principal actors in the witness box, I am satisfied that Cholmondley, aware of the Bing Project and its obvious success, had as his dominant purpose, the intention of entering into a similar venture and succeeded in pursuading Mr Roe (and his other fellow directors) to join him in this venture. This was amply corroborated by Mr Roe who, I find, shared the same intention. Furthermore, the various contemporaneous feasibility studies amply support the expectation that such a project would achieve its hoped for objective. It was convincingly put by Cholmondley in evidence-in-chief, when he was asked what he discussed with his fellow directors after his preliminary discussions with Wade. This is what he said:

"I discussed with them the fact that, as an (architecture practice) we were not building up any assets and that if we could enter into the development of a building, with the use of our own expertise, and hold that building, we could help to stabilise the firm in a profession which is notoriously unstable. They understood the principles that I was talking about and I used, as an example, the situation which I understood to have occurred in respect of Berne House, wherein Rob Bing, another architect, entered into a similar arrangement to develop a building with John Westminster and Paul Wade.

Are you aware whether they occupied that building? --- Yes. I am aware that they did occupy part of the building.

Was that a factor in your consideration? --- It was something we always were conscious of. At that time we were occupying space in the old Philp Building. We were aware, from discussions with Philp, that in the not too distant future they would sell and when it was going to be sold it would, inevitably, be demolished and we would be looking for premises. So I was conscious of the fact that we might be a tenant within the building (ie 33 Straw Street) but it would depend on circumstances. ... I did a number of feasibilities not only on the project which is outlined here but a number of alternatives and those alternatives arose from the fact that the property on which the building now sits was, in fact, held on several titles when we purchased it; so some of the options that were available in terms of how to develop it were related to which pieces of land were used in what form. I did five or six alternative studies.

In terms of the feasibility, from the point of view of Mainwaring arid Cholmondley personnel contributing capital which they could afford to contribute and seeking a return on that capital in the long term, what sort of examination did you do? Well, the studies all revolved around two aspects: one was to ensure that the cumulative cash flow did not get into the negative area or, if it did, only minimal and that it got into the positive side as soon as possible. The second major point of consideration in those studies was to ascertain the likely valuation of the property so that one could assess the mortgage raising ability. ... We looked at two principal alternatives; one was to inject a substantial amount of capital ourselves and seek mortgage funds against the capitalised value of the project and the other alternative was to seek a joint venturer - a partner, a godfather - who had access to greater funds and was willing . to put them in and join with us. We chose the latter."

  1. Nothing in any of the evidence called by either side did anything to throw doubt on the above assertions. I am therefore satisfied, as previously noted, that Mr Cholmondley (and therefore Mr Roe) did not have as their main or dominant purpose the intention to resell the land at a profit. Whether such a finding concludes the matter in favour of the applicant will be dealt with when I turn to the application of sec 25(1) and the second limb of sec 26(a).
  2. Much of the evidence called by both sides was, in view of my conclusion, ultimately irrelevant to the outcome of this case. However, in deference to counsel, I will deal briefly with some of the evidence which related to the sale and attempted sale of the project at various stages. This evidence was, no doubt, called in an attempt to rebut the assertion that the project was undertaken as a long term investment. Since the building was sold shortly after completion and, indeed, was on the market well before that time, the respondent had every reason to view such an assertion with grave suspicion, the more so since Mr Roe (as well as all his fellow directors) per medium of various interposed trusts, sought artfully to conceal the venture in their relevant tax returns. It is, of course, trite to observe that a tribunal must make a wide sweep and close scrutiny of the activities of a taxpayer who claims that he bought property for long-term investment and then proceeds to sell it in unseemly haste just after sec 26AAA would have "caught" the profit, whatever his intention.

10. Turning to the various offers and counter-offers made during the construction of the building by and to Apex Insurance, I am satisfied, after hearing the various witnesses called by both sides, that these negotiations were undertaken, initially by agents acting without authority and subsequently with the active, and indeed enthusiastic, encouragement of Mr Wade. I am also satisfied that Mr Cholmondley was, in the main, kept informed of these various manoeuvres and that his professed astonishment and anger at the "audacity" of these estate agents - whom he blames for all his subsequent tax problems relating to this project - seemed a trifle overdone. I totally reject the attempt to blame "pressure to sell", allegedly coming from O'Shea, for the protracted negotiations and the various counter- offers. It was put that O'Shea had ulterior motives because of some perceived connection with Apex Insurance. I find this suggestion has no substance. I have no doubt that O'Shea, on behalf of Billingham, was keen to cash in on a profit that was substantially higher than anticipated. I also have no doubt that the counter-offer, made on behalf of the joint venturers to sell the project for $2 million would have been celebrated with champagne by all three of them if Apex Insurance had been a willing purchaser at that price. It seems that all the applicant's witnesses assumed that this concession would be, somehow, fatal to the outcome of this application. It is not. Provided that the intention for acquiring property does not have, as its predominant purpose, its resale at a profit, an irresistible temptation to take a quick and substantial gain when it comes "out of the blue" does not become assessable income pursuant to sec 26(a). It is, of course, legitimate to test the original intention against subsequent events, but, having found, as I have, that Cholmondley/Roe had a genuine investment purpose at the time of acquisition, the negotiations between the group and Apex Insurance have not persuaded me to resile from that finding. The ultimate sale was likewise - and justifiably - tested with great thoroughness. Here, I find the explanation offered by Cholmondley not quite so persuasive. Thus he was asked:

"Did any doubt (as to the viability of the project) arise shortly thereafter? --- Yes, they did. As the project proceeded towards completion which was in October, I think, the leasing proved to be less responsive than we thought and we had a couple of government decisions with reference to the Water Supply Building I think, and a couple of others which softened the market substantially and quite unpredictably in West Penrith. So we got less easy as time went on. That is on the income side; and on what it was actually costing us to hold the property, the interest rates were going up. Interest rates are not something that I am super involved with, but the feedback was that it was going to get worse rather than better. So we had income coming down and costs going up. But personally as far as I was concerned, equally importantly were some trends that were occurring in our own business which were unfortunate.

What were those trends? --- Well the trends were that the market was going off the boil and our cash flow out of our business was going down, and this reached a sort of a - it knocked off my enthusiasm and replaced it with a less enthusiastic position about life in general; which I might say was exacerbated by the fact that I was foolishly trying to build my own house at that time. But nonetheless that was significant.

The market was off the boil. How was Mainwaring & Cholmondley's business progressing at that time? --- In September of 1980, when we thought that we had the Imperial Hotel project completely tucked under our wing we were advised that due to our own errors that was not going to be the case; and we in fact lost the project. That represented about two and a half years years income to us which sort of wandered out the door, very quickly, So that was a huge blow.

But had much work been done in relation to getting that contract? --- About two man years. A hell of a lot of work, yes.

What effect did that have upon your thinking, or the thinking of yourself and your fellow members of Mainwaring & Cholmondley? --- I think it had most effect on myself because the way in which we lost this contract was bad business on our part; and it was because the firm at that stage had its priorities in a different way in which we now have them; and we followed what I considered to be old fashioned priorities, and I felt as though I was bashing my head against the wall. As a consequence of this particular exercise, not only did we have financial pressures but we had a major trauma in the office which resulted in me actually resigning and then being reinstated and one of the other guys resigning. So things were less than comfortable."

The evidence did not support a significant increase in interest rates, and the letting of the building appeared highly satisfactory. In any event the fact that the Cholmondley group held a mere 25% interest in the project, spread over all of the directors of that group, would hardly make the holding costs a significant factor to be seriously taken into account in the decision to sell. I am satisfied on the evidence that the real answer for the sale, is to be found in the fact that the negotiated sale price was so substantially in excess of the projected value of the completed building which the parties had used in their feasibility studies, that the temptation to sell proved to be commercially irresistible, the more so since the magic 12 months period had by then expired. However, this is not necessarily fatal to the outcome of this application. It would have been refreshing however, if it had been frankly conceded.

  1. I do not propose to deal with the evidence of the other bit players. Having satisfied myself as to the state of mind of the various principal actors, no useful purpose would be served in going over the evidence of the chorus which was called to test the intention of Cholmondley and Mr Wade in 1979. In my view, the collective evidence of the various estate agents has done nothing to detract from the finding I have made with respect to that intention.
  2. I now turn to the law. Firstly, it was put to me by Mr 0'Sullivan of counsel for the Commissioner that I should treat this profit as income in accordance with the ordinary usages and concepts of mankind, and assessable pursuant to sec 25(1). I do not agree. This transaction was a "one off" as far as this applicant was concerned. Furthermore, it is not a trading transaction of a kind commonly associated with the carrying on of the business of architecture. It was, as I find, merely the realisation of an investment; it certainly lacks any of the characteristics of the carrying on of a business 0f land development of the kind considered in FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd 82 ATC 4031. I have therefore concluded that the profit is a capital profit not assessable pursuant to sec 25(1). Whilst the share of the profit in the hands of Billingham is undoubtedly assessable income; it may even be assessable income in the hands of Wade (as to which I express no opinion), it is not income in accordance with the ordinary concepts in the hands of Roe. I have no conceptual difficulties with three parties jointly acquiring one and the same property, each with a different purpose.

13. The first limb of sec 26(a) is quickly disposed of. I indicated to Mr Fletcher, the applicant's solicitor, that he need not trouble himself with addressing me on this aspect. Having found that the subject property was not acquired by Cholmondley/Roe for the purpose of profit-making by sale, it follows that the so-called first limb of that section has no application. It was at one stage forceably urged upon me by Mr 0'Sullivan that the reality was that Billingham, with its 50% equity and with a clear profit-making purpose, when added to the interest of Wade, who for present purposes may be described as having "two bob each way", left the Cholmondley group with no means of giving effect to their intention of holding the project as a long-term investment since a Billingham/Wade combination could compel a sale at any time. Mr 0'Sullivan put it succinctly in his address:

"In the light of those objective factors, it is, in my submission, not enough just to say: well, Mr Roe meant to keep his interest so that must have been his intention. He is fixed with the intention, with the result, which he must have known in my submission, flowed from the legal relationship into which he entered with other partners who held controlling interests in the venture should they all 'gang up' against him."

Plausible as the argument appears, it is nevertheless, in my respectful view, flawed. The operative date for purposes of the first limb of sec 26(a) is the date on which the Wade/Cholmondley groups acquired the subject land; viz June 25, 1979. At that time, there had been no concluded agreement with Billingham, albeit preliminary negotiations had commenced. For what it is worth the Wade/Cholmondley group had held an option over the subject land since April. In any event I am not prepared to make the assumption for present purposes that the Wade group had a sec 26(a) intention at any relevant time. It is worth recalling that it had engaged in a similar venture with Bing and Associates in 1977 and that the building was retained by them until 1984.

14. Mr 0'Sullivan's argument assumes more substance when transcribed to the second limb of section 26(a). Here, so the argument runs, even assuming that Cholmondley had no intention to resell at a profit at the time of acquisition, it became a profit-making undertaking or scheme by the time the land was committed to the erection of an office block - and that occurred well after Billingham joined the venture. It was submitted that the profit thus became assessable income even though it would not ordinarily be regarded as such. Although the High Court decision in Whitfords Beach (supra) was not relied on in argument, it seems that it comes closest in supporting Mr 0'Sullivan's submission. The facts in that case were as follows: The taxpayer company acquired some 16 hundred acres of land adjacent to Whitford Beach, 24 km north of Perth in 1954. The land was acquired to give shareholders of the company access to beach front shacks and not for profit making by sale or for business purposes. In December 1967 (by which time the market value of the land had increased), all of the shares in the company were acquired by three other companies with the purpose of gaining control over the land and with the intention that the taxpayer company would cause the land to be subdivided, developed and sold at a profit. New Articles of Association were adopted by the taxpayer company to permit it to achieve this purpose. Two of the prospective companies were appointed by the taxpayer company as managers to carry out the work, including the procuring of the necessary rezoning of the land. The project was carried through on a massive scale, involving provision of roads and parklands. Eventually, substantial profits from the sale of these allotments were received by the taxpayer company, which was assessed for income tax in the years 1972, 1973, 1974 and 1975. Whilst the whole Court was unanimous in the result, namely that the profits were assessable income, there was little unanimity in their Honours' reasoning. Thus Gibbs CJ, Mason and Wilson JJ concluded that after December 1967, the taxpayer had become a company which existed for the sole purpose of carrying out the operation on which its new shareholders had decided when they acquired their shares. In the light of these circumstances, the development, subdivision and sale were therefore more than the mere realisation of an existing asset (such as was found, for example, in the Scottish Australian Mining Co case ((1950) [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188) and amounted to the carrying on of the business of land development. In the result, the profits constituted income within ordinary concepts and assessable under sec 25(1). Wilson J took the view that on such a finding, no concluded decision on the application of sec. 26(a) was required. Gibbs CJ and Mason J, on the other hand, concluded that sec 26(a) would only operate where sec 25(1) does not, (a view which sits uneasily with the majority decision of the Privy Council in McClelland v FC of T 70 ATC 4115). Murphy J concluded that the profit constituted assessable income pursuant to the second limb of sec 26(a), viz that what was done constituted a profit-making undertaking or scheme. The question nevertheless arises: Having concluded that what Roe did in this case did not constitute the carrying on of a business for profit, so as to "catch" the gain pursuant to sec 25(1), is it nevertheless a "profit-making undertaking or scheme" of the kind encompassed by the second limb of of sec 26(a)?

15. I do not propose to give a dissertation on the relationship between the two limbs of sec 26(a) and sec 25(1) in a case where the point was not argued. I merely content myself with a citation from Gibbs J (as he then was) in XCO Pty Ltd v FC of T 71 ATC 4152 where, at p4155 his Honour stated:

"The second limb of sec 26(a), unlike the first limb of that paragraph, does not refer in express terms to purpose but, in my opinion, a scheme is not a 'profit-making scheme' simply because it yields a profit when none was intended: in the ordinary sense of the words a 'profit-making scheme' is a plan devised in order to obtain a profit, and a scheme only- answers that description if the taxpayer carries it out with the purpose of making a profit."

I find that that statement of the law has withstood the test of time; it certainly survived the assault on the Scottish Australian Mining Company case ((1950) [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188) mounted by the Commissioner in Whitfords Beach (supra).

16. Applying the above statement to the West Penrith Project and the state of mind of Cholmondley/Roe, I must conclude that it does not answer the description of a "profit- making scheme" within the meaning of the second limb of sec 26(a). I have therefore concluded that the instant profit is not only a capital profit, but one not caught by either limb of sec 26(a).

17. In the result, no concluded opinion on the issue of penalty is required. However, in view of Mr Fletcher's bold submission that there had, in the instant case, been a full and true disclosure, I feel constrained to point out that the statutory requirement imposed on taxpayers to make a "full and true disclosure" is not satisfied by lodging a series of haystacks, one of which contains a needle, and then submitting that because one of the haystacks had a magnet attached to one of its schedules, the Commissioner had ample opportunity of finding the needle. The Commissioner cannot be expected to play "blind man's bluff" - with or without a magnet; of North Coast Grazing Pty Ltd v FC of T, 86 ATC 4942.

18. The decision under review is set aside.
