REASONS FOR DECISION

27 November 1987 Deputy President RK Todd

  1. The applicant, who represented himself at the hearing of these references, is a public servant with legal qualifications. In the three years of income before the Tribunal he had a variety of claims for deductions disallowed upon objection.
  2. In the year of income ended 30 June 1983 ("fiscal 1983") the Commissioner disallowed a claim of $826 for the preparation of research papers, $90 of rent relating to a home office and $410 of entertainment expenses. In fiscal 1984, the Commissioner disallowed claims of $4,212 incurred in attending conferences, $52 incurred as a result of being a judge for a moot competition, and $540 entertainment expenses. Claims of $2,394 relating to attendance at a conference, $55 in relation to the moot, and $680 entertainment expenses were disallowed in fiscal 1985. Quantum is in dispute only in relation to the amounts claimed as entertainment expenses.
  3. A preliminary issue was raised by the applicant at the hearing. The matter involved an amount of $2,184 which was apparently credited twice, instead of once, to the applicant and which the Commissioner had later sought to recover by withholding refunds due to the applicant in a later year. I find that the Tribunal is without power to review this matter and, as it has been fully dealt with by the Commonwealth Ombudsman, I intend to take the matter no further.

4. Evidence was given by the applicant and by his current work supervisor. The supervisor explained that the applicant had been interviewed for his current position in late 1981 and had been offered the position in April 1982, but, due to difficulties in obtaining a security clearance, was not approved to transfer to the position until September 1983. In his evidence the applicant explained that he was overseas at a conference in September 1983, and that while he was on paid leave (until 20 October 1987) he was considered by the Public Service to be an officer of his previous department. The importance of this is that it was conceded by the applicant that the expenses were not to "any great extent" connected with his employment with the former department.

Research Papers;

  1. Three papers and a book were prepared by the applicant in fiscal 1983, giving rise to the claim of $826. Of this amount, $97 related to the preparation of the book. In relation to the book, the connections with the production of income were submitted by the applicant to be, first, the expectation of future royalties, and secondly "enhancement of my skills basically, as an international lawyer, which would come in handy once I got to [his current department]". In relation to expenses relating to the other papers only the second connection was put forward by the applicant.
  2. The difficulty for the applicant in establishing the second contention is that the expenses were incurred prior to his commencing work at his current department.
  3. Miss Taylor, who represented the Commissioner, relied on Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd v FC of T [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 and the decision of Board of Review No 3 in Case 143 (1959) 8 CTBR (NS) 796 to support the submission that "to be deductible under the first limb of sub-section 51(1), expenditure must have been incurred in doing the work for which the taxpayer is paid in that year of income". I am unable to agree with this submission.

8. In Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Dixon J (as he then was) stated:

"In the present case, the actual expenditure was met in the current year. But it was completely dissociated from the gaining or producing of the assessable income of that year. ... What is important is the entire lack of connection between the assessable income and the expenditure. ... It was a payment independent of the production of the income, not an expenditure incurred in the course of its production."

9. A case in which there were problems similar to those before the Tribunal here is Evans v FC of T 86 ATC 4901. In that matter, the taxpayer claimed a connection between expenses of preparing his thesis and the income from a position he obtained after completing the thesis. In a judgment which contains a clear analysis of the question whether, for an expense to be deductible, the income must be produced in the same year as the expense is incurred, Connolly J stated at 4903:

"At first glance it might be thought that the expression 'the assessable income' must refer to the income of the year of tax. However, the course of judicial decision is to the contrary. Thus in FC of T v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 at p68 Dixon CJ, after referring to the argument that the presence of the definite article meant that one looks only at an intent or purpose of gaining or producing the assessable income of a current year, continued:

'It may be remarked that the argument based on the word "the" cannot be regarded as unaffected by the use of the word "in". For it is impossible to suppose that an expenditure directed to gaining future income cannot be allowed as a deduction unless its productive effect within the current year is seen or expected. If, therefore, the word "the" actually was read as limiting the expression that follows to assessable income of the current year, the consequence must surely be that the word "in" is to be read as importing no element whatever of purpose or motive and meaning no more than "in the course of" in a very general sense. The better view, however, is that s.51 as now drawn does not in either limb require a rigid restriction to the gaining or production of assessable income of the current year.'

Again, in FC of T v Smith 81 ATC 4114; [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 CLR 578, the majority (Gibbs CJ, Stephen, Mason and Wilson JJ) at ATC p4117; CLR pp 585-586 said:

'The section does not require that the purpose of the expenditure shall be the gaining of the income of that year, so long as it was made in the given year and is incidental and relevant to the operations or activities regularly carried on for the production of income. What is incidental and relevant in the sense mentioned falls to be determined not by reference to the certainty or likelihood of the outgoing resulting in the generation of income but to its nature and character and generally to its connection with the operations which more directly gain or produce the assessable income.'"

• • •

"Accepting as I do that a deduction may be allowable under sec 51(1) although the income to which it is intended to lead may not be expected to be derived until a later year of tax, the appellant is nevertheless faced with the difficulty that the section requires that the outgoings be incurred not merely with a view to the obtaining of income in the future, but 'in gaining or producing' assessable income."

The applicant's claim that the deductions claimed which relate to research papers were incurred in producing his assessable income at his current department is confronted by the same difficulty and consequently cannot be upheld.

10. The same applies to the claim for expenses relating to the book. In that instance however royalties were received and the claim is also put on the footing that the expenditure was in fact productive of assessable income. This claim cannot however succeed however as the expenditure was being used to create a profit-yielding subject, a capital item from which income was then derived. It was therefore of a capital nature. To claim these expenses the applicant would have to rely on Division 10B of the ITAA, although the deduction would have to be spread over the life of the copyright. The claimed $469 is therefore not an allowable deduction under s51(1).

The Fiscal 1983 Conferences

11. The applicant attended these conferences immediately prior to commencing employment with his current department. He claimed that it was incurred in the production of income from his job with that department. This claim, however, raises the same difficulties as the previous claims dealt with above as the expenses were incurred at a time prior to the applicant taking up his appointment in the position in respect of which he claimed that the expenses were incurred. For the same reasons as given in relation to the research papers, the claim of $4,212 in respect of the first conference must be disallowed.

Rent

  1. A deduction was claimed for twenty per cent of the rent of the applicant's residence, due to use of part of the home as a study for preparing the applicant's research papers and for his public service work. There have been many cases involving the deductibility of rent due to use of part of the home as an office, and in only quite limited cases, such as Swinford v FC of T 84 ATC 4803, and Case U87 87 ATC 500, is it now likely to be allowable.
  2. The facts before the Tribunal in this case fall well short of what is required for deductibility. The applicant during his working day had an office provided by the public service, and he also made use at other times of university libraries for study purposes. The calculation of twenty per cent was justified by the applicant as being "a reasonable amount of proportional rent that I could allocate for that purpose, considering that I used the house mainly to live in and not to work in". This statement encapsulates the difficulty which the applicant has in establishing his claim. In both Handley v FC of T 81 ATC 4,165 and FC of T v Forsyth 81 ATC 4,157 the High Court described the predominantly private and domestic nature of expenses that relate to a whole house.

14. I find that the claimed proportion of rent expended is not an allowable deduction.

Moots

15. These claims arose from the applicant's participation as a judge at moots in fiscal 1984 and fiscal 1985. Evidence was given, both by the applicant and his supervisor, that the second department "actively encouraged" appropriate officers to assist with the moots and that the applicant would not have needed to have taken leave to attend the moot.

16. The moots were conducted over three days, including a Saturday. The applicant received no remuneration for his attendance.

  1. It is clear that attending the moots can be characterised as arising directly out of his employment and that, prima facie, expenses incurred in attending the moot can be said to be "incurred in the production of assessable income".
  2. The difficulty that arises in this case is that the claim was put in the following, quite general, terms in the applicant's income tax returns:

"... Moot ... (Attended with duty leave)

Lunches, dinners, travelling and other expenses".

19. Miss Taylor for the Commission sought clarification of the expenses from the applicant during her questioning of the applicant in the following excerpt from the transcript:

"And on the claim made it has been described as lunches, dinners, travel. Could you just give the Tribunal a break-up or an explanation of what basis this claim is made on?------ The lunches account for the fact that once you attended a session in the morning you would normally have lunch at the university itself. The dinner is a formal dinner which normally follows the final moot or there could have been - I cannot remember whether it actually did happen or not, but if a moot took place at night it is quite likely that I had dinner at the university as well. The travelling was travelling between work and the university.

Did the travelling include any claims for the Saturday travelling?----- Yes.

And that would have been from your home to the moots?----- Yes.

Is the claim based on an estimate, a recollection of the cost of meals and travel or - - -?-----It is an estimate rather than a detailed accounting."

20. In many cases, most notably Lunney v FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478, travel from home to work has been found to be a private expense as has the cost of lunch, a question which was examined by Mr PM Roach (Senior Member) in Case U148 87 ATC 868. Combined with the fact that the total figure is merely an estimate, the inclusion of these clearly private expenses leaves the Tribunal in the position of being asked, without any evidence before it, to evolve for itself the quantum of an amount allowable as a deduction. As Dr Gerber, then a Member of Taxation Board of Review No.3, stated in Case K54 78 ATC 523 at 532:

"It goes without saying that it is not the function of a Board of Review to 'third guess' and supply its own calculations in the absence of any satisfactory evidence on which to base such calculations."

21. When Dr Gerber's statement and the facts of this claim are considered in the light of s.190 of the ITAA which states:

"190. In proceedings under this Part on a review before the Tribunal or on an appeal to a Supreme Court -

(a) ...
  • (b) the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer"

it is clear that this onus has not been discharged and the claim must therefore fail.

Entertainment Expenses

22. The applicant was unable to provide anything but a general description of the incurring of the expenses to support this claim. Considering the onus imposed on the applicant by s190(b) of the ITAA and the comments of Dr. Gerber discussed above, I am unable to find for the applicant on these claims.

The Fiscal 1985 Conference

  1. In the 1985 fiscal year the applicant attended a conference in the USA. He was employed with his current department and the evidence of his supervisor was that, while the department encouraged officers to attend such conferences, budgetary constraints often meant that officers would have to do so at their own expense. The claims were made up of $405 being the total accommodation, living and incidental expenses while at the conference, and $1,989 being 60% of air fares. The applicant is in my opinion entitled to the whole of the accommodation and living expenses, and to $1,105 being one third of the air fares.
  2. The facts in this claim in my opinion fall within the principles stated in FC of T v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 in which Dixon CJ stated:

"For it is indeed important that officers and employees engaged at a salary in the exercise of a skilled profession should not be in a worse position in respect of the costs of better equipping or qualifying themselves in point of knowledge and skill than are those exercising the same profession as a calling remunerated in fees paid by clients or by the members of the public who, under whatever style, enlist their services."

Kitto J in agreement with Dixon CJ stated:

"I am of the same opinion, and even if the relevance of the expenditure to the respondent's prospects of promotion were to be put on one side I should consider that enough would remain in the facts of the case to entitle him to the deduction he has claimed. His assessable income consisted of or included a salary which was payable from time to time in virtue of his holding the office of a senior design architect in the Public Works Department of Western Australia. It is, I think, a correct application of the terminology of s51 to say that he was engaged in 'gaining' that salary whenever and so long as he acted in the fulfilment of his office; for the salary payable was his remuneration for everything comprised in or incidental to his service.

• • •

Its professional status implied an obligation of progressive acquaintance with a living and developing art."

Windeyer J stated:

"Generally speaking, it seems to me, a taxpayer who gains income by the exercise of his skill in some profession or calling and who incurs expenses in maintaining or increasing his learning, knowledge, experience and ability in that profession or calling necessarily incurs those expenses in carrying on his profession or calling. Whether he be paid fees by different persons seeking his skilled services from time to time, or be paid a regular salary by one person employing him to exercise his skill, matters not in my opinion. Moreover, it would surely be wrong to assume that the Crown is so indifferent to the professional attainments of those whom it employs that their rights and prospects in its service are not affected by the true measure of those attainments."

  1. Reference was made by the Commissioner's representative to FC of T v Klan 85 ATC 4060 and to Case S37 85 ATC 320 at 321.
  2. As to Klan's Case, the facts were indeed very different. The claimed expenditure, as pointed out by Ormiston J at 4064, was incurred after Mr. Klan had resigned from his post at the Southport School: "If they were immediately incidental and relevant to the earning of any income, it was the earning of his somewhat delayed stipend at the Rydal School in Wales". At 4067 Ormiston J said: "This appeal is quite different from Finn's Case ... where an architect travelled overseas to increase his professional knowledge during his long service and recreational leave and returned thereafter to the same position". In my respectful opinion neither the reasoning of Ormiston J, nor the actual decision in Klan's Case detracted one whit from Finn's Case.
  3. I am not in agreement with what was said in Case S37. I do not think it appropriate for any administrative tribunal to speak of what Dixon CJ said in any case as a "pious hope", least of all what he said in Finn's Case. That case was described in Case S37 at 321 as a "barnacle encrusted wreck, sunk by the many judicial glosses since Finn and the near impossible task of attempting to reconcile that case with Hatchett". I am unaware how it is that Finn's Case, a major decision of the High Court, comprised of the Justices from whose judgments the above passages are cited, can be described as a wreck. The problem in my opinion, with the greatest respect, is with the barnacles and not with the alleged wreck, and it is high time that the ship was raised and refloated.
  4. Within the principles laid down in Finn's Case, the conference attended by the applicant was, I find, incidental and relevant to his employment and the expenses incurred in attending it are therefore deductible.
  5. An apportionment of the total expenses of the trip must however be made, as recreation leave was taken during the trip which is accepted by the applicant as having constituted a private purpose. In his return, the applicant claimed 60 per cent of the air fares plus his expenses while at the conference.

30. Of his 36 days overseas, 9 were spent by the applicant either at, or travelling to or from, the conference. I find that I am in a similar position to that of Walsh J in Smith v FC of T 72 ATC 4110 in which he stated at 4121:

"The evidence does not make it possible to determine by any precise calculation what was the extent to which the outgoings were so incurred. But I think that the evidence enables me to make an estimate of the amount which may be reasonably allowed."

  1. No doubt a relatively small diversion to recreational pursuits need not always lead to any reduction at all in a claim to deduct fares and associated expenses expended for the purpose of travelling to a work-related conference. The full amount has to be expended in order to get to the conference. If the proportion of the time devoted to the recreational activities as compared with' the time devoted to work-related activities is great, it may be that the answer will sometimes lie in the need to disallow the claim altogether. But that would be a decision on the facts of the particular case, and no rule can be laid down. Here I consider that I should balance against the fact that the whole of the fares had to be expended in order to get to the conference the fact that when all is said and done the applicant was at the conference, or travelling in aid of his participation thereat, for but 9 out of 36 days. On this basis the proportion of air fares allowable should in my opinion be one-third, with the whole of the accommodation, living and incidental expenses while at the conference being allowed.
  2. The amount of the claim that is allowable under this heading is therefore $1,510.

Conclusion

33. For the reasons given above the objection decision of the Commissioner is varied in respect of fiscal 1985 by allowing a further deduction of $1,510 for expenses incurred in attending the conference. The objection decisions of the Commissioner are otherwise affirmed.
