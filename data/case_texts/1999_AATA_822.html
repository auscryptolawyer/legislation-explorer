DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 822

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No NT98/472 &

TAXATION DIVISION
) NT99/213

DECISION



Tribunal
Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President)


Date 3 November 1999

Place Sydney

Decision

  1. The objection decision in application NT98/472 is affirmed.
  2. The deemed objection decision in application NT99/213 is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the objection should be upheld and an amended assessment be issued accordingly.




(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President

CATCHWORDS

TAXATION – whether sign-on fee income or capital gain – whether paid to individual or corporate applicant – whether contract valid or void – whether income derived at exchange

SUPERANNUATION – whether sign-on fee an employer contribution – whether applicant corporate or individual – employer contribution

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 25(1), 160M(6), (6A), 226Y, 227Y(3)

Industrial Relations Act 1991 s 275

Taxation Administration Act 14ZYA(2),(3)



Kelly v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4283

Re Pickford and Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1998] AATA 707; 40 ATR 1078

Tilley and The Official Receiver in Bankruptcy [1960] HCA 86; (1960) 103 CLR 529

Parkside Leasing Limited v Smith [1985] BTC 63

Commissioner of Taxes v The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Ltd (Carden’s Case) [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108



REASONS FOR DECISION
Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President)


  1. This is an application to review two objection decisions. Both of them concern the treatment for taxation purposes of a sum of $50,000, paid in the year ended 30 June 1995. In fact, there were two discrete payments of $50,00. The applicant regards them commercially as the same sum and the respondent has come to the agreement to which I will later refer in its manner of treatment of the two objection decisions. The respondent contends that the first payment was taxable income of the applicant either as income under section 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act) or as a capital gain pursuant to the operation of subsections 160M(6) and (6A). Both parties agree that if the amount in question is not taxable in the hands of the individual applicant, it should properly be treated as an employer’s contribution to the corporate applicant which is a superannuation fund associated with the individual’s private company.
  2. At all relevant times, the individual applicant (the player) was a professional rugby league player contracted to a Sydney club (the Club). On advice from his accountant, he had formed a company (Consultants) to do promotion and marketing in the football field and an associated superannuation fund (the fund) to maximise provision for his retirement after a career that would normally be expected to be relatively short.
  3. After a training session at the club’s sportsground on the afternoon of 30 March 1995, the club’s coach asked the player to come to a meeting that night with Mr R at an office in Goldfields House, Sydney. It was not made clear to the player just what would be discussed at that meeting but he regarded the request to be an official one as it was made by the club’s coach. He drove to the meeting together with a handful of other players from the club, arriving at Goldfields House at about 8pm.
  4. There were 8 players present who were addressed by Mr R. His exact status was not known to the player but it was clear from what he said that he was a person associated with the proposals then being advanced by News Limited for a new international rugby league competition. The assembled players were told that the new competition would be called “Star League”, that it would involve elite players with teams throughout the world and that the games would be televised globally. After Mr R had outlined the proposal, a senior executive of News Limited arrived and told the assembled meeting about that company’s support for the “Star League” proposals. The meeting lasted well over an hour.
  5. The player’s evidence was that until he arrived at the meeting, he was not aware that he would be offered a contract with “Star League” or indeed that “Star League” even existed. He said that he was taken by surprise because he had not anticipated being asked actually to make a firm commitment within an hour or so of being told about the proposals. At the end of the meeting, Mr R told each of the assembled players that he wished to speak to them individually and to discuss terms on which they might sign with the new “Star League”. The player believed that the Club’s coach had already signed on with “Star League” and that the Club had become aligned with the “Star League” proposals. He thought that if the coach and Club had changed their allegiances to “Star League”, he really had no choice but to follow and sign any relevant agreement.
  6. During his individual meeting with Mr R he was asked what he earned at present. The two men then discussed a possible basis for remuneration if the player joined the “Star League” competition. The player asked for a 3-year package of $250,000 per year. Mr R countered with an offer of $225,000 per year plus an amount of $50,000 “as some form of loyalty incentive to encourage me to sign up”. The player agreed with this proposal.
  7. Mr R then produced a form of contract, which consisted of 3 pages of contractual clauses, a page for signatures and a schedule. The employer’s name shown in the schedule was that of a company which subsequently changed its name to the Club, the player’s present employer. The player’s name was already typed in. An amount of $50,000 was then written in opposite the item marked “sign on fee”. Against yearly payments for the calendar years 1997, 1998 and 1999, the figures of $225,000 were then written.
  8. The player signed his name twice, once in the signature provisions on page 4 and once “for identification” at the foot of the schedule on page 5. The form of contract was to come into possession of the player some 3 months later, when it was simply handed to him at the Club’s premises.
  9. At the time of signing, however, Mr R declined to give him a copy of the contract and said that he would get a copy in due course. Mr R said that his principals wished to keep the contracts confidential for the time being. The player asked Mr R how the amounts would be paid. He was then told that he would need to work out any packaging arrangements for the annual amounts directly with the Club. Thereupon he handed the player a cheque drawn by News Limited in his favour for $50,000 “to seal the deal”.
  10. The contract form which the player subsequently received, is reproduced in the section 37 documents at T10/100. It is drafted as an employment contract made between the company, then described by its former name as a shelf company but which is now known as the Club as employer, and the player as employee and Star League Pty Ltd. The contract provides for employment during the period stipulated in the schedule. Clause 2 deals with the “sign on fee” and provides that the Club “understands that News Limited has undertaken to pay the sign on fee to [the employee] in the amounts and on the dates set out in item 5 of the schedule”. As previously noted, the amount shown in item 5 of the schedule was a single sum of $50,000 represented by the cheque handed to the player on signing the agreement.
  11. The agreement form goes on to provide for yearly payments, confidentiality, method of variation and other supplementary material.
  12. Before he left the meeting, Mr R told the player not to discuss the arrangements with anyone, particularly any of the other players who were still waiting in another room for their interviews. He told the player that if there were any questions or issues that he needed to discuss, he should contact a nominated officer of News Limited who was to handle all these matters while Mr R was away from the office. The player eventually left the meeting and arrived home at a Sydney suburb after midnight.
  13. His evidence was that he was concerned that he had to sign the contract immediately without discussing it with his accountant or his wife. However, he regarded the document as being more in the nature of a heads of agreement, the details of which would be sorted out later. He said in his evidence that this had always been his experience in negotiating football contracts. In cross-examination, however, he agreed that he had previously signed two contracts with one Sydney club and another contract with his present club without any further negotiation. He also agreed that he was principally concerned with the term of the agreement (3 years) and the annual remuneration. He was prepared to allow the method of payment of the gross amount and the packaging arrangements to be worked out between his accountant the Club’s financial advisers.
  14. On the following day (or thereabouts) he telephoned his accountant to tell him that he had agreed to join the new “Star League” and asked him to advise how the proposed gross amount should be packaged. Apart from the annual sums, he told his accountant that he would like the $50,000 to go into his superannuation fund and asked him how that should be handled. The player had the cheque for $50,000, which he had kept in his wallet. He did not think that he should bank it until the details were better sorted out. The cheque was made payable to him personally.
  15. His accountant told him that only an employer could make a tax deductible superannuation contribution on his behalf. He advised the player that because News Limited was not his employer, it would be necessary to cancel the cheque and amend the terms of their agreement. This would require News Limited to provide his employer with the necessary funds to contribute to his superannuation fund.
  16. At this time he had three employers namely, the Club, his private company (Consultants) and a public company for which he did marketing and sales work.
  17. The same day he telephoned the nominated executive at News Limited to tell him that he wished to change the arrangements concerning the $50,000. He asked that the amount appearing in the schedule of the contract as a sign on fee should be changed to a superannuation contribution on his behalf. He confirmed the advice that he received from his accountant and it was arranged that News Limited was to pay the same amount to Consultants so that it could contribute to his superannuation fund.
  18. The nominated executive then couriered a News Limited cheque for $25,000 made out in favour of Consultants. On receipt of this, the player immediately telephoned the nominated executive and told him that the cheque should have been for $50,000. Accordingly, he then arranged for the courier to deliver a second cheque for $25,000 in favour of consultants. The player gave the original unbanked cheque for $50,00 to the same courier to take back to the nominated executive.
  19. The player later spoke to his accountant again to tell him about the two cheques he had received in favour of Consultants. The accountant then advised that the cheques should have, in fact, been made out directly to the Club because the amount was too high to be paid as a contribution by Consultants. Accordingly, the player then spoke with an officer of the Club who agreed that if the Club received $50,000, it would be happy to make an equivalent contribution to the superannuation fund. After some further discussion with the accountant it was agreed that in order to avoid the risk of further confusion, the two cheques should be banked into the account of Consultants and a cheque for $50,000 should be drawn by Consultants in favour of the Club. This was later done.
  20. Bank records show that the $50,000 was received on 5 April 1995, while the account was in overdraft. Receipt of this amount immediately put the account in credit. The statements show that further transactions took place in the account before the amount of $50,000 was paid out again on 18 April bringing the account again into overdraft. Between 5 and 18 April 1995, the amount in question was mixed with the general funds of Consultants. That fact alone would be sufficient to preclude any submission that Consultants merely held the monies on trust for the Club or to be dealt with otherwise at the player’s direction. It further emphasises, to my mind, that subsequent events were quite separate from the events of 30 March. The player arranged for Consultants to pay a corresponding amount. On receipt of Consultant’s bank transfer for $50,000, the Club drew a cheque for the same amount in favour of the superannuation fund and paid it to the fund. Prior to 30 June 1995 as a result of further negotiations, the Club paid an additional amount of $40,00 for the benefit of the player. This came about as a result of re-negotiations concerning the terms of the player’s employment, which was not finally documented until May 1997.
  21. It is the player’s case that on the night of 30 March 1995 when he accepted a News Limited cheque for $50,000 he was taken by surprise. In fact it was submitted on his behalf that the contract was signed under duress and was consequently voidable. Nevertheless, no steps were taken by the player to void the contract in any legal way. Some other players took proceedings in the Industrial Court to have their particular contracts declared void under section 275 of the Industrial Relations Act 1991. The judgement of Hill J, given on 21 December 1995 was tendered in evidence before me. Although His Honour’s judgement contains some general information concerning the “Star League” arrangements, no part of that judgement has any relevance in the characterisation of the sum of $50,000 presently under consideration. The facts found by His Honour I have treated as merely background to the events with which I am concerned.
  22. It is the player’s case (as I understand it) that the agreement form which he signed on the night of 30 March may be completely disregarded because the terms of his employment were subsequently re-negotiated, the final agreement taking effect some two years later. The invalidity of the original agreement, it was submitted, is evidenced by the fact that the player did not have an opportunity to read the document or to take advice on its contents. The player’s representative also placed some reliance on the fact that the document was not signed by any other party, that the player’s signature was not witnessed, and that News Limited was not a party to the agreement. It was submitted that the reference in the document to a “sign on fee” was not a condition of the contract but was merely a statement about a third party’s intention.
  23. In my view, the document which the player signed on the evening of 30 March 1995 cannot be ignored and indeed, except in one particular, has not been ignored by the player. The evidence shows that there was an intention to enter into a legal relationship. There was an offer involving the fundamental terms, which were accepted by the applicant. The fact that the document was not signed on behalf of the Club does not derogate from the existence of a contract because the employment continued as a matter of fact. The player continued to carry out his duties as an employee of the Club and the Club continued to treat him as an employee, notwithstanding any formal defects in the documentary evidence of their arrangement. This part performance clearly evidences a continuing contract between the two parties. The player was concerned principally with the proposed term and the gross remuneration. These fundamental terms were not varied. The only item re-negotiated was the way in which the signing on fee was to be paid. The applicant, as I have said, took no steps to have the contract declared void. In my view, the fact that there was a subsequent playing contract entered into in May 1997 has no bearing on the manner in which the carrying out of the 1995 arrangements should be treated for taxation purposes.
  24. The fact is that a cheque for $50,000 in favour of the player, a professional rugby league player, was handed to him on the night of 30 March 1995. It was expressed to be, and was understood by the player to be, a sign on fee intended to “seal the deal”. The fact that the drawer of the cheque was not the proposed actual employer does not change the character of the receipt in the hands of the recipient (Kelly v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 85 ATC 4283). The money was paid as a reward for services to be provided in the future (Re Pickford and Federal Commissioner of Taxation, [1998] AATA 707; 40 ATR 1078 and cases therein cited).
  25. The nature of the proposed relationship governs the way in which one should view the payment. The sign on fee was intended as a reward for signing the contract and to secure the applicant’s services as a player in the “Star League” competition. Immediately it was paid to him, the sum involved “came home”. It was in payment for the service rendered by him there and then by signing the contract and pledging his future to the “Star League” arrangements. Whether the contract was valid or voidable, whether or not the signing of such contract was in breach of his other obligations with the NSW Rugby League, whether or not it was susceptible of an application to have it declared void are all beside the point. The contract was not voided and the player received $50,000 simply for signing the form of contract.
  26. At that stage, the income was “derived” by the player for the purposes of subsection 25(1) of the Act. Receipt of a cheque is equivalent to the payment of cash, although it is subject to the condition subsequent that the original debt can be sued upon if the cheque is dishonoured (per Dixon J in Tilley and The Official Receiver in Bankruptcy[1960] HCA 86; , 103 CLR 529 at 532 and per Kitto J at 535). This case dealt with payment for goods, but the principles expressed in general terms by a Full Court of the High Court are binding on this Tribunal. The passages to which I have referred have often been quoted and have been relied upon as statements of general principle in many different contexts. In one English case to which the applicant’s representative referred there was some apparent persuasive authority that payment did not occur until after a cheque was cleared. He referred to the observations of Scott J in the Chancery Division in Parkside Leasing Limited v Smith [1985] BTC 63. Although respect is due to His Honour’s reasoning in that case, it seems to me not helpful in the present circumstances. It was dealing with a situation in which the fixing of the time of receipt of money was the principal concern. The case dealt with a concept quite different from the concept of derivation of income under the Australian Act.
  27. By accepting the initial cheque, the player was at liberty to bank it or apply it as he wished. It follows that the amount of $50,000 was derived because it had “come home to the taxpayer in a realised or immediately realisable form” during the relevant year of income (Commissioner of Taxes v the Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Ltd [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 at 155 (Carden’s Case)). It is not open to the applicant to postpone derivation by carrying the cheque around in his wallet for a number of days, nor it is possible to cancel the derivation of income by returning the cheque in exchange for some other cheques in favour of some other entity. At the time of signing the employment agreement form and at the time of receiving the first cheque, the applicant had derived income to the extent of the value of that cheque.
  28. In my view, it is therefore not necessary to explore whether the sign on fee was dealt with at the direction of the applicant within the terms of section 19 (thus deeming the applicant to have derived that amount) or to explore whether the sum in question is a capital gain pursuant to section 160M. In my view, the relevant sum was clearly income in accordance with ordinary concepts and was derived at the time the cheque was received.
  29. The objection decision in the player’s application was to disallow the Notice of Objection dated 27 July 1998 to the player’s amended assessment, which included the sign on fee in the applicant’s assessable income for the year ended 30 June 1995. For the reasons which I have given, that objection decision will be affirmed.
  30. In the application brought by the Fund (NT99/213) the question related to the Fund’s return for the year of income ended 30 June 1995. It had included the sum of $50,000 as employer contributions. The objection of 5 November 1998 claimed that the assessment should be reduced by the excision of this sum upon grounds set out in that notice. The respondent did not make a decision in relation to the objection after being given notice under section 14ZYA(2) of the Taxation Administration Act and, accordingly, by virtue of section 14ZYA(3) there was a deemed decision disallowing the objection. For the reasons I have given, it seems to me that the payment of $50,000 by Mr R through the medium of a News Limited cheque to the player and the payment by the Club to the fund of a similar amount some 3 weeks later could be regarded as two completely separate and discrete transactions. It could well be argued that the first sum was assessable income in the hands of the player and that the second sum was properly shown as an employer contribution. Nevertheless, the parties have agreed that if the player was unsuccessful in his application, then the Fund should be successful in relation to its objection. My formal decision will reflect this agreement.
  31. Submissions were made concerning the penalty of 25% culpability component which was imposed on the player. It was said that if he were unsuccessful then the amount of the penalty should be reduced because of his general cooperation. There is no suggestion that he was in any way obstructive. Nevertheless, I do not see any circumstances which would warrant the remission of penalty tax pursuant to sections 226Y or 227(3) of the Act. The applicant was seeking a tax advantage for himself. In my view, he failed. I see no reason why the normal consequences should not follow.

I certify that the 31 preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President).
