Introduction

1. This is an application under section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") for review of two decisions of a delegate of the respondent made on 15 April 1987:

(a) varying the decision taken by another delegate on 7 February 1984 to issue, pursuant to sub-section 184(2) of the 1976 Act, in respect of Mr Rayment a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") specifying the conditions "Recurrent Headaches Related to Nervous Tension", "History of Bronchitis" and "History of Cartilage Injury to Right Knee" by substituting for the first mentioned condition the condition "Recurrent Headaches"; and

(b) confirming the decision also taken by the other delegate on 7 February 1984 to be of the opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Rayment's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a condition or conditions specified in the BCC, or by a condition or conditions connected with such conditions.

2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") and numbered TI to T30, together with certain medical reports and other materials lodged at the -hearing, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant and his wife, Dr Davie, an orthopaedic surgeon, Dr Trigg, a general surgeon, Dr King, a neurologist, Professor Jones, a psychiatrist who is also qualified as a physician and has practised as a neurologist, Dr Hocking, Dr Cole and Dr Moore, all of whom are psychiatrists, and Dr Spencer, who was at one time the applicant's treating general practitioner. The applicant was represented by Mr Friend of counsel and the respondent by Mr Whithear of the Commissioner's office.

3. Mr Rayment, who was born in 1944, enlisted in the Army in 1961. While in the Army he qualified as a motor mechanic and worked in that capacity on Army vehicles of various kinds. He was discharged from the Army in 1973. On 13 March 1973 he commenced civilian employment with the Department of Defence. It appears that he applied to become a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") .

4. On or about 5 July 1973 Mr Rayment was examined by Dr Johnston, Commonwealth Medical Officer, as a prerequisite to a decision under section 5 of the 1922 Act as to whether his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of that Act or to the Provident Account.

5. Dr Johnston recommended that Mr Rayment be accepted for the Superannuation Fund. However, on 23 July 1973 he was accepted by a delegate of the Superannuation Board, pursuant to sub-section 5(1) of the 1922 Act, as a contributor to the Provident Account.

The relevant legislation

6. The 1922 Act provided for three different possible results, based on the medical examination of a person who was, or became, a permanent employee, of an application to become a contributor under that Act: acceptance as a member of the Superannuation Fund; acceptance as a member of the Provident Account, which provided significantly lower benefits; or outright rejection..

7. The Superannuation scheme established by the 1976 Act ("the scheme") was organised upon a different basis from that established by the 1922 Act. Every permanent employee was accepted into the scheme, but benefits were adjusted, on the basis of the medical examination, by the issuing of a BCC which related to the risk of the employee's being unable, by reason of a particular condition, to remain an employee until the retiring age. The inclusion in the scheme of employees like Mr Rayment, who were contributors to the previously existing Superannuation Fund or Provident Account, required the enactment of transitional provisions in the 1976 Act. The present matter arises under those provisions.

8. The relevant provisions of the 1922 Act, as in force at the relevant time, are contained in section 5, which reads as follows:

" 5(1) Except as otherwise provided by this Act, and notwithstanding anything contained in any other Act, a person who becomes an employee after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 shall not contribute to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account unless

(a) he has, before becoming an employee, or , within such period after becoming an employee as the Board allows, undergone a medical examination by a legal qualified medical practitioner approved by the Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Board; and

(c) the Board is satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III or to the Provident Account, as the case may be.

(2) Where -

(a) a person who becomes an employee after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 has, whether before or after becoming an employee, undergone a medical examination by a legally qualified medical practitioner approved by the Public Service Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Public Service Board; and

(c) the Public Service Board certifies, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account,

the Superannuation Board may, in its discretion, accept him as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account."

The definition of "employee" in sub-section 4(1) read, so far as relevant: "a person employed in a permanent capacity by the Commonwealth, who is by the terms of his employment required to give his whole time to the duties of his employment ...".

9. The relevant provisions of the 1976 Act are sub-sections 7(1), 66(2) and 184(2) and (7), which at the relevant time (as to which see paragraph II infra) read as follows so far as relevant:

"7 (1) A reference' in this Act to the retirement of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be read as a reference to his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to.perform his duties."

66 ( 2 ) ...where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled -

[to reduced benefits]

184 ( 2) ...where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was a contributor to the Provident Account -

(i) ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) at the time when the person so ceases to be an eligible employee ...the person's period of contributory service is less than 20 years and the person has not attained the person's maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person ...was caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the I: certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement...

...

(7) In this section -

(a) a reference to a contributor to the Fund shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the existing Fund under Part III of the superseded Act;

(b) a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act; and

(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall b e read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which at the time when the person became, or last became, a contributor to the Provident Account -

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund;"

10. Section 184 of the 1976 Act was amended by section 76 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No 2) 1986 ("the amending Act") with effect from 18 December 1986; i.e., before the making of the decision under review and lodging of this application for review. The text of section 184 as set out above incorporates those amendments so far as they relate to the portions of that section with which we are concerned.

11. Mr Whithear submitted, and the Tribunal accepts, that the effects of the amendments were not relevant to the issue before us. Accordingly, it is not necessary for us to pursue the question as to whether our decision is to be made under the legislation as amended, or under the legislation as it stood prior to the enactment of the amending Act, and we will proceed to consider the matter on the basis of the text as it stood at the time of making of the decision under review.

Mr Rayment's work history and retirement

12. Mr Rayment's initial position with the Department of Defence in March 1973 was as a storeman at Balcombe. In June 1974 he transferred to a position as a motor mechanic, also at Balcombe. In 1976 he transferred to the Army Apprentices ' School, still at Balcombe, as a maintenance fitter, and in 1978 he was given certain teaching responsibilities in addition to his other duties. The school left Balcombe in April 1983 and Mr Rayment was redeployed to HMAS Cerberus.

13. On 17 October 1983 a delegate of the Public Service Board certified, in pursuance of section 14 of the Commonwealth Employees (Redep1oyment and Retirement) Act 1979, that he was satisfied that Mr Rayment "should, for the reasons [sic] that he is, in consequence of physical or mental incapacity, incapable of performing his duties, be retired from his employment". On 10 November 1983, with effect from 6 October 1983, Mr Rayment was retired from the Australian Public Service by a delegate of the permanent head of the Department of Defence.

14. On 7 February 1984 a delegate of the respondent issued a BCC specifying recurrent headaches related to nervous tension, history of bronchitis, and history of cartilage injury to right knee. The basis of issue of the certificate was that the delegate formed the opinion, in terms of sub-paragraph 184(7)(c)(i) of the 1976 Act, that those were the conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board had not been satisfied that Mr Rayment's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

15. That delegate of the respondent formed the opinion, in terms of sub-paragraph 184(2)(c)(i) of the 1976 Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Rayment's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the BCC or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions. That being so, he decided on 7 February 1984 to apply sub-section 66(2) of the 1976 Act, with the result that Mr Rayment received a reduced pension benefit under the scheme.

16. Mr Rayment requested a reconsideration of those two decisions of the delegate, and the result of that reconsideration was the making, on 15 April 1987, of the decisions under review, which are set out in paragraph 1 supra. Insofar as the preceding paragraphs contain factual material, those facts are not in issue and we find accordingly.

The Issues

17. In effect, the matters which the Tribunal must determine are:

(a) what, if any condition or conditions should appear on the BCC: i.e. what was the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board, on 23 June 1973, was not satisfied that Mr Rayment's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the 1922 Act;

(b) what was the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Rayment's retirement on 10 November 1983; and

(c) did

(i) the condition or conditions described in (a) or any of them; or

(ii) a condition or conditions connected with them or any of them;

cause or substantially contribute to that incapacity.

18. Prior to the coming into operation of the amending Act, a further question would have arisen under sub-sub-paragraph 184(7)(c)(iii)(A) of the 1976 Act, namely whether, if the answer to question (c) was in the affirmative, the condition or conditions described in question (a) existed at the time when Mr Rayment became a contributor to the Provident Account. That provision was repealed by the amending Act. As Mr Whithear pointed out, a condition which did not exist could not, in any case, contribute to an incapacity or be connected with a condition contributing thereto. On the basis set out in paragraph 11 supra, we are, as we there said, proceeding to consider this matter on the basis of the legislation as it stood at the time of making of the decision under review.

Question (a) : 23 June 1973

19. The delegate of the Superannuation Board, in 1973, gave no reason for his decision. As the Tribunal said in Re Gunzburq and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 3466: decided on 7 May 1987):

"This, however, is not a novel situation. It has been faced by this Tribunal in other decisions."

It must be remembered that the role of the Tribunal in this context is not to review the decision of the Superannuation Board; but merely to ascertain what was the physical or mental condition (or conditions) by reason of which the decision was made.

20. In Re Bailey and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 352: decided on 24 December 1979: noted at (1979) 2 ALN 666) the Tribunal, presided over by Mr Hall, Senior Member, (as he then was) said:

"In a case such as the present, where there is no direct evidence to establish the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, it appears to us that the proper test to apply when considering the material before the Board when it reached its decision is -what condition or conditions must have influenced the Board to accept the person into the Provident Account rather than the Fund. Unless, on a fair consideration of that material, the Tribunal can feel satisfied that a particular physical or mental condition was, in fact, a reason which influenced the Board's decision, the Tribunal would not, in our view, be justified in finding as a fact that the condition should be taken as being specified in the benefit classification certificate which is deemed, by s 184(2) of the Act, to be in force in respect of the applicant."

In that case the Superannuation Board had accepted the recommendation of the Commonwealth Medical Officer who had examined the applicant, and the Tribunal said (not reported on this point) :

"Where in the medical report relating to a person who has applied for permanent appointment to the Public Service, the section at the end headed "Reasons for Recommendation and Remarks" clearly identifies a mental or physical condition or conditions which may lead to that person's early retirement and where the Superannuation Board or its delegate has accepted that recommendation without comment, it is proper, in our view, to accept that the Board has adopted the reasons given by the Commonwealth Medical Officer in support of his recommendation. This was the view which the Tribunal adopted implicitly, if not explicitly, in Harrison's Case [cited in paragraph 22 infra]."

However, in the present case, the recommendation of Dr Johnston that Mr Rayment be accepted to the Superannuation Fund was not accepted.

21. In Re Richardson and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 478: decided on 5 June 1981) the Tribunal, presided over by Mr Todd, Senior Member, (as he then was) said of sub-paragraph 184 (7) (c) (i) :

"This definition thus requires a reference not to a condition of the person that is merely proved to have existed and to have been a condition to which reference might properly have been made, but rather to a condition that has actually been taken into account by the Superannuation Board for the purposes of coming to a conclusion as to the health and physical fitness of the person."

22. We note that in Re Harrison and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 137: decided on 1 February 1979: noted at (1979) 2 ALN 353) the Tribunal, also presided over by Mr Todd, found that, for reasons related to the purposes of the provisions of the 1922 Act relating to medical examinations,

"a medical history of a condition, physical or mental, may be described as a "condition" , for the purposes of the statutory provision, even though the condition itself be not apparent at the time of the examination."

23. Dr Johnston, after the examination on or about 5 July 1973 referred to in paragraph 4 supra, gave the following reasons for his recommendation that Mr Rayment be placed in the Superannuation Fund:

"His main disability appears to have been headaches of uncertain aetiology. These have improved in the past year and he has had only one severe headache this year. They appear to have been related to nervous tension which should not arise in his proposed job as a storeman: this should be well within his capabilities.

Mild bronchitis and occasional displaced cartilage in knee unlikely to be disabling."

24. The form on which his report was prepared indicated that it was to be despatched to the Commonwealth Public Service Inspector ("CPSI"). It appears that this was done, and we so find, as the next document, chronologically, before the Tribunal is a memorandum from the CPSI to the Director, Health, dated 12 July 1973 headed "B.G. Rayment" and reading "The attached papers are referred for review of country med". The memorandum is signed "D. Hodgson". In the space provided for reply at the foot of the same page, there appears a handwritten recommendation addressed to the CPSI, which reads:

"Recommend acceptance for Provident Account recurrent headaches, history of bronchitis and cartilage injury to R. knee."

That recommendation is signed for the Director of Health and dated 19 July 1973. A note endorsed on the memorandum reads "OK Prov. DH. 23/7/73" and is countersigned on the same date with illegible initials.

25. A separate memorandum dated 23 July 1973 and signed "R.A.M. D.H. Delegate of Superannuation Board" records that Mr Rayment is accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account from the date of that memorandum or of taking up duty whichever is the later.

26. The recommendation of the Director of Health on 19 July 1983 was thus accepted by the delegate of the Superannuation Board on 23 July 1983. On that basis we are satisfied, and we find, that the conditions by reason of which that delegate was not satisfied that Mr Rayment's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund were the conditions listed in the recommendation of the Director of Health, namely, "recurrent headaches, history of bronchitis and cartilage injury to right knee".

27. Accordingly, those conditions are, by virtue of sub-paragraph 184(7)(c)(i) of the 1976 Act, to be specified on the BCC to issue in respect of Mr Rayment in accordance with paragraph 184(2)(c) of that Act should the Tribunal, standing as it does in the shoes of the Commissioner by virtue of section 43 of the AAT Act, be of the opinion defined in that paragraph.

28. It should be noted that the question of whether or not Mr Rayment could properly be described, in July 1973, as suffering from any of those conditions, is not relevant to the finding which we have made. It is clear from the authorities cited above (and see also Re Heqedues and Commissioner for Superannuation (1982) 4 ALN N139) and indeed from the terms of sub-paragraph 184(7)(c)(i) itself, that what must be ascertained in the present context is not what was the applicant's medical condition at the time, but what was, rightly or wrongly, in the mind of the delegate of the Superannuation Board.

Question (b) : 10 November 1983

29. The next question to determine in respect of Mr Rayment is, what was "the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement" on 10 November 1983. That retirement followed a medical examination on 19 April 1983 by Dr Johnston, who was asked to assess Mr Rayment's fitness for continued duty.

30. At the request of the Department of Defence, Dr Johnston referred Mr Rayment to Dr Moore for a psychiatric opinion. Dr Moore's report of 18 June 1983 to Dr Johnston reads in part:

"To look at this man in some detail, he is suffering from panic disorder. He has nine out of the twelve diagnostic criteria (four are required) and the appropriate frequency i.e. more than three episodes in three weeks.

The panic disorder is particularly associated with crowded public places, and the social consequences have been avoidance of -

(a) Shops

(b) Crowded public meetings.

Avoidance behaviour (b) is particularly important to Mr. Rayment as he is a local councillor.

There is a depressive component in this man's case, unsurprisingly in view of his life's circumstances, and for reasons which I will outline later, I don't believe it is particularly relevant at this point to define whether in fact it is a reactive or endogenous process.

Prior to the development of the panic attacks in 1983, this man had noted an increasing difficulty in coping with his trade specifically in coping with moving machinery due to difficulties which he had with co-ordination. He attributes this partly to anxiety and partly to interference by Serepax which he was using to control his anxiety."

31. Dr Johnston said in his report, under "brief history of the condition(s) including origins and the time first noted by contributor":

"Anxiety symptoms for many years. Recently worse. He is 'frightened of moving machinery, being afraid of being pulled into it'. He is too nervous to drive and has to be driven to and from work by his wife.

Migraine since age 18. Insomnia. Takes Serepax, Allegron. Pes cavus -Has to wear special boots which limit his activity. Recent attack of herpes zoster and strained back complaint."

He ticked the box opposite the sentence "I consider that the examinee. is unfit for continued employment and should be retired on grounds of invalidity". He found Mr Rayment's incapacity to be contributed to as to 90 per cent by anxiety state and as to 10 per cent by pes cavus (elevated arch of the foot).

32. It appears from the index to the materials before the Tribunal that certain sick leave records and supporting medical certificates among those materials may have formed part of the documentation which provided the basis for the decision to retire Mr Rayment on invalidity grounds. The sick leave records appear to have been rewritten from original records, photocopies of which were before the Tribunal. They dealt with a number of occasions in the period from 7 September 1980 to 11 February 1983, and the conditions specified were viral infection, bronchitis, torn ligaments left ankle, lacerated left hand, stomach disorder, migraine (twice), back muscles torn, back trouble, shingles (twice) and post shingles neuralgia. One of the medical certificates related to anxiety state and covered the period from 3 September to 3 December 1983. We are not able to form any concluded view as to whether that material was before either of the delegates who made the decisions set out in paragraph 13.

33. There does not appear to have been any other documentation relevant to the decision to retire Mr Rayment. The space for completion of a recommendation of the Department of Health-" -.on the medical report form on which Dr Johnston's report was prepared, was completed and stamped on 2 September 1983. The recommendation is difficult to decipher on the copy before the Tribunal, but appears to read "Invalidity retirement is justified". The certificate of the Public Service Board and the formal retirement document are described in paragraph 13 supra.

34. The Tribunal, presided over by Mr Todd, Senior Member, as he then was, said in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 411: decided on 30 September 1980: note only at (1980) 3 ALN Nl) at paragraph 22:

"The phrase \ incapacity which was the ground for retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a 'condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions. We understand Dr Kay's difficulty with the phrase 'coronary insufficiency' as having been that he did not regard it as a disease process. In Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed, coronary insufficiency is defined as 'Prolonged precordial pain or discomfort without conventional evidence of myocardial infarction; subendocardial ischemia due to a disparity between coronary blood flow and myocardial needs'. It is thus a result, a symptom, which is precisely what Dr Kay meant when he declined to regard coronary insufficiency as a disease. But it is we think appropriate to regard it as having constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Likewise, coronary sclerosis may on the evidence be aptly regarded as having been the physical condition which caused or substantially contributed to the coronary insufficiency."

35. In the present case, the incapacity apparent from the reports of Dr Johnston and Dr Moore was anxiety, leading to Mr Rayment's having increasing difficulty in coping with his trade, and particularly in going close to moving machinery. Those reports indicated that that anxiety was, to adopt the words of the Tribunal in Burr, "the outward result" that led to Mr Rayment' s "'being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform his ...duties".

36. Mr Friend submitted, however, that, on the evidence of Dr Trigg and Dr Davie, Mr Rayment's back was, at the time of his retirement, in a condition which would have precluded him from engaging in heavy manual labour, and thus he would have been, for that reason, unable to carry out all of his duties. Mr Friend referred to sub-section 7 (1) of'." the 1976 Act, which in effect equates "invalidity" with "incapacity" in the context of retirement. On that basis, he submitted, in effect, that we should find that Mr Rayment's inability to use his back for- heavy manual labour was a significant factor in "the incapacity which was the ground for- his retirement".

37. Whether or not the conclusion that Mr Rayment's back would, in November- 1983, have precluded him from engaging in heavy manual labour, can be drawn from the evidence of Dr Trigg and Dr Davie, who saw him only in September 1988 and February 1989 respectively, is, in view of the conclusion which we have reached, a matter which it is not necessary for us to decide. We note that in the sick leave records described in paragraph 32 supra only three days of absence, all in December- 1982, were for reasons relating to Mr Rayment's back. He gave evidence of an onset of pain in his back while lifting an anvil late in 1982. However, in December 1982 and January and February 1983, immediately following those three days, he had a total of 38 days sick leave for shingles and post shingles neuralgia, and it may well be that his back continued to trouble him during that period. However, he did not refer in evidence to any such continuing back trouble. He returned to work after those 38 days absence and worked from 22 February to 30 April 1983. He said in evidence that he was physically fit to work at that time, but his wife had to drive him to work (impliedly because of his anxiety).

38. We note also Dr Johnston's letter of referral to Dr Moore, written on 10 June 1983, in which Dr Johnston states that he had examined Mr Rayment on 19 April 1983 to determine his fitness for continued duty and had considered that he should be retired on invalidity grounds, impliedly because of his anxiety state. Dr Johnston mentions in passing that "In December 1982 he 'did his back' while lifting at work and also developed shingles". Given that Dr Johnston was aware of that history, it is to be assumed that he would, in the course of his clinical investigation, have examined Mr Rayment's back and taken the condition of his back into account. He refers in his report to "strained back complaint" but appears not to have considered that the condition of Mr Rayment's back at the time of the examination produced any incapacity relevant to his fitness for continued duty. As set out in paragraph 31 supra, he found Mr Rayment's incapacity to be contributed as to 90 per cent by anxiety state and as to 10 per cent by pes cavus.

39. We will assume, however, for present purposes, that it is a correct conclusion from the evidence that Mr Rayment's inability to perform his duties in November 1983 resulted to a significant extent from an inability to use his back for heavy manual labour as well as from anxiety, so that he had incapacity of two quite different and distinct kinds. It appears to us that there is a further distinction to be drawn beyond the distinction between "incapacity" and a "condition" which is so precisely explained in the passage which we have cited from r. The incapacity or the several incapacities which may lead to a person's being unable to perform duties should be distinguished from "the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement".

40. There is an analogy with the authorities referred to in paragraphs 20 to 22 and 28 supra. What is in issue at this stage is not what were the various forms of incapacity by reason of which the applicant may have been unable to perform his duties. The question is, what incapacity was in the minds of the delegates of the Public Service Board and of the Department of Defence who made the decisions referred to in paragraph 13 supra. From the evidence before us, we are satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that the incapacity in the minds of those delegates which was the ground for the two decisions described in paragraph 13 supra, culminating in Mr Rayment's retirement, was the incapacity apparent from the reports of Dr Johnston and Dr Moore, that is to say, anxiety, using the word in a general and non-technical sense. No specific incapacity resulting from the condition pes cavus is referred to in any of the evidence before us, and we are satisfied that the effects of that condition are not significant for present purposes.

41. Mr Whithear referred, in support of the submission of Mr Friend, to the decision in Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70 at 75-76. However, in our view that decision is authority for the principle there expressed "that the provisions of section 66(2)(c) give to the Commissioner power to have regard to incapacity resulting from conditions other than those recorded as the basis of the Public Service Board's satisfaction as to incapacity", and to do so where it would not be "totally irreconcilable with the view adopted by the Board". The Tribunal was there speaking of other conditions, but not of another incapacitv.

42. The expression "the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement" may well comprise more than one incapacity (section 23(b) Acts Interpretation Act 1901). But that expression cannot be taken as meaning "an incapacity which existed at the time but which was not one of the incapacities by reason of which he was retired". We are concerned here only with the incapacity by reason of which Mr Rayment was retired, and that we have found to be anxiety. The distinction between anxiety on the one hand, and inability to use his back for heavy manual labour for the other is very great. They are two quite separate incapacities. We are satisfied that, on the evidence which is before us, to find that the latter incapacity was a ground for Mr Rayment's retirement would be, in the words of the Tribunal in Whiteford, "totally irreconcilable with the view adopted by the Board".

Question ( c) : the relationship between (a) and (b )

43. We nor turn to consider whether any of the conditions, by reason of which the Superannuation Board, in June 1973, was not satisfied that Mr Rayment's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, or any condition connected with any of those conditions, caused or substantially contributed to the anxiety which we have found to be the incapacity which was the ground of his retirement in November 1983.

44. We would, with respect, adopt in this context the meaning given to the word "substantially" in the following extract from Whiteford (at pages 78-79). This meaning is to be contrasted with the meaning given to that word by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott (1987) 6 AAR 143 where the meaning of the word is affected by its appearing in the expression "wholly or substantially dependent". The Tribunal said in Whiteford:

"Essentially, we are satisfied, the legislation intended that, for a condition to be regarded as substantially contributing to incapacity for the purposes of s 66(2)(c), it must be of such significance in relation to that incapacity as to warrant depriving the employee of a significant part of the benefits which he would otherwise receive under the provisions of the Act. The meaning of \substantially' must, therefore, be considerably higher up the scale of substantiality than \not trivial, minimal or nominal' .On the other hand, in some cases the incapacity may be caused by two or three conditions with each contributing equally to it. In such a case, we have no doubt, each of them must be regarded as substantially contributing to the incapacity. So the meaning of \substantially' in s 66(2)(c) is not as far up the scale as \in the main' or \in the greater part' ."

45. As set out in paragraph 26 supra, we have found the relevant conditions to be recurrent headaches, history of bronchitis and cartilage injury to right knee. It is not in issue that neither the bronchitis nor the cartilage injury is related in any way to the retirement, and those two conditions can be disregarded, leaving for consideration the condition described as "recurrent headaches". The question before us is thus whether that condition, or any physical or mental condition or conditions connected with that condition, caused or substantially contributed to the anxiety which was the incapacity which was the principal ground of Mr Rayment's retirement.

46. It is convenient at this stage to determine what physical or mental conditions caused or substantially contributed to that incapacity. As the Tribunal found in Whiteford, we are not limited to considering only the conditions which may have been in the mind of the delegates of the Public Service Board and of the Department of Defence in making the decisions referred to in paragraph 13 supra.

47. Dr Moore, the specialist psychiatrist who examined Mr Rayment in June 1983 in the context of the assessment of his fitness for further duty ( see paragraph 30 supra) , found him to be then suffering from panic disorder with a depressive 'r component. He saw Mr Rayment again in December 1985, and expanding on his earlier report, wrote:

"The eventual specific illness which this man suffered causing him to leave Commonwealth employment in 1983 was panic disorder with agoraphobia. This is a specific and severe form of anxiety of a disease or illness type."

At the hearing he said "The effect of anxiety disease or generalised anxiety disorder or anxiety state is to make people inefficient", and later "I believe that at the time I saw him. .. -18.6.83- he was unable to work at that time because of this condition".

48. The only other psychiatric witness who made his own finding on this point was Dr Hocking, who examined Mr Rayment in December 1987 at the request of the respondent. He found the mental condition which caused Mr Rayment's retirement incapacity to be "a tension-anxiety state that manifests itself in contraction-type headaches, phobic-anxiety symptoms, and possibly triggers or aggravates episodes of migraine".

49. Dr Spencer, who was Mr Rayment's treating general practitioner from June 1977 to November 1981, treated him with active medication for anxiety and depression throughout that period. Dr Spencer said that when Mr Rayment first consulted him on 15 June 1977 he had been previously taking Serepax, and Dr Spencer continued with that medication and subsequently prescribed Sinequan and Valium. The Serepax was anti-anxiety medication and the Sinequan anti-depressant. The Valium was initially prescribed for functional stress-related symptoms. Mr Rayment said in evidence that he had not taken the Valium which Dr Spencer prescribed, because of his previous experience with that drug. However Dr Spencer said that although he did not see Mr Rayment after November 1981, Mr Rayment continued to receive prescriptions for Valium for a further six to nine months.

50. Dr Johnston, as has been said, found the incapacity to be caused as to 90 per cent by anxiety state.

51. The appropriate conclusion would appear to be that the mental conditions which caused or substantially contributed to Mr Rayment's incapacity were panic disorder with agoraphobia (being a form of anxiety state) and depression. The physical condition pes cavus did not substantially contribute to his incapacity and can be ignored for present purposes.

52. The final question before us is whether either of the conditions panic disorder with agoraphobia (being a form of anxiety state) and depression is the same as, or connected with, the condition described as "recurrent headaches" in the recommendation of the Director of Health set out in paragraph 24 supra. It is not seriously suggested that either of those conditions is "the same as" the condition "recurrent headaches"; the single question in issue is whether they are "connected with" that condition.

53. As to the meaning of "connected with" in this context, we would, with respect, adopt the following passage from page 12 of the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (No ACT G14 of 1988: decided on 4 April 1989 by Morling, Beaumont and Burchett JJ):

"The construction which should be adopted, in the case of this Act, is a reading of s.66(2)(c) as referring to cases where there is a real and substantial connection between the certified condition and the condition that caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity."

That passage is, of course, equally relevant to paragraph l84(2)(c).

54. Mr Rayment said in evidence that in 1963 he had an accident on a trampoline and injured his neck. Since that time he had suffered from headaches at the back of his skull, which would come on if he was working in a cramped position, for example under the dashboard of a vehicle. Later in the same year he began to suffer from a different kind of headache, with tingling in the scalp, blurred vision and vomiting.

55. Both types of headache were diagnosed as migraine by his treating medical practitioner. He was prescribed Cafergot, Valium and anti-histamines. The first doctor who differentiated between the two types of headache was Dr Waugh, in about 1976 or 1977, who diagnosed the first type as muscle tension headaches and the second type as migraine.

56. Mr Rayment continued to suffer from both kinds of headache for a number of years, with one or two migraines and three or four muscle tension headaches each year. The muscle tension headaches increased in frequency after a motor cycle accident in 1970. While serving in Brisbane in 1972, he was advised by a psychiatrist that the migraine might be caused by diet. He altered his diet, ceased taking Valium (suffering significant withdrawal symptoms), and since then has suffered fewer attacks of migraine. We accept the evidence set out in this and the preceding two paragraphs and find accordingly.

57. On that basis we find that the "recurrent headaches" by reason of which (inter alia) the Superannuation Board, in June 1973, was not satisfied that Mr Rayment's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, comprised both the tension headaches and the migraines.

58. The condition diagnosed by Dr Moore as panic disorder with agoraphobia developed in 1982 and was described as follows in Mr Rayment's evidence:

"Did you suffer any other conditions just around Christmas 1982?---Well, I had become extremely nervous at that stage. I could not handle going anywhere where there was a crowd. I found that if I tried to get away from the house -especially by car -I would get maybe a mile away from home and then everything would close in around me. It was the weirdest sensation. It was as if you were si"tting right out in the front of the vehicle trying to drive it and getting along the road with what I class as a maximum speed that I was capable of handling then, of about a mile an hour. I could not go into a crowd; I could go into the shops and do shopping. I would get into a shop and suddenly have to drop anything I was doing and just go. I became a real mess at that stage and did not know which way I was up.

Had you ever felt like that before?---Never in my life." .

59. Professor Jones said in his report of 23 June 1986:

"I note that he has been investigated neurologicallyand it is unlikely that any intracerebral focal cause for migraine exists. The condition can, of course, emerge without obvious cause and that is the likely explanation for it in his case. On balance, therefore, it does seem likely that he has attacks of true migraine and these are mixed with tension headaches. There is a nexus between migraine and tension. It is -;;ell known that, under stress, migraine is cften more frequent in individuals prone to this condition.

[IJt does seem probable that he has an obsessional personality, although this is not a major aspect of his clinical state and I personally believe that a relationship between these two states exists, an obsessional personality being associated with migraine.

There does seem to be sufficient evidence in the notes to indicate an association between his headaches, whether migrainous or tension, and stress, generally they are more prominent under conditions of stress, e.g. imminent moves, dissatisfaction with particular conditions pertaining at various times in his life, death of an uncle, etc.

It is probable that, when faced with particular adverse circumstances, e.g. stress, he was and is prone to suffer adverse mental health manifesting itself as headache, anxiety and sometimes migraine.

[T]here is similarly insufficient evidence to comment on whether his low stress tolerance was a manifestation of an underlying personality disorder prior to July, 1973 but it is probable that it was. In other words the condition which manifested itself as headaches and low stress tolerance while he was in the army was the same condition which led to Mr. Rayment's retirement incapacity, i.e. Panic Disorder, although very substantial progression in it had occurred at the time he was discharged."

Asked about Mr Rayment's evidence of the effect of change of diet on his migraine, he said that particular foods "could be a reason for precipitating migraine given that he is predisposed to it", but that tension was by far the most likely cause of the two.

60. Dr Hocking's view as to the cause of Mr Rayment's retirement incapacity is set out in paragraph 48 supra. Asked as to the possibility of a connection between that condition and the "recurrent headaches related to nervous tension" noted in 1973, he said that the conditions had a common cause or that it was more accurate to state that "slightly different terminology was used to describe what is in effect the same condition". Asked at the hearing what was the common cause, he said:

"Well, the common cause is really his basic personality type of a rather tense and anxious man who, when exposed to stress -or what he perceives as stress, develops the symptoms that have been discussed earlier, particularly the severe. I believe that all of this stems from that cause, that it is anxiety-induced, not perhaps so much the anxiety that we feel the rapid heart beat and all the other things that one can get in an anxiety state, but in his case more so with headaches and, and as I say, later with the development of phobic anxiety symptoms. You see, psychiatric or psychological conditions are not like physical conditions. You cannot sort of get depression one year and a couple of years later develop schizophrenia and then a year after that get bipolar depression. It is not like that. The conditions that have developed the psychiatric conditions -always stem from a similar base and, in this case, as I say, I believe that anxiety has been the basis for all of the symptoms."

61 Dr King said in his report of 6 January 1988 that both migraine and muscle tension headaches are precipitated by stress, although individual attacks of migraine are often triggered by certain foods, or by changes in light, weather or work patterns. He concluded:

"The cause of this man's migraine is firstly an inherited predisposition, the background anxiety state producing stress and the attacks are triggered by certain foods."

As to the other headaches he said "these are muscle contraction or muscle tension headaches and are due to anxiety. Occurring under large trucks does suggest a phobic component". At the hearing he said that anxiety was a common factor in both muscle tension headaches and panic disorders.

62. Dr Moore, in his report of 11 December 1985, saw the migraines as associated with an obsessional personality having an inherent anxiety trait. He found no nexus between the traits of anxiety which Mr Rayment showed on his discharge from the Army in 1973 and the panic disorder with agoraphobia which was present in 1983. He drew a clear distinction between anxiety as a personality trait and anxiety state (including panic disorder with agoraphobia> as a psychiatric condition. It was coincidental that Mr Rayment, having anxiety as a trait in his personality, had developed an anxiety state.

63. Dr Cole, in his report of 8 September 1988, did not see the tension headaches as necessarily due to anxiety, but thought. they were more likely to have been the result of Mr Rayment's working with his head in an awkward position. He made no comment on the migraine in his report. The Tribunal did not find Dr Cole's evidence to be of assistance to it.

64. Having considered the evidence, we find, on the balance of probabilities, that the condition panic disorder with agoraphobia, being a form of anxiety state, is connected with the condition recurrent headaches, in terms of the meaning given to the expression "connected with" in the extract from Benham cited in paragraph 53 supra. We prefer the evidence of Professor Jones, Dr Hocking and Dr King, which, while varying in detail, is generally consistent, to the evidence of Dr Moore and Dr Cole.

65. That being so, we have formed the opinion in terms of paragraph 184(2)(c) of the 1976 Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Rayment's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to bya condition connected with the condition which was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act. Accordingly, as set out in paragraph 27 supra, a BCC is to issue in respect of Mr Rayment setting out the conditions "recurrent headaches, history of bronchitis and cartilage injury to right knee". By virtue of paragraph l84(2)(c) that BCC is deemed to have been in force immediately before Mr Rayment's retirement. The opinion which we have formed necessarily leads to the forming of the opinion, provided for in paragraph 66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act.

66. For the reasons given, both decisions under review will be affirmed.
