ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Pension payable under National Insurance (Industrial Injuries) Act 1946 - whether exempt in terms of Section 23AD(3)(d) - Statutory Interpretation

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.23AD(3)(d)

Social Security Act. 1947

REASONS FOR DECISION

24 August 1989 Mr Dean J. Trowse (Member)

  1. The question for determination in this reference is whether a United Kingdom Industrial Disablement Pension is exempt from income tax in terms of Section 23AD(3)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The year of income is 1986 and the amount in issue totals $3087. The applicant in this matter has unsuccessfully argued this same point before Taxation Boards of Review and the Supreme Court of South Australia and in my understanding this represents his final endeavour. It should be appreciated from the outset that a decision of a judge of the Supreme Court is binding on this Tribunal unless it can be demonstrated that new facts have since emerged and that those facts are of such significance to warrant a different finding.
  2. The applicant was born in England in 1928 and lived there until 1971. During the late 1940s and early 1950s he was employed as a farm labourer and in that role was involved in a tractor accident in August 1951. As a result, he lost fingers and part of the palm of his left hand, which in turn led to an assessment that he had suffered a degree of disablement to the extent of 50%. On that basis he became entitled to a pension under the provisions of the National Insurance (Industrial Injuries) Act 1946 (hereinafter referred to as the Industrial Injuries Act) with payments commencing from 21 October 1951.

3. The Industrial Injuries Act provided a system of compulsory insurance against personal injury caused by accident arising out of and in the course of a person's employment and against prescribed diseases and injuries due to the nature of a person's employment. It repealed the old workmen's compensation legislation and effectively removed the liability for compensation previously placed upon the employer. However, the repeal was subject to the condition that the workmen's compensation legislation continued to apply in cases where the right to compensation arose or had arisen in respect of employment before 5 July 1948.

  1. The applicant emigrated to Australia in 1971 and at all times thereafter has been a permanent resident of that country. Since his arrival in Australia, he has continued to receive payments in respect of his injury. Throughout the whole of the 1986 financial year, the applicant received payments by way of monthly cheques forwarded direct to him from the United Kingdom Department of Health and Social Security. The Australian equivalent of those remittances totalled $3087 and it is that amount which is in dispute. In that same year, the applicant worked as a storeman and received a wage of $15,435.
  2. Section 23AD of the Income Tax Assessment Act provides for the exemption from tax of certain pensions. Subsection 3(d)(i), which is solely relied upon by the applicant, reads as follows:

'(3) The following payments are exempt from income tax:-

(d) payments (other than excepted payments) by way of

(i) pension, allowance or benefit under the Social Security Act 1947;'

The design and effect of subsection (3) is to exempt from tax all welfare payments made under the Social Security Act other than those coming within the description of excepted payments.

6. It thus becomes apparent that the only questions requiring the Tribunal's attention are - were the payments of $3087 made under the Social Security Act 1947 and if so were they other than excepted payments. In broad terms, excepted payments refer to those pensions that are assessable. They appear to fall into two categories -

(1) those pension payments that are assessable only if paid to a prescribed person; and

(2) those pension payments that are assessable irrespective of the age of the recipient.

The term 'prescribed person' is defined in Section 23AD(1) and it is notable that a man who has attained the age of 65 years falls within that classification. It follows that an invalid pension paid by the Commonwealth to a man aged less than 65 years is not an excepted payment and as such will be exempt from tax. Once that same person attains 65 years he becomes a prescribed person and the exemption is lost.

  1. The Social Security Act of 1947 is welfare legislation. Its purpose is to provide for the payment by the Commonwealth of Australia of certain pensions, benefits and allowances to those in need of financial support. Those benefiting in terms of the legislation include the aged, invalids, widows, the unemployed and the sick. Entitlement to these benefits for persons answering the required tests is subject to a means test.
  2. On a preliminary appraisal, it is difficult to perceive an argument supporting the contention that the amount in question was made under the Social Security Act. It was not paid by the Commonwealth of Australia, nor does the character of the payment sit easily with the types of pensions and benefits provided for in that Statute and, in any event, his earnings from other sources would have disqualified the applicant from any entitlement. Nevertheless, the applicant contends that an agreement relating to the reciprocity of Social Services as between the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth of Australia, entered into on the 29 January 1958, transmogrifies the payments in question to payments made under the Social Security Act. Before addressing this submission it is necessary to examine relevant United Kingdom legislation and events which overtook that legislation.
  3. As previously mentioned there existed the Industrial Injuries Act which provided cover for disabilities resulting from employment. It superseded the system of workmen's compensation and was the authority for the pension payment to the applicant. Concurrent with that legislation was the National Insurance Act 1946, the purpose of which was the provision of compulsory insurance against unemployment, sickness, retirement and loss of earnings through pregnancy, widowhood, orphanhood and death.
  4. In 1973 the United Kingdom enacted the Social Security Act 1973, one of the effects being that pensions previously paid in terms of the Industrial Injuries Act were to be paid from a new National Insurance Fund. The nature of the benefit remained unaltered, the only change was the source of funds.
  5. Further changes occurred in 1975. The Social Security Act 1975 came into force on the 6 April 1975. One of the effects of that legislation was to consolidate the Social Security Act 1973 with the Industrial Injuries Act and yet it seems that the original scheme of the later Act continued to operate in the same way albeit from a new and consolidated Act.

12. I now return to a consideration of the agreement entered into by the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth of Australia, the purpose of which is revealed in the preamble to the First Schedule of the Social Services (Reciprocity with United Kingdom) Regulations as:

'... enabling persons who go from one country to the other to receive social security benefits for age, widowhood, invalidity, sickness, unemployment and family responsibilities, as far as possible at the same rate and under the same conditions as persons who have been resident in the latter country.'

13. Article 2 of the agreement, as far as it is relevant, provides -

'(1) The provisions of this Agreement shall apply -

(a) in relation to the United Kingdom -

(i) to the National Insurance Act, 1946, the National Insurance Act (Northern Ireland), 1946, the National Insurance (Isle of Man) Act, 1948, and the legislation in force before the 5th July 1948, which was replaced by those Acts;'

(ii) ...

(b) in relation to Australia, to the Social Services Act 1947-1957.

The Industrial Injuries Act is not included nor has it subsequently been added to the other Acts appearing in the agreement. Clearly the Industrial Injuries Act does not come within the purview of the reciprocity agreement and this is not unexpected having regard to the purpose of the arrangement between the two countries and the nature of the benefit payable under the Industrial Injuries Act. Conversely, one is able to identify a close similarity between the benefits provided in terms of the National Insurance Act, 1946, and the Australian Social Security Act. They represent social welfare payments and thus the reason for reciprocity.

  1. The consolidation of the National Insurance Act into the United Kingdom Social Security Act and the effect of same calls for some comment. Clause 2 of Article 2 of the Agreement of Reciprocity provides that all laws, orders and regulations which carry into effect, amend, supplement or consolidate the provisions contained in the National Insurance Act shall also have application to the Agreement. Those provisions ensure the future operation of the arrangement in accordance with its original intention.
  2. In summation the Industrial Injuries Act does not come within the reciprocal agreement dated the 29 January 1958 and, consequentially, the payments in question were not by way of pension, allowance or benefit under the Social Security Act 1947. Expressed in another form, they are not exempt in terms of Section 23AD(3)(d)(i). This decision accords with those in K38 78 ATC 363 and others which for the sake of anonymity must remain unquoted. However, as previously indicated one of those other cases involved a decision of a judge of the Supreme Court of South Australia which, unless the facts presented or submissions made are significantly different, is binding on this Tribunal. On this issue the applicant alluded to several matters which in his opinion had not been considered in earlier references, but in the main the Tribunal takes the view that those points represent old material re-cycled in a slightly modified form. The exception relates to a possible interpretation of the agreement between the Commonwealth of Australia and the United Kingdom, and I now turn to that submission.
    1. The applicant contends that similar agreements entered into by the United Kingdom and other countries include the Industrial Injuries Act as part of their reciprocal arrangement and that accordingly the exclusion of same in the agreement with the Commonwealth of Australia should be seen as an error or oversight. On that basis it was submitted that the agreement between the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth should be interpreted in such a way as to overcome that inconsistency. Whether this variation does exist was not established and yet it seems that the Commissioner was prepared to accept that such was the case. In short it was submitted that the reciprocity agreement should be read as if the words 'National Insurance (Industrial Injuries) Act 1946' appeared in Article 2.
    2. It is well established that a statute is to be interpreted according to the intention of the Parliament that made it and that that intention is to be found by an examination. of the language used in the statute as a whole (see Amalgamated Society of Engineers v The Adelaide Steamship Co Ltd (1920) 28 CLR at 161). True it is that courts have developed certain rules to assist in the task of statutory interpretation and yet it seems abundantly clear that the judiciary will not place a meaning on the legislation that is contrary to what Parliament intended. Furthermore, whilst it is acknowledged that courts have been known to correct typographical errors and other minor inconsistencies the thought of reading the suggested words into the statute is far too revolutionary. The following quote taken from the case of Thompson v Goold & Co (1910) AC 409 at 420 demonstrates the point -

'It is a strong thing to read into an Act of Parliament words which are not there, and in the absence of clear necessity it is a wrong thing to do.'

  1. The intention of the legislation was to enable persons who go from one country to the other to receive social security benefits for age, widowhood, invalidity, sickness and unemployment. Payments made under the Industrial Injuries Act have as their origin compensation to injured employees. They are not social welfare payments and thus do not come within the previously mentioned description. It is obvious that Parliament never intended that payments made under the Industrial Injuries Act be included in the reciprocal agreement and accordingly the interpretation suggested by the applicant is rejected.
  2. Finally, the applicant referred the Tribunal's attention to certain provisions of the Industrial Injuries Act and more specifically to those contained in Section 31 which relate to the disqualification of entitlement because of absence from Great Britain. In essence, except where regulations otherwise provide, a person shall be disqualified from receiving any benefit for any period during which that person is so absent. For reasons not readily apparent, the applicant takes the view that the regulations calling for the re-instatement of entitlement deems all payments of same to be classified as having been made under the National Insurance Act and as such they come within the province of the Agreement of Reciprocity. The Tribunal has examined the regulations attaching to the Industrial Injuries Act and has not located any reference to such a deeming clause. In fact regulation 30 provides, inter alia, that a person entitled to a disablement benefit shall not be disqualified from receiving benefit because of absence from Great Britain irrespective of the period of that absence. The applicant's submission lacks substance and is therefore rejected.

20. For the reasons stated, the Tribunal affirms the Commissioner's decision upon the objection.
