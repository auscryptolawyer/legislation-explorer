Re Taxation Appeals [1988] AATA 293 (30 September 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. AT86/670-673

AAT No. 4659

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATION APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - lump sum payment by partnership for renewal of Crown Lease - whether capital or revenue payment

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.51(1)

Case V55 88 ATC 413

Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183

W. Neville & Co. Ltd. v F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290

F.C. of T. v Lau (1984) 6 FLR 202

Ure v F.C. of T. [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 50 FLR 219

F.C. of T. v Creer (1986) 17 ATR 548

Sun Newspapers Ltd v F.C. of T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337

Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd v F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 68; (1953) 89 CLR 428

B.P. Australia Limited v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386

HEARING

CANBERRA

30:9:1988

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

This review of objection decisions made by the respondent raises again the problem that was before the Tribunal in Case V55 88 ATC 413. The parties had agreed upon a Statement of Facts and I now set out that statement. There was no oral evidence and I therefore rely upon the agreed Statement, and upon the documents that were tendered in evidence and marked as exhibits, for the findings of fact upon the basis of which these Reasons for Decision proceed.

2. The Statement (which with annexures was marked Ex.B) was as follows, subject to some changes of style and to the deletion or alteration of names and expressions that would identify the applicant:

BLOCK 1 SECTION 33

(i) As at 9 June 1980 the taxpayer was in partnership with

the D. Pty. Limited. The taxpayer had a 60 per cent

interest in the partnership. The partnership

beneficially owned, inter alia, the Crown Lease in

respect of Block 1 Section 33 . . . in the Australian

Capital Territory. The Crown Lease was for a term of

99 years commencing on 1 January 1979 and provided for

payment of an annual rental of $7,300.00. A copy of

the same was annexed.

(ii) On 9 June 1980 the then Minister for the Capital

Territory . . . issued a "Statement for Press" outlining

new policies adopted by him under which business

leases existing in Canberra at that time were able to

be extended or renewed. A copy of the Statement for

Press was annexed.

(iii) By letter dated 24 November 1980 the Department of the

Capital Territory offered to the partnership,

conditional upon payment of a sum of money, the right

to obtain a new Crown Lease of the land providing for

payment of rent at the rate of five cents per annum if

and when demanded. A copy of that letter was

annexed.

(iv) The partnership in which the taxpayer was a partner

agreed to that offer and on 17 July 1981 paid the sum

of $74,443.20. This amount included a payment

calculated on nine times the annual rent under the

previous Crown Lease, totalling $65,700.00. A copy

of the receipt was annexed.

(v) (This paragraph was deleted from the agreed Statement)

(vi) At the same time as payment, the old 99-year Crown

Lease was surrendered. A copy of the Memorandum of

Surrender was annexed.

(vii) On 27 November 1981 the partnership was granted a new

Crown Lease for a term of 99 years commencing on 1

January 1979. A copy of that Lease was annexed.

BLOCK 7 SECTION 29 PHILLIP

(i) As at 9 June 1980 the taxpayer was in partnership with

D. Pty. Limited. The taxpayer had a 60 per cent

interest in the partnership. The partnership

beneficially owned, inter alia, the Crown Lease in

respect of Block 7 Section 29 . . . in the Australian

Capital Territory. The said Crown Lease was for a

term of 50 years commencing on 10 April 1979 and

provided for payment of an annual rental of $4,200.00.

A copy of the same was annexed.

(ii) On 9 June 1980 the then Minister for the Capital

Territory . . . issued a "Statement for Press" outlining

new policies adopted by him under which business

leases existing in Canberra at that time were able to

be extended or renewed. A copy of the Statement for

Press was annexed.

(iii) By letter dated 24 November 1980 the Department of the

Capital Territory offered to the partnership the right

to obtain a new Crown Lease for the land with no

obligations to pay rent upon payment of a sum of

money. A copy of that letter was annexed.

(iv) The partnership in which the taxpayer was a partner

agreed to that offer and on 17 July 1981 paid the sum

of $38,100.20. This amount included a payment

calculated on nine times the annual rent under the

previous Crown Lease, totalling $37,800.00. A copy

of the receipt was annexed.

(v) At the same time as payment the old 50-year Crown

Lease was surrendered. A copy of the Memorandum of

Surrender was annexed.

(vi) On 26 August 1981 the partnership was granted a new

Crown Lease for a term of 99 years commencing on 10

April 1979. A copy of that lease was annexed.

3. As Mr. T.J. Higgins, Q.C., who appeared for the applicant, said at the outset of his submission, the applicant was during the four years of income in question undoubtedly carrying on a partnership business on the lands in question which I shall call Block 1 and Block 7. The relevance of the expenditure that had occurred, namely the payments under paragraph (iv) of the Statement in relation to each block, to the purpose of the partners and thus of the applicant of producing assessable income, was beyond doubt. The question for the Tribunal was whether the payments came within the exceptive provisions of s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), which withdraw from deductibility expenditure that is to be characterised as an outgoing of capital, or of a capital nature. The issue was whether the payments were made for the grant of a new lease, or rather to acquit future payments of rent.

4. Mr. Higgins' contentions were set out in a written outline which he expanded in oral argument. In respect of each property, it was said the partnership was liable to and did pay a market rent. In the case of Block 1 the applicant was offered, and accepted, the opportunity to extinguish the liability for future rent, a liability having a present value in the order of $121,000 and an absolute value of not less than $700,000 according to the applicant's representative, by the present payment of $65,700. The taxpayer also was offered and accepted the opportunity to extinguish the liability for future rent on Block 7, a liability then having a present value of $69,000 and an absolute value of more than $200,000 as calculated by the applicant, by making a present payment of $37,800. No additional consideration (above the amounts payable to extinguish future rental obligations) for the extension of the term of the lease of Block 7 was sought or paid.

5. It was contended by Mr. Higgins that the partnership carried on a business of developing and sub-letting leasehold interests. Its claims to deductions therefore fell for consideration under the second limb of s.51(1). The outgoing in the present case was one arising under contract, and its character was to be determined by reference to the contractual quid pro quo and any other relevant advantage: see Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v. F.C.T. (1980) [1980] FCA 150; 49 FLR 183, esp. at 191, 206. The advantage which arose from the contract entered into was that the partnership was relieved of the payment of future rent which would itself have been a deductible outgoing. In such a case it was submitted that the outgoing answered the description "incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of (or simply 'in') gaining or producing assessable income": see W. Neville & Co Ltd v FCT [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290, at 300-301, 304, 307, 309. The fact that the outlay was incurred in advance of the enjoyment of the said quid pro quo did not, it was said, make it one of capital. To this end, F.C.T. v. Lau [1984] FCA 401; (1984) 6 FCR 202, esp. at 208, 218-9 was relied upon. Here the advantage enjoyed was relief from future liabilities. This was not a case where there was some collateral benefit to an associate (contrast Ure v F.C.T. [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 50 FLR 219; F.C.T. v Creer (1986) 17 ATR 548). Here the parties were entirely at arm's length.

6. The applicant's representative then submitted that the guiding principles were to be found in W. Neville & Co Ltd v F.C.T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290, at 300, 303-4, 306, 308 and Sun Newspapers Ltd v F.C.T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 363. I think it helpful to set out the test laid down by Dixon J. in the latter case to determine whether a payment is capital or revenue in nature at 363:

"There are, I think, three matters to be

considered, (a) the character of the advantage

sought, and in this its lasting qualities may play

a part, (b) the manner in which it is to be used,

relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under the

former head recurrence may play its part, and (c)

the means adopted to obtain it; that is, by

providing a periodical reward or outlay to cover

its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with

the payment or by making a final provision or

payment so as to secure future use or enjoyment."

7. When these guidelines are applied to the present case it will, the argument ran, be seen that no asset was acquired by the outlay (cf. Neville at 300.1, 303.9, 306.6; Sun Newspapers at 363 paragraph (a)). Both before and after the payment the taxpayer partnership had the use of the leasehold land. No more happened than that it was relieved of future rental outgoings. As to Block 7, see below. Furthermore, relief from rent did not "affect the whole structure of the company's business" (see Neville at 300.2). The payment of, and thus the procurement of relief from later payment of, rent was an ordinary incident of the partnership business. The leasehold estate continued to be used and enjoyed in the taxpayer's business: Sun Newspapers at 363 paragraph (b). The outlay was in a sum commensurate with, and certainly not excessive having regard to, the future rental commitments in respect of, the enjoyment of the land for the balance of the term of the lease (Sun Newspapers at 363 paragraph (c)). Despite some of the casual language used, the essential nature of the outlay was that it extinguished future rental obligations "accruing de die in diem" (Sun Newspapers at 363).

8. As to the circumstance that the lease in respect of Block 7 was varied not only by reducing the rent almost to nil but also by extending its term, it was contended for the applicant that the extension of the term was a variation independent of the abatement of rent. Reference was made to "Case (C)" referred to on the last page of Attachment B to Ex.B, the Statement for Press issued by the then Minister for the Capital Territory. As was evidenced by a table annexed to the submission, the present value of rent payable at market rates for 99 years differed by an insignificant margin, namely less than 2 per cent from the present value of such rent for a term of 50 years. The sum of $37,800 was paid, it was argued, for the extinguishment of future rent and not for the extension of the term. There was no difference in the calculations for Block 1 and Block 7.

9. In Case V55 I had before me a situation on the face of it precisely the same as that which is here in issue in relation to Block 7. In that case a prepayment had been made which resulted in the grant of a new Crown Lease for a term of 99 years in replacement of the former lease for only 50 years. At the time of the surrender thereof the lease had run about eight years. I held that the prepayment was not deductible.

10. It is convenient to refer at this point to what Mr. Higgins said about Case V55. He said that to the extent that that decision proceeded on the basis that the sum paid was to be characterised as a payment for the grant of a new lease for an extended term he did not quarrel with it. But he contended that on that basis of reasoning, the situation in the present case in relation to Block 7 was different from the situation in Case V55 in that here the applicant claimed to have established that the extended term of 99 years that had been obtained was not obtained in consideration of payment. That, he said, had not been demonstrated in Case V55. If Case V55 was to be perceived as having been based on the view that the payment having been a once only payment to secure the acquittal of a future obligation extending over a considerable period of time it should on that footing be regarded as an outgoing of capital, then Case V55 was wrongly decided. The mere fact that the making of a payment procured an enduring benefit was but one factor, and the real question was for what the payment was made. As previously stated, Mr. Higgins' argument was that the extension of the term was a variation independent of the abatement of rent, and the sum of $37,800 that was paid was so paid for the extinguishment of future rent and not for the extension of the term. As to Block 1, no alteration of the term of the lease was involved and the reasoning in Case V55 did not, it was argued, apply.

11. Into this combat rode Mr. Gibb, counsel for the Commissioner, entering the lists in the first place as champion of Case V55. He observed that the Tribunal had not in that case relied on any one test or factor but had pointed to a number of features which were all found to have led in the one direction. The Tribunal had had regard to-

(i) Fullagar J's test as expressed in the Colonial Mutual

Life Assurance Society Ltd v F.C. of T. (1953) 89 CLR

428, stressing an inquiry into what the money was paid

for, and as to whether what was really paid for was in

truth and substance a capital asset. Such enquiries

which should look to matters of substance rather than

of form.

(ii) The fact that the partnership was able to use the

property for a further 91 years.

(iii) Payment was by a lump sum whereby an asset of lasting

value was acquired.

(iv) The method adopted for calculating the payment for the

new lease.

(v) The resemblance of the payment to a lease premium.

12. The Tribunal in Case V55 having had regard to all of these features, it was, Mr. Gibb argued, nothing to the point for the applicant to seize on one aspect of the matter and to claim that the precedent value of Case V55 was lost. What had to be done was to draw overall inferences from all of the circumstances as a whole: See B.P. Australia Limited v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 at 387, 397.

13. Approaching the matter in this way, and leaving Case V55 to its destiny, Mr. Gibb went on to submit that, leaving aside the de minimis rent of five cents per annum, the payments here were made for release from a recurrent liability to pay rent periodically, and to secure future retention of an interest in land tantamount to, if not identical with, a freehold estate capable of use and enjoyment for a very long time. It was erroneous to suggest that the payments were made merely to obtain release from the obligation to pay rent. There was also obtained a right to a continued use and enjoyment of the property for a very long time, in one case for an expanded time.

14. The point was made clearer, it was argued, when the differences between the situation pertaining to Block 1 and to Block 7 were regarded. So far from concluding that there was any material difference between the two situations, the Tribunal should find in their similarities confirmation of the approach which holds that more than a commutation of rent was involved in the payments. The enduring benefits that were obtained by each transaction were different in quantitative terms as between the two blocks, but the terms by which the payments were calculated were the same notwithstanding that the interest acquired was of a longer duration in one case than in the other. That, Mr. Gibb said, highlighted the unacceptability of any suggestion that there was a commuting of rent. There was no material difference between the payment in respect of Block 1 and that in respect of Block 2, save that the overall inference of capital was more easily drawn in the case of Block 7.

15. Mr. Higgins had argued that the fact that the prepayment was a direct multiplication of the pre-existing rent showed that it was related to nothing else but the obligation to pay rent, and did not differ in the case where there was an increased term. This however, like much else in the arguments in the case, seems to me to be a two-edged sword, and it is in my opinion at this point that the case should be found to go against the applicant. If the payments were related to no more than an obligation to pay rent, it could surely have been expected that there would have been a differential between the prepayment required in the case of Block 1 and that required in the case of Block 7. The "nine times" multiplication factor was arbitrary and of generalised application notwithstanding the length of the unexpired terms that were to run. True it is that no additional amount could be pointed to as relating to the term of occupation as opposed to an amount relating to the acquittal of rent, but neither does it appear to me that the amounts paid can be found to have been no more than a commutation of rent. Their only perceived connection with rent was the nine times multiplication factor. In each case there was a grant of a new lease. The extinguishment of a future obligation to pay rent was occasioned not by the payment of the "nine times rent" but by the termination by surrender of the old lease, the grant of a new lease, and the subjection of the parties thereto to each and every one of the terms and conditions therein contained. It is a fact that the payment was calculated by reference to the amounts payable under the old lease. The new lease however provided for no rent other than a nominal amount. Once there was a new lease the rights conferred by it, and the liabilities imposed by it, stood apart from the old lease, which had required, as a liability imposed upon the lessee, the payment of a significant rent.

16. In determining whether the amount paid, on the execution of each of the new leases, was a capital or revenue payment it is necessary to refer to the test considered in Sun Newspapers at 363 (see paragraph 6 of these Reasons for Decision). I consider that the character of the advantage sought and obtained here was of an enduring nature. It was not unlike the purchase of a freehold interest. As I stated in Case V55, had the partnership decided that it no longer wanted to use the blocks, and thus wished to determine the leases, the sum paid under each of the leases would not have been refunded. Furthermore, the payment was made by way of a lump sum to secure the future "use or enjoyment" of each block. There was no recurrency of either payment. As was stated in BP Australia Limited, in deciding this issue it is necessary to look at all factors. In this case, as in Case V55, all the facts point in my opinion towards the payments being capital in nature. In short, I agree with the submissions put by Mr. Gibb and in consequence the objection decisions under review must be affirmed.

17. I should say for the record that I do not see the Minister's Press Statement as being a legitimate reference source for the purpose of determining the nature of the contractual arrangements that ensued and in the context of which the payments here in question fall to be characterised. I do not see how this can possibly be such a reference source, although both sides referred to the statement, presumably as somehow being part of the general background. I intend no disrespect by saying that to me it seems no more admissible than a sales pitch by a vendor's estate agent, save that it is legitimate to take from it, as the parties did, the uncontested fact that the prepayment was calculated on the basis that it was to represent a sum being nine times the annual rent payable under the existing Crown Lease. That it was so could of course have been deduced, all relevant facts being known, by a mathematical process without referring to the statement. The case must in the last resort be decided by having regard to the legal arrangements entered into by the parties.
