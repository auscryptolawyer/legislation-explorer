1. These references arise out of the commercial aspects of the life of a married couple. I shall refer to them as "Troy" and "Helen". In 1976 they caused a Company ("the Company") to be established to carry on the business of an estate agency. From the commencement of the agency Troy worked full-time in the business and Helen on a casual, irregular, part-time basis, lending such support as was needed and she was able to provide. By 30 June 1978 there was a deficiency in shareholders' funds amounting to $2,804 but, in the year following, there was a substantial improvement in sales commissions (from $127,707 to $189,257) and a net profit of $3,205 was returned for the year. That net profit arose after taking into account wages for Troy at $18,955; for Helen at $11,462; and "superannuation contributions", said to have been made on behalf of "employees", in the sum of $34,500.

  1. The Fund. ("the Fund") to which the contributions had been made was "The (Company) Employees' Retirement Fund" established by a deed of 28 February 1979 which, at some date unspecified, but after August 1979, and after amendments had been effected to the deed at the suggestion of the Commissioner, received the approval of the Commissioner as satisfying the requirements of S.23F of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act"). When the Fund lodged its 1979 income tax return in November 1979 it showed that no income had been derived for that year; that a loss of $420 had been incurred; that Members' Funds stood at $34,122 ($34,542 less $420) and were represented by moneys at bank $29,863 and "insurance premiums at cost" $4,529 - a discrepancy in the asset figures of $270 was not explained. No evidence was put forward to identify the policies referred to or even to identify in relation to which persons the policies had been taken out. The Company from time to time had employees other than Troy and Helen, as can be seen from a later Table.
  2. Unbeknown to Troy and Helen at the commencement of the Fund, major problems lay ahead for them; both commercially, in that the business was never again to achieve the turnovers of the year of income ended 30 June 1979; and personally, in that Troy's health was to deteriorate markedly. That was ultimately to result in major heart surgery for him in November 1983. For a substantial period before that date and at all times since, Troy was said by Helen to have suffered from a condition of acute depression. Even so, for some years prior to the hearing, he was able- to maintain a full-time occupation as a real estate salesman in the employ of a third party. I am not persuaded that his health was so poor that it was reasonable for him not to appear as a witness either on the first day of hearing when he was at work or some two months later when the hearing was resumed. As to the latter occasion, a medical certificate provided confirmation that he is not a well man, but not that he was too ill to attend and give evidence.

6. As 30 June 1983 approached, Troy and Helen were troubled about the financial state of the Company, but I accept the evidence of Helen that they remained optimistic and concerned to continue with the business. The fact that major heart surgery for Troy was pending was not then recognized. As a result, in June 1983, decisions were taken which, according to the case presented for the Applicants, resulted in the entirety of the Fund being distributed to Members. But only two Members were to benefit. They were Troy ($69,038) and Helen ($20,838). In due course I shall address questions which have arisen as to whether any other persons ever had been Members of the Fund; whether they had had any entitlements; whether those entitlements had been forfeited; whether the benefit of any forfeited entitlements properly passed to Troy and Helen; and whether anything at all was distributed to anyone.

Having heard the evidence of Helen, I am satisfied that both she and Troy drew regular salaries as employees of the Company up to the time of payment to them of the moneys from the Fund and that thereafter they did not again draw any regular amounts by way of salary. I find that they continued in office as Directors of the Company and that Director's fees were paid to them, substantially in accordance with earlier practice as to Director's fees. In any event what was paid to them thereafter as Director's fees was not so different from what had been paid before as Director's fees as to suggest that what was paid to them was a substitute for what had previously been paid to them by way of both Director's Fees and salary. On the other hand, I am not persuaded that there was any change or reduction in the services rendered by Troy or Helen to the Company after they ceased to draw "salary".

"lump sum payment included in assessable income increased from $3,047 to $60,938".

No additional tax was levied.   1. The Fund was established by a Deed executed in February 1979. It resulted from approaches made to Troy by a life assurance commission agent. The agent gave evidence before me. In doing so he reflected the standards common to his calling: he was well presented and a gifted talker, confidently and easily expressing himself in relation to familiar matters. He is a man who has had a long history in that calling. I accept him as a completely honest witness. He promoted the establishment of the Fund with a view to commissions to be derived by himself by negotiation of policies of life assurance and also because he shared with a firm of superannuation consultants the profit to be generated for them on the setting up of superannuation funds.
  2. The salesman was well aware of his standard practices but he had little memory for detail in relation to the particular Fund. He remembered calling from time to time on Troy at his office and he also remembered clearly an occasion on which he had travelled a substantial distance in order to speak with Paul in relation to the Fund. Beyond that he could not re-call any detail. In the time available to him he had not even been able to obtain details of the life policies he negotiated. No witness from the superannuation consultants was called although the firm is still in business. I accept that the agent believed that he would have told Paul, and any one else whom he thought had been admitted to the scheme, something of their entitlements and I accept that he thought it probable that he had told the "Members" that they would have no vested entitlement to contributions credited to their names until they had served the Company for a substantial qualifying period of, perhaps, 10 years from which date vested entitlements would rise progressively. He spoke of such practices as being common, offering proprietors the prospect of ultimately benefiting from the forfeited benefits of others; and of a vesting schedule which would specify particulars in relation to a vesting programme; but said that he believed that such a schedule could not be incorporated in the trust deed because the deed needed to provide for an absolute discretion in the Trustee in order to satisfy the requirements of the Commissioner as to Section 23F of the Act.

15. I accept his evidence that his communications with "members" or prospective "members" would have been brief and that, explaining matters such as he had in mind, would be quite forgettable. But although I accept that he spoke with Paul and, more probably than not, with Peter and Mary, I am not persuaded that those communications were detailed, or that they communicated to the "members" any sense of entitlement either to receive benefits or to have an accounting from the Trustee for the administration of the Fund. Further, I am not satisfied that any explanation such as he might, have given took place at any particular point in time. Moreover, I am not satisfied that any individual was provided with any notice in writing advising that person of his or her entitlement, whether in the form of "notice of entitlement" set forth in the schedule to the deed or otherwise. Further, despite the statement in that specimen notice of entitlement that "a copy of the Trust Deed is held at the employer's office where it is available for inspection", I am not persuaded that the "members" were aware of any contention that the Trust Deed was at the Employer's office available for inspection.

16. I next proceed to consider what the supposed "members" knew of the matter. I accept that both Troy and Helen were aware that a Fund existed; that substantial moneys had been transferred to it; that amounts were being credited to Peter, Paul and Mary and to themselves; and that future disposition of all of the Funds was under their control as Directors of the Company. I accept that the Trust Deed was available to Troy and Helen prior to any date upon which the Company first made contributions to the Fund. As to the other "members", I have heard the evidence of both Peter and Paul, and although I accept the evidence of the agent that be had spoken with all whom he believed to be "members", I am not persuaded that any communication was made such as made Peter, Paul or Mary aware of any interest in the Fund personal to himself or herself; of any rights they might have against the Trustee; or of any right to be considered as a possible recipient of accumulated benefits on leaving the service of the Company. I am satisfied that none of those "members" consented to forfeiture of any claim which any of them have against the Fund.

No "written" report of all decisions, transactions and findings of the Trustee" such as was contemplated by clause 27(a) was produced in evidence before me and I am not persuaded that any such written record existed. The only thing purporting to constitute any written record of the decisions of the Trustee are such entries as appeared in the income tax returns of the Fund.

20. Superannuation schemes commonly exist in order to provide benefits for Members in the event of their retirement or for their dependants in the event of death before retirement or later. They provide benefits which are deferred: benefits generated by earlier income-producing activity of the member. As a matter of social policy, they have been encouraged. Superannuation funds meeting statutory criteria have been subject to favourable rates of tax upon their earnings even to the point of being exempt; employers have been entitled to deductions (within approved limits) for contributions made; income earners have been able to take immediately the benefit of the prospect of future superannuation payments without being liable to income tax on the present value of that entitlement; and ultimately to take "lump sum" benefits with limited liability to income tax. Just what advantages there have been has varied from time to time. Provision for such superannuation may be made by the contributions of employers, or members, or both. Many schemes exist in the public sector and among the major bureaucracies. Many schemes are promoted commercially by banks and life assurance offices. Generally speaking, the larger schemes are so managed that they cannot be said to be capable of being manipulated in the interests of any small group of persons. On the other hand, in smaller organisations, community of interest between the trustees of the funds and the employers is commonly reflected in the circumstance that the same individuals control both. That makes it possible for superannuation schemes to be manipulated to the detriment of employees who are without such control and of the Revenue; and to the advantage of the controllers. The submissions for the Commissioner are that that is what happened in the present instance. It is the further contention of the Commissioner that by reason of such considerations:

(a) the Fund was not a "superannuation fund" at all or, alternatively, that it was not a superannuation fund which satisfied the requirements of Section 23F of the Assessment Act; and

(b) That the monies received by Troy and Helen from the Fund did not constitute amounts paid "in a lump sum in consequence of retirement from or the determination of any office or employment, ...." within the meaning of Section 26(d) of the Assessment Act so as to constitute only five per cent of the amount received as assessable income.

THE FUND ISSUE

21. The first question to be determined is whether the rate of tax payable by the fund should be 46% or the higher rate of 61.07% (60% in the second and later years) contended for by the Commissioner. Section 7(2) of the Income Tax (Companies and Superannuation Funds) Act 1979 ("the Tax Act") provides:

"7(2). The rate of tax payable by a trustee of the superannuation fund in respect of investment income of the fund in respect of which the trustee is liable, in pursuance of S.1210 of the Assessment Act, to be assessed and to pay tax is 46."

22. Section 121D of the Assessment Act provides (so far as is material)

"The trustee of a superannuation fund

(a) ...;
  • (b) to which s.23F applies in relation to a year of income, shall be assessed and is liable to pay tax, at the rate declared by the Parliament for the purposes of this section, upon the investment income of the fund of the year of income, to the extent to which that income is, by virtue of S.121C, not exempt from income tax."

23. To succeed in its contention the Fund must establish that it is in the relevant sense a "superannuation fund" and in addition, that it is a superannuation fund to which Section 23F of the Assessment Act applies. Section 3(1) of the Tax Act and Sections 23F and 121B of the Assessment Act all define "superannuation funds" in common terms. Those terms are as follows:

"In this Act (Division or section, as the case may be), unless the contrary intention appears

...

Superannuation fund means a provident, benefit, superannuation or retirement fund;

....

However, as Windeyer J. said in Scott v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) [(1966) 14 A.T.D. 333 at 351]:

"There is no definition in the Act of a superannuation fund. The meaning of the term must therefore depend upon ordinary usage, the attributes of a thing thus denominated being those which things ordinarily so described have

Later his Honour went on to say:

"I have come to the conclusion that there is no essential single attribute of a superannuation fund established for the benefit of employees except that it must be a fund bona fide devoted as its sole purpose to providing for employees who are participants money benefits (or benefits having a monetary value) upon their reaching a prescribed age... I do not put this forward as a definition but rather as a general description."

In that case his Honour went on (at p. 352) to consider whether the scheme under consideration (including the deed by which it was constituted):

"was intended to be a mere facade behind which activities might be carried on which were not to be really directed to the stated purposes but to other ends

...

In the extreme circumstances of that case his Honour concluded that:

"there never was in truth a superannuation fund established for the benefit of employees."

His Honour then added:

"J should add that if there were such a fund established it was not in my view being applied at any time for the purposes for which it was established, that it to say for the benefit of employees according to its terms."

24. However, by the standards expressed by his Honour in Scott, I am satisfied that the fund here in question was not "a mere facade" but that it was intended that it should be operated and maintained as the law permitted with a view to providing benefits for employees at retirement or for their dependents in the event of death. It was not the less so because membership and benefits might (as his Honour recognised in Scott);

"depends very much on the wishes, perhaps on the whims, of the employer."

Nor was it the less a fund for the benefit of "employees" because the only employees to ultimately "benefit" were Troy and Helen.

25. The next problem is to determine whether the Fund is a fund to which Section 23F of the Assessment Act applies. The principal provision of Section 23F as initially enacted and as it was to remain in force until the enactment of Division 9B was Section 23(15). It provided:

"Subject to the next three succeeding sub-sections, the income derived during the year of income by a superannuation fund to which this section applies in relation to the year of income is exempt from income tax. "

In the period immediately following its enactment, Section 23F of the Assessment Act conferred a most substantial benefit on all funds to which the section applied. It conferred exemption from tax. With the enactment of Division 9B of the Act the benefits for a fund such as this, assuming that Section 23F applied, were over-ridden leaving the fund liable to pay tax at a rate of 46%: the corporate tax rate, being the rate also payable by the employer-company.

26. In determining whether or not the Fund is a fund to which Section 23F of the Assessment Act applies it is necessary that the Fund should satisfy all of the requirements specified in Section 23F(2) of the Assessment Act or be relieved from the consequences of non-compliance (Section 23F(7)). The argument for the Commissioner was that the Fund did not comply with Section 23F of the Assessment Act because it failed to satisfy the requirements of Section 23F(2) which provided that (inter alia):

"... this section applies ... to a superannuation fund ... if

...

(e) the right of each employee and his dependents to receive benefits from the fund is defined by the terms and conditions applicable to the fund and notice in writing of the existence of that right was given to the employee not later than the time when contributions were first paid to the fund in respect of the employee or of his dependents or 31 March 1966, whichever is the later, or before such later date as the Commissioner approves in relation to the employee;

...

I am satisfied that the rights so referred to were all defined by the terms and conditions applicable to the Fund. But I am not satisfied that notice in writing of the existence of those rights was given to the employees prior to the required date. That being so, the Fund did not at any time constitute a fund to which Section 23F of the Assessment Act applied, unless Section 23F(7) can be invoked to grant relief.

27. Section 23F(7) provides that:

"Where a requirement specified in sub-section (2) has not been complied with in relation to a superannuation fund in relation to a year of income but the trustee of the fund satisfies the Commissioner that, by reason of special circumstances that existed in relation to the fund during that year of income, it would be reasonable for this section to have effect as if that requirement had been complied with, this section has effect as if that requirement had been complied with."

It may be that, in relation to many funds for the benefit of employees, the awareness of entitlements on the part of employees and the quality of administration of the fund by those controlling it may be such that a failure to comply strictly with the provisions of Section 23F(2) should be readily excused. But with a fund so closely controlled as the present; when consciousness on the part of employees of membership is non-existent; and when the person principally directing the affairs of the fund has chosen not to present as a witness, I have not been persuaded that "special circumstances" such as would justify relief did exist. That being so the assessments against the trustee of the fund will be confirmed.

THE MEMBERS' ISSUES

28. The question which then arises for the individual Applicants is not likely to be of so little significance. The Applicants by their tax agent and accountant put the matter simply. They acknowledged having received distributions from the Fund amounting to $60,938 and $20,838 for husband and wife respectively and they each claimed that they should only be assessable as to 5% of that sum. But as I have indicated, the notion that those sums or any part of them might have constituted something of value in any meaningful sense is an illusion.

29. Prior to the distribution the Applicants were contingently entitled to make claims upon the Fund and, as the only persons entitled to make such claims to the assets of the Fund, they could effect its winding up and the distribution of its nett worth to themselves. That nett worth, as I have found, was nil. Instead of the Fund being wound up and it being recognised that there was nothing to be be claimed by the Applicants, by a series of transactions recorded in the relevant accounts, there was a "round robin" whereby it was represented that:

(a) the Company paid in full to the Fund what it owed the Fund;

(b) the Fund paid in full to the Members the entire book value of the Fund without any provision having been made for the writing off of bad debts or of other assets of reduced or non-existent value; and

(c) the Members (of necessity) on-lent the monies so credited to them to the Company.

thereby completing a circle - a circle appropriately represented by the symbol for "Zero".

  1. Having carried out those transactions, the Applicants then acknowledged receipt of $60,938 and $20,838 respectively and in each instance claimed that 5% of the amount received, and only 5% of the amount received (Reseck v F.C.T. [(1975) 133 C.L.R. 453, was liable to assessment to income tax. The Commissioner contends that the Applicants are not entitled to the relief provided by Section 26(d) of the Assessment Act and contends that the entirety constitutes assessable income. The Applicants did not dispute the Commissioner's assertion that, if not assessable as to only 5%, the entirety constituted assessable income.
  2. If it was appropriate to consider the transaction as an entirety and to use the approach adopted by the High Court of Australia in F.C.T. v Becker [(1952) 67 C.L.R. 456] in measuring "profit", one might conclude that the value received by the Applicants was "nil" and that in consequence the "income" derived was "nil". Being concerned at the apparent injustice of considering persons as being liable to income tax when they had derived no value, I invited submissions from the parties. In doing so, I made particular reference to the decision of the High Court of Australia in Permanent Trustee Co. of N.S.W. Limited v. C. of T. ((1940) 2 A.I.T.R. 109). In that case, a creditor accepted from his debtor then in default a further promise to pay and a security which was quite inadequate to ensure performance of the obligations as to which default had been made. The Commissioner contended that, by accepting the security, thereby the taxpayer had derived income. Rich J. rejected the submission saying (inter alia at p. 111):

"But here the facts show that the deceased got nothing except a new obligation to pay in exchange for an existing obligation to pay. He was no nearer getting his money or of transferring it into anything of any value".

  1. The difficulty facing the taxpayers in seeking to invoke the principle expressed in that statement was that, instead of dealing only with their debtor, they discharged their debtor and substituted another - albeit a debtor of no greater substance.
  2. When submissions were received it was Counsel for the Commissioner who argued that the "round robin" was a commercial reality; was not a sham; and was anything but a fiscal nullity. His argument was that the transactions were real and effective in that they brought about the release of the trustee from its obligations to the taxpayers as "members" of the superannuation fund; and at the same time brought about the satisfaction of the indebtedness of the company to the trustee. By the same course of events, the taxpayer members ceased to have any further claim upon the trustee and in lieu thereof, became creditors of the company. In the event, the claims they had against the company were to prove to be valueless, but it was not necessarily so. What value if any, they would ultimately derive from their investment in the Company would depend upon whether or not the Company was returned to prosperity.
  3. The argument was well-founded in authority. In Joseph v. Campbell ((1933) [1933] HCA 34; 50 C.L.R. 317), in a joint decision, the High Court of Australia had rejected an argument

"that an apparent payment in cash is (necessarily) illusory because it is annihilated by a counter payment" (at p.324)

although acknowledging that

"payment and counter payment by crossed cheques may raise suspicion" (p.326).

But I am satisfied that on the facts established before me the transactions were anything but illusory. To quote the words of Helsham J. in Perpetual Trustee Co. Ltd. v. Barnett ((1969) 90 W.N. (Part 1) N.S.W. p.637 at 650- 651):

"But no authority has been cited to me which would dispose me to hold that an exchange of cheques in pursuance of a real and legally permissable transaction can be attacked on the basis that each party knows and intends that the cheques shall cancel each other out so far as the bank is concerned, or that this procedure is adopted from the knowledge that neither party has funds aliunde to meet them".

35. Unfortunately for the individuals concerned their liability to income tax must be determined by reference to what they did do; not by reference to what they might have done; and not by reference to any concept of economic equivalence. If the Applicants have followed a course which has needlessly exposed them to liability to income tax, they must accept the consequences of that course of conduct. That being so, I hold that it is not open to have regard to the significance of what was done by reference to anything other than the acknowledged dollar value of the amounts acknowledged to have been received. For those reasons, I hold that they did derive the amounts of $60,938 and $20,838; and that, subject to Section 26(d) of the Act, the amounts derived constituted assessable income within the meaning of Section 25(1) of the Assessment Act.

36. The question then arising is whether the sums received constituted the

"capital amount of any allowance, gratuity or compensation ... paid ... in a lump sum in consequence of retirement from, or the termination of any office or employment ..." (Section 26 (d))

Upon the evidence the drawing of wages ceased on some date unspecified during the year of income ended 30 June 1983. The date of the last payment of salary was not clearly specified but, in as much as salaries for the year fell to less than one third of the figures for the previous year, in all probability the salary was last paid during 1982. The winding up of the fund came on 30 June 1983. Section 26(d) of the Assessment Act requires that payment such as was made must have been paid in a lump sum (which is not an issue) but it must have been;

"in consequence of retirement from, or the determination of, any office or employment ...."

I am not persuaded that it was. The evidence does not persuade me that there was any "office or employment" of which one could say that there had been a "termination" or "retirement". The concept of retirement involves the notion of being relieved of duties: but for this couple there was no such relief. The notion of "termination" in this context contemplates an act which brings to an end pre-existing obligations to serve for, and to be rewarded for, service. But all that happened was that there was a failure upon the part of the payee to pay. There is no evidence which persuades me that the "right", such as it was, of Helen and Troy to be remunerated for their services had come to an end. The evidence is that after the "payment" was made both Applicant were still carrying out all the duties to the Company which they had been performing previously in the service of the Company. The only material changes were that they had ceased to draw a salary (as distinct from director's fees) and that the superannuation fund was wound up. For those reasons the argument based upon Section 26(d) of the Assessment Act must fail.

38. The argument for Troy that there no power in the Commissioner under Section 170 of the Act to issue amended assessments against him can be shortly dealt with. The argument was wholly founded in an assertion that the Commissioner was precluded from amending because Troy had made "a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment" under the original assessments. 1 am not persuaded that there was. What had been told to the Commissioner and before the assessment of Troy under the original assessments may have been true, but it was certainly anything but "a full disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment". That argument must fail.

ADDITIONAL TAX

  1. When the assessments in question were raised against the individuals in 1986, additional tax was levied against Troy in the sum of $20,414. and against Helen in the sum of $4,020. Upon the hearing, Counsel for the Commissioner conceded that additional tax should be wholly remitted. The concession was appropriate, even if belated. Accordingly, additional tax will be wholly remitted.
  2. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons the determinations of the Commissioner under review in relation to the Fund will be confirmed. In relation to Troy and Helen they will be varied by setting aside in each case the imposition of additional tax for incorrect returns.

40. Whether any further relief will be available for this couple will be a matter for the Taxation Relief Board.
