Papadopoulos and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 540 (29 June 1993)

Last Updated: 25 August 2008



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - deferred benefit - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise election - whether discretion should be exercised - whether acceptable explanation for delay - decision affirmed



Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss135, 136, 137, 157

Tribunal : Dr D J Grimes, Senior Member

Mr T R Russell, Member

Mr H G Julian, Member



1. This is an application to review a decision of 29 November 1991 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation( the Commsioner). The decision, made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), was that the Commissioner declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 14 April 1989 under sub-section 137(1) should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act. This decision was affirmed by a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation under section 154 of the Act.



2. The benefit which the applicant seeks is that provided by s 136, entitled deferred benefits, but often referred to as preservation of superannuation rights. Sub-section 137(1) of the Act prescribes the period in which it was open to the applicant to make an election to preserve her superannuation rights.; that being 21 days immediately after she ceased to be an eligible employee. The relevant parts of s137 are as follows:

"s137(1) A person who -



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply, and upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.



(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -



(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and



(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."



3. Section 157 sub-section (1) contains a discretionary power, vested in the Commissioner for Superannuation, to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. These circumstances are as follows:

"s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."



4. The hearing of the applicant's appeal was held in Canberra on 2 April 1993. The applicant was represented by Mr Michael Coghlan, and the respondent was represented by Ms T Munoz, and evidence for the respondent was given by:

(a) Ms Cindy Royston, an officer of the Personnel Department of the Department of Primary Industries and Energy.

(b) Mr Bryan Charles Hooley, an officer of the Department of Industry, Technology and Commerce (as it was then called) from 1982 to 1988, in both the salaries and personnel section.



5. The following exhibits were taken into evidence by the Tribunal:

For the Applicant



Resignation Action Sheet Exhibit A1



Letter Ms Royston to Commissioner for Superannuation

dated 2 February 1993 Exhibit A2



Induction Record of Applicant Exhibit A3



For the Respondent



Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme Contributions

letter dated December 1978 Exhibit R1



Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme New Contributors

The Tribunal also had before it documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.



6. The applicant was born on 25 June 1948 and after graduating with an Honours Degree in Economics from the University of Adelaide, joined the then Bureau of Agricultural Economics (BAE) within the Department of Primary Industry in late 1969 and continued in that employment for several years. In 1970 the applicant joined the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS), having become an "eligible employee" as provided in the Act, by virtue of her employment in the BAE.



7. In February 1980, the applicant's husband, a permanent employee of the Commonwealth Public Service, was transferred to Melbourne. The applicant's husband was of the belief that he would be relocated to Canberra, however he was uncertain of when that would occur.



8. The applicant stated that in order to join her husband in Melbourne she had applied for several positions in the Commonwealth Public Service in Melbourne but



was not successful. Finally, she secured an appointment in Melbourne, on a 2 year assignment with the Victoria Government Ministry of Housing, and applied for leave without pay from the Commonwealth Public Service to take up that appointment. The applicant said that her application to take up the Melbourne appointment was approved by the Commonwealth Public Service Board on the basis that the experience gained in such an appointment would enhance her economic skills valued by the BAE, and subject to the following further conditions:

".........



4. The Department of Finance has advised that officers granted leave under Section 72A of the Public Service Act are required to continue their contributions to the superannuation scheme during the period of leave. Ms Papadopoulos should be advised of this requirement.



5. It is noted that the Bureau accepts responsibility for Ms Papadopoulos' placement at her substantive (Class 9) level on her return to duty. Accordingly, approval is given to the unattachment of Chrysanthi Papadopoulos on and from the date of commencement of leave without pay."



9. The applicant stated that such conditions were acceptable to her and, in particular, after taking up her employment, she continued to make her contributions to the CSS.



10. The applicant stressed in her evidence that she did not leave her position in Canberra until she had a specific position to go to in Melbourne. This is borne out by evidence that, although her husband had relocated in Melbourne in February 1980, the applicant remained in Canberra until May 1980.



11. After taking up her position in Melbourne, the applicant became pregnant with her second child. Prior to the birth she made inquiries of the Department of Primary Industry as to the options available to her as far as leave was concerned, particularly maternity leave. It appeared that the applicant felt that she would have no leave entitlements from her secondment position in Melbourne but may have some leave entitlements from her permanent employer, the Commonwealth Public Service.



12. The applicant stated that she discussed her situation with the Personnel Officer of the Department of Primary Industries, Ms Cindy Royston, and also with Mr Neville Lamb, the Principal Executive Officer of the BAE, and was advised that she could not avail herself of maternity leave as she was on "leave without pay". A file note, made by Mr Lamb, in the 'T Documents' at page 31 reads as follows:

"Ms Papadopoulos telephoned on 29 June 1981 to discuss several issues relating to her leave without pay and the requirement specified by the Public Service Board that she should resume duty in order for her period of leave without pay to count as service.



Ms Papadopoulos said that she was pregnant and that she had been advised by the Personnel Officer that she could not apply for leave without pay in accordance with maternity leave provisions until she resumed duty with the Commonwealth Public Service. Similarly, she had been advised that she could not obtain payment in lieu of long service leave until such time as she returned to the BAE.



On 30 June I telephoned Ms Papadopoulos and confirmed the advice given to her by the Personnel Officer and advised her of the Bureau's attitude in relation to this matter. I said that I had informed the Executive of her situation and that there would be no objection to her return to the Bureau at a time convenient to her so long as she remained for a reasonable period. Ms Papadopoulos said that she had been appointed by the Victorian Authorities to work on a specific project which was coming to an end (within a fortnight). She said that her only commitment after that would be to remain in Melbourne for about four weeks in case any difficulties arose in relation to the project outcome.



We agreed that a minimum time period would be no less than one month although three months would be desirable. Ms Papadopoulos said that she would consider her position and would probably write to the Director seeking approval to resume duty in mid August. She said she would decide on her course of action regarding her long term employment in the Commonwealth Public Service after resuming duty. I advised that I could see no difficulties."



13. The applicant said that the advice given by Ms Royston and Mr Lamb clearly indicated that she had two options; she could complete her 2 year assignment in Melbourne, or withdraw from that position and return to the BAE as soon as possible.It was necessary for the applicant to return to the BAE, and remain there for a 'reasonable time', in order for her to re-establish her leave entitlements. She stated that the progress with the Melbourne position had reached a point that she could withdraw from it in August 1981 even though the secondment period was not due to end until 26 May 1982. She returned to Canberra, accompanied by her first child, 2 years old, on 21 August 1981. In evidence, the applicant stated that, despite difficulties associated with being the sole caretaker for her 2 year old child, her advanced pregnancy, no motor vehicle and the responsibilities of her home, should would nevertheless "give it a go". The applicant stated that at no time in her discussions with Ms Royston and Mr Lamb was she informed of the option to preserve her superannuation benefits on resignation.



14. In evidence the applicant said that the option of resignation was not considered by her "at this time" and emphasised that her primary intention to stay employed with the Commonwealth. The Tribunal notes that this evidence is inconsistent with that of Mr Lamb. Contained in the section 37 documents is a note, signed by Mr Lamb and dated three days subsequent to his discussions with the applicant, stating that the applicant would decide "on her course of action regarding her long term employment in the Commonwealth Public Service after resuming duty.."



15. However, after returning to Canberra, the applicant told the Tribunal that she experienced great difficulty in maintaining separate households:

"It was not until after I had been back at work that it became clear, both on physical health grounds, I was six-months pregnant, family considerations and financial grounds that I could not sustain full-time work and the care of a child in the final few months of my pregnancy. I was under a lot of stress. My husband was working full time in Melbourne and therefore we were maintaining two households plus we were looking to the expense of weekly visits either by my husband or myself until I was eligible for maternity leave. Even an application for long-service leave at that time would not have been a solution. I would then still have had to return to duty at about the time my baby was born, this was after I had returned and resumed duty. Under these circumstances I made the decision to resign."



16. In light of these difficulties, it was the applicant's belief that the only recourse available to her was to offer her resignation. In her notification of her intention to resign dated 8 September 1981, the applicant referred to "financial considerations" as being the operative factor :

" As financial considerations have been the deciding factor in my resignation, I would appreciate a speedy notification by the Department to the Commissioner for Superannuation regarding the refund of my superannuation contributions. Any monies due to me or any other correspondence may be sent to the address below, after 18.9.81."



  1. The applicant reiterated these sentiments in evidence, and the Tribunal queried whether these words indicated that the applicant had resigned for the purpose of "cashing in" her superannuation and long service leave entitlements to meet the financial circumstances she had described. In reply the applicant said that was not so: " ....

Yet in this statement you say that the financial considerations were the thing that actually decided you to resign"---That's correct. That the costs were starting to exceed the benefits of my coming and working in Canberra.



That's right, so that could be read as meaning that you were simply cashing in your contributions to get some cash flow?---I'm sorry. That is purely an economic evaluation.



That's true."



18. The applicant gave evidence that at the time of her resignation, dated 8 September 1981 to take effect from the close of business on 18 September 1981, she was sent a form headed "APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS/LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS" (form S2A). In evidence the applicant stated that she received the form "with no information, counselling or advice". Her signature was witnessed by a fellow officer at the BAE. She said that she regarded the signing of the form as a "mere formality" to obtain a refund of her superannuation contributions. She stated that the only information she received about the CSS during her period of employment was in a letter dated 1 October 1970 which stated that "all contributions paid to either the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account will be refunded if you should leave the service before reaching retiring age" .



19. The applicant further stated that had she been aware of an option to retain benefits in the CSS then, given her intention to rejoin the Commonwealth Public Service at the first opportunity, then she would have taken that option. "I did not make that election because I did not actually choose between any options. I was not aware that an option was available to me", but "as an econometrician, I would have been quite capable of assessing the benefits to me of staying in the scheme if I was to rejoin the service in one, two, three or any number of years in the future".



20. Finally, the applicant stated that during the period of her employment with the Commonwealth Public Service she had attended several induction processes, and at no time had any induction process included information on the CSS. She said she first became aware of the CSS preservation entitlements in 1989 (that is some 4 years after rejoining the Commonwealth Public Service). On 14 April 1989 the applicant wrote to the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office (AGRBO) stating:

".... I have just recently learnt .... and.... I would like to point out that at no time was I given any advice nor received any information about the rights of permanent public servants to preserve or elect not to preserve superannuation entitlements".



21. In cross-examination the applicant reiterated that she resigned primarily because of the conflict she experienced in maintaining two households and a young family. She agreed that she signed the form S2A and that prima facie she had exercised a choice, that being a request for a refund of her superannuation benefits. The applicant gave evidence however that this action did not amount to a choice as she was not informed of the options available to her. She was of the belief that applying for a refund of contribution was routine. She also agreed that she did not make any inquiry about other superannuation options. A consequence of this belief was that the applicant completed the application form with the belief that the only option available to her was refund of contributions. The applicant stated that the money she received was paid into her savings account. She said it merged with her current finances and was used to pay general expenses.



22. The applicant rejoined the service in March 1985, initially on a consultancy basis and in December 1986 she became a permanent officer in the Department of Primary Industry. The applicant informed the Tribunal that the first time she became aware about the option to preserve her superannuation entitlements upon resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service was in early 1989.



23. In cross-examination the applicant was shown pro forma information sheets that were sent out by the respondent in 1987 and 1988 (Exhibits R3 and R4 respectively) She agreed that she had received these information statements and recognised the front of the sheet but not the back. It was the back that contained information regarding resignation benefits. Although the applicant could not recall reading the back of the page, she indicated that even if she had read it she would not have made inquiries regarding the option to preserve benefits.



24. The Tribunal heard evidence given by Ms Royston and Mr Hooley concerning the procedures and practices of the applicant's employer in handling the resignation of, and the re-employment of, members of the Commonwealth Public Service and particularly members of the CSS.



25. Ms Royston gave evidence that she joined the Commonwealth Public Service in 1968. She moved to the personnel department of the Department of Primary Industry and Energy in 1973 and has been there ever since. At the time of the applicant's resignation in 1981, it was Ms Royston responsibility, as the Personnel Officer of the Department, to review resignation notices before deciding to accept them. This involved contacting intending resignees and counselling them about their resignation to see if there was any other viable option to resigning, and to advise them of their entitlements, if resignation was the final decision. In evidence, Ms Royston said that the counselling advice included information about options in relation to superannuation. In particular, where employees were members of the CSS, AGRBO required them to complete form S2A. Ms Royston stated that she was the officer who accepted the applicant's resignation and her normal practice was to discuss the resignation with the employee before accepting it.



26. However, she could not recall any specific conversation with the applicant at the relevant time, and could only inform the Tribunal of the "normal practice" before accepting a resignation. She further stated that so far as superannuation was concerned, employees were given no advice by her concerning preservation options.



27. Ms Royston said that whilst her knowledge of superannuation "was very good", she would refer most inquiries about superannuation to AGBRO. That is, any counselling about superannuation was carried out by AGBRO and not by her. Ms Royston stated that even though preservation rights were available in 1981, it was a rare thing for resigning employees to preserve their superannuation rights.



28. The Tribunal considered that the evidence of Ms Royston as to her practices and procedures at the relevant time was not convincing. Exhibit A1 is described as "Resignation/age Retirement Action Sheet" for the applicant, and contains some 27 check boxes for actions to be taken to process a resignation. However, it fails to indicate that the important counselling advice described by Ms Royston had in fact been carried out. Ms Royston, in cross-examination, stated that all pre-resignation counselling advice described by her took place before Exhibit A1 came into place, and only after a resignation had been accepted. That is, there is no documentary evidence to support the contention that all of the counselling procedures, as described by the witness, had in fact been carried out.



29. In such an important matter, the Tribunal found the procedure less than satisfactory and less than certain. It appears that whilst Form S2A was sent to the applicant for signing, and then sent on to AGRBO, no specific advice was given to the applicant about the implications of requesting a refund of superannuation entitlements.



30. Mr Bryan Hooley, in evidence before the Tribunal, stated that he commenced employment in the Department of Industry, Technology and Commerce as it was then called in 1982 and since then had been engaged in the personnel area. His duties include induction of new appointees, and general training. The induction procedures included details of superannuation conditions and the provision of appropriate leaflets. He described his personal knowledge of superannuation in 1987 as "fairly comprehensive", including the option to preserve superannuation rights.



31. He stated that when the applicant joined his Department in 1987, the induction procedures were such that, on the balance of probabilities, the applicant would have been required to complete an induction course. He was quite familiar with the leaflets available to new contributors to the CSS at that time and also the Superannuation Manual. He said he was able to handle most superannuation queries, but would refer to AGBRO on occasions.



32. Mr Hooley stated that where a person was rejoining the Commonwealth Public Service, questions concerning past service, such as superannuation, and relevant leave entitlements, would be raised with the new employee. However this would be dependent upon the time interval between an earlier resignation and rejoining.



33. The witness was shown Exhibit A3, the applicant's induction record. This record contained the words; "leave, probation, deduction, salaries cut-off, superannuation, flex time and compensation" and next to these was bracketed the notation "was aware of these". Mr Hooley, who had signed the form, indicated that the above words had been written by him, and conceded that such words could mean that the items were given a fairly cursory treatment only. However, he stated that it was the normal procedure "to go through the various conditions of service in any case". In particular he stated that at the relevant time in 1987 the question of superannuation options such as preservation would have been mentioned in the induction process.



34. The Tribunal also found the evidence of Mr Hooley quite unhelpful. In particular, his evidence about the induction procedures for former employees was somewhat inconsistent with that as given by Ms Royston and his evidence vague as to several questions about which, in the Tribunals view, he ought to have been able to provide precise answers. Ms Royston gave evidence to the Tribunal that former employees who had taken out their accumulated funds (and therefore had no `investment' in the CSS) would not be counselled in any way on re-entry, whereas Mr Hooley gave evidence that there was some counselling depending on the intervening time period.



35. The Tribunal notes with concern the evident lack of uniformity in the administration of important personnel procedures. The Tribunal considers that a minimal evidentiary requirement in all personnel practices is always to have employees sign particular forms stating that all matters contained therein "have been fully explained to and understood by them". That is a procedure known to the Tribunal and practised in the establishment of Workers' Rehabilitation programs and in several procedures in the Civil Aviation Authority.



36. We now turn to consider the evidence, and to decide whether, on the evidence before us, we should exercise the discretion provided in S157(1). The discretion given to the Commissioner under s 157(1) is at large subject to any limitations that may be seen in its terms. Recently, Deputy President McMahon in Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 8553, 26 February 1993) stated the principles to be applied in such cases in which a wide discretion is conferred upon the decision maker. The Tribunal in that instance agreed that the discretion conferred by s 157 was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". The Tribunal, in this instance, is guided by the reasoning of Deputy president Forgie in Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456, in its consideration of the exercise of the discretion. In Liddle it was said that the word "desirable" strongly suggests a positive connotation, that is something "worthy of achievement" rather then "merely advisable". The Tribunal in Lander, was in agreement with this approach and then turned its attention to other matters of principle that should be addressed in cases of this nature and said:

" Firstly, it should be made clear that a person who seeks the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion in his or her favour must satisfy the Commissioner (and this Tribunal) that the discretion should be exercised. It is not for the respondent to establish that the applicant does not have a case for the beneficial exercise of discretion. There is in this Tribunal no formal onus of proof. There is however an evidentiary onus which an applicant must bear if the result of this administrative inquiry is to be to the applicant's satisfaction. The principles discussed in Hickey & Ors v Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 176; 47 ALR 517 are useful guides to appropriate considerations and circumstances such as the present ones.



  1. Council for the respondent paid considerable attention to the principles stated in Lander and drew the Tribunals attention to the legislative intention of the Act, that being that elections must be made within the time limit stipulated. In the case of a superannuation fund preservation election, the time period is relatively short, being 1 month before resignation or 21 days after resignation. In the context of late election cases the Tribunal in Lander was insistent that these time limits are not to be ignored: "One can not ignore these time limits. They are in the statute. If they are to be bypassed regularly to give effect to the benign intentions of the legislature, then in our view legislative amendment to the terms of s 157(1) would be necessary to spell this out. What s 157(1) does is to give an applicant a right which is tantamount to a right to apply for an extension of time. In considering whether an extension should be granted, the principles considered by the courts when dealing with applications under the Defence Forces Death and Retirement Benefits Act and the Administrative Decision (Judicial Review) Act are useful. "

38. The principles regarding an application for extension of time have been considered and summarised by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Limited & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at pages 319-320. These principles have been applied by this Tribunal in a number of contexts, and in this instance, we do not propose to repeat the passage in Wilcox J's judgement. However, the Tribunal will consider the evidence before it having regard to those principles in determining how to exercise its discretionary power, and will consider whether the applicant has shown an accaeptable explanation for delay and that it is fair and equitable in the circumstances to extend time. Action taken by the applicant, prejudice to the respondent, and considerations of fairness as between the applicant and other persons in like positions are all relevant to the consideration of the exercise of the discretion. The Tribunal is also conversant with other decisions which touch directly on the exercise of the discretion under section 157. The more recent of those cases are; Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 8609 19 March 1993), and Morgan and Commissioner for Superannuation ( No. 8584 12 March 1993).



39. Wilcox J's first principle is that the prescribed time limit is not to be ignored. The fact that 21 days is prescribed as an appropriate time within which to make an election indicates that the applicant bears the burden of satisfying the Tribunal that it is desirable to exercise the discretion conferred by s 157. The applicant raised with the Tribunal that the respondent bears an evidentiary burden also. The applicant paid considerable attention to the fact that the Commonwealth Superannuation scheme is a compulsory scheme and therefore the respondent was under a duty to inform the applicant of the options concerning her superannuation entitlements, and that on the evidence presented, the respondent did not satisfy this requirement.



40. The Tribunal, whilst sympathetic to the applicant's submission, is mindful of the authorities that state in no uncertain terms that it is the prima facie rule that proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be considered, unless the applicant for an extension can show an acceptable explanation for delay and that it is " fair and equitable in the circumstances" to extend time. See Wilcox J in Hunter Valley, and Deputy President McMahon in Lander.



41. It is the Tribunals view that the applicant's explanation for delay is premised on 3 factors:

(1) She was not counselled or fully advised of her superannuation rights at the time of her resignation in 1981 or at the time of her re-employment in 1986.

(2) that form S2A was misleading; and

(3) she did not become aware of the principle of preserving rights in a superannuation scheme until 1989.



42. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant was not fully appraised of her rights to preserve superannuation benefits at the time of her resignation. On the evidence before us, it is apparent that the applicant sought information regarding her resignation from personnel officers, however no mention was made of an attempt to inquire from the office of the Commissioner. In this regard the Tribunal is of the belief that the applicant had ample opportunities to make further inquiries. The applicant is an educated person, a graduate in economics with honours, substantial work experience of a highly skilled nature, fully aware of financial affairs, and a person without apparent health or language problems, and therefore capable of finding out the financial implications of her resignation. In the matter of superannuation in particular, the Tribunal does not accept that a senior officer who has contributed to the CSS over a long period was not fully aware of all the rights attached to her contributions.



43. In considering the argument that the form S2A is misleading, the Tribunal shares the views expressed in Secourable and Morgan that the form is ambiguous and badly designed. However the Tribunal reiterates that the applicant is a highly educated officer and, on this basis alone, the shortcomings of the form does not constitute a sufficient explanation for delay. On the evidence, the Tribunal is of the view that the mere formality of completing the S2A form should have been sufficient to put the applicant on notice that other options exist. The form S2A, completed by the applicant, rejects the election for preservation of rights and requests, as does her letter of resignation, a refund of accumulated contributions. The Commissioner is entitled, in the Tribunals view, to accept these documents as reflecting the applicants intentions, and therefore the delay between the applicant ceasing to be an eligible employee and requesting a late election will occasion material prejudice to the respondent.



44. It is a pre-condition to the exercise of the discretion conferred under s 157 that the applicant show an "acceptable explanation of the delay", and this precondition extends to aspects of fairness and equity. The delay in the applicant's case is a considerable one. A period of 8 years ensued between her ceasing to be an eligible employee, her request for a refund of contributions and her application for a late election. The Tribunal is of the belief that the applicant is a person who, having the ability to command good advice, failed to take the steps necessary to obtain that advice in relation to her options upon resignation, and by virtue of her inaction the Commisioner was allowed to believe that the matter was finally concluded, and for these reasons the Tribunal does not consider that an acceptable explanation has been put forward such as to satisfy the Tribunal that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed.



45. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
