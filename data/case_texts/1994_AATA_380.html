Re Taxation Appeals [1994] AATA 380; (1994) 95 ATC 112 (1994) 30 ATR 1030 (21 December 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT93/93-99

AAT No. 9937

Number of pages - 12

Taxation

(1994) 95 ATC 112

[1994] AATA 695; (1994) 30 ATR 1030

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. MCMAHON

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - review - whether sum is assessable as a dividend - characterisation of amount - bonus issue of shares - whether assessable - characterisation of right under conditional contract - whether purpose of purchase was resale - dominant purpose test - interest - whether part of applicant's income - onus of proof - positive onus on applicant

HEARING

SYDNEY, 12-13 December 1994

21:12:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Carnovale with Mr McMillan

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Bloom QC with Mr Edmonds

Solicitors for the Applicant: Gould Ralph Services Pty Ltd

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decisions under review

DECISION

B.J. MCMAHON This is an application to review objection decisions in relation to assessments raised under ss 166 and 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") following an audit of a group of companies. Included in these assessments were those in respect of the applicant (a company which acts as trustee of a family trust) for the years ended 30 June 1983 to 1989 inclusive, pursuant to s 99A. In relation to each year, assessments covered various amounts arising out of different subject matters. By the time these applications came on for hearing, there were only 3 issues remaining for determination. These will be dealt with separately.

2. Oral evidence was given by only one person, whom I will call the Proprietor. Effectively, he controlled the applicant and all other companies referred to in these reasons except the 2 referred to in the next section. Although other witnesses who could have assisted were alive and in Australia, they were not called. In some cases this has allowed me to draw inferences with a greater degree of comfort than I would otherwise have experienced.

CAPITAL PROFITS RESERVE $397,387

3. The balance sheet of the applicant at 30 June 1985 carried a balance of $397,387 in a capital profits reserve account shown as having arisen during that year of income. No particulars were supplied with the return of income. In the 1988 return of income, there is a statement in relation to the capital profits reserve, connecting it with a building in which the applicant had never had an interest. Consequently, this was not accepted as a satisfactory explanation of the origin of the reserve. During the conduct of the audit, the tax agent was asked to furnish a further explanation. By letter dated 23 April 1991 (exhibit 7) the agent said : "At this stage we are unable to determine precisely the nature of the disposal of the asset that gave rise to the creation of this capital profits reserve".

4. In the absence of a satisfactory reply to the queries issued in relation to this issue, the respondent decided that the adjustment made to the assessment to treat the amount of $397,387 as a dividend assessable under paragraph 44(1)(a) of he Act should be maintained. This assessment was arrived at after a consideration of the facts to which I will now turn.

5. Evidence given at the hearing focused on the activities in mid 1984 of 2 companies, which I will call Management and Holdings. At all relevant times, both companies were owned as to one half by the applicant and as to the other half by G, or companies or persons associated with him. Holdings owned various units in high rise apartment blocks on the Gold Coast and Management had contracts for managing the apartment blocks in which the Holdings apartments were located.

6. In mid 1994, the Proprietor decided that the applicant should sell its 50 per cent shareholdings in the 2 companies to G and his associated interests. In order to finance the purchase, G arranged for the incorporation of a joint venture company, owned as to 50 per cent by his interests and as to the remaining 50 per cent by a public finance company. At the request of the finance company, it was agreed that the assets of Holdings and Management should be sold to the joint venture company. It was also arranged that the applicant should sell its shares in Management and Holdings. In evidence, the Proprietor asserted that the arrangement involved nothing more than the sale of the shares, although the price of those shares was determined by reference to the price paid for the value of the assets of the 2 companies.

7. Because the applicant did not have a bank account, moneys arising from the transaction were in fact banked into the account of another company in the group, which I will call Developments. Those moneys came directly from the joint venture company, pursuant to a direction given on settlement by the G interests (exhibit 5). That document recited that those giving the direction were all of the shareholders of the companies other than the applicant. They disclaimed all their right title and interest in and to the consideration up to $400,000 payable to the company for its assets, and authorised and directed the joint venture company to pay the stipulated sum to the vendor. That sum, less legal costs, constitutes the reserve in question. The respondent took the view that this amounted to a dividend as defined in s 6(1), as it was a distribution made by a company to one of its shareholders of money representing the proceeds of sale of assets of the company. Although the sale of assets and the transfer of shares took place at approximately the same time, the applicant was still a shareholder at the time the direction was acted upon.

8. The accounts for both companies for the year ended 30 June 1985 attached to their returns of income do not show that a dividend had been paid and the evidence of the Proprietor was that he had had no discussion with his co-owner concerning any such payment. Schedule 29 to both returns, however, confirm that on 16 July 1984 (5 days after the date of the direction), the 2 shares held by the applicant in each company were transferred to G or his nominees for the sum of $1 and notes that the transfer was due to a resignation of the Proprietor as a director of the companies.

9. The entries in the body of the balance sheets (upon which counsel for the applicant largely depended) are unreliable. Following the filing of the accounts, the accountants for Holdings and Management wrote letters of correction to the respondent. The tax agent's certificates endorsed on the returns were dated 25 February 1986. On 9 June 1987 the accountants wrote in relation to Management, pointing out that an error had been made in that sale proceeds of management rights were erroneously disclosed as $343,150 instead of $693,150. In the case of Holdings, it was alleged in another letter that another error was made in the accounts in that management rights shown as having been written off resulting in an extraordinary loss of $75,000 were in fact sold for $200,000 and that a profit of $125,000 should be recorded instead of a loss of $75,000. No amended accounts were filed as it was alleged that there were no income tax consequences flowing from these adjustments. The result, however, is to leave the impression that the accounts can not be relied upon as accurate records of the company's affairs for the year in question. Accordingly, the fact that no dividend, or loan equivalent, appears in the accounts does little to assist in identifying the true nature of the transactions the subject of the assessments.

10. It was suggested by counsel for the applicant that moneys from the sale of the company's assets could have been loaned by the joint venture company to the G interests, to enable them to purchase the applicant's shares. Such a transaction, of course, would have been illegal. Nevertheless there is no evidence upon which to build on that speculation. Certainly there is not sufficient evidence to discharge the applicant's onus of proof that the amounts in question were not dividends as defined.

11. No evidence was given by G or his wife, or by any other person with whom he was associated. There is no reason to suppose that they were not available if they had been required.

12. Three explanations were given by the tax agent for the existence of the capital profit reserve, 2 of which have already been referred to. On 13 August 1993 (exhibit 8) the accountants offered a third explanation, some 8 years after the event. They alleged that a general ledger printout from the now obsolete computer system on which the applicant's accounting records were then maintained showed that the capital profit reserve arose through debiting by way of inter company loan, the funds of Developments. Whilst conceding that this "appears to be unorthodox accounting" the tax agent did not offer any further explanation for the creation of the account.

13. I do not accept any of these 3 "explanations" as sufficient to discharge the applicant's burden of proof. As the Commissioner may not pluck a figure out of the air or make an uninformed guess in arriving at an assessment, so also a taxpayer may not succeed merely by suggesting other possible, but unsubstantiated, explanations for specific elements of the assessment. I will return to the question of burden of proof later in these reasons.

BONUS SHARE ISSUE

14. During the year ended 30 June 1986, a bonus issue of 4 million fully paid 25c par value shares in a public company which I will call Group Limited was declared and satisfied out of an asset revaluation reserve, following a director's revaluation of interests under contracts, which were described in the s 37 reasons as "property options". In essence, the directors of Group Limited on 30 December 1985 revalued their company's interest in 2 conditional property purchase contracts from $95,000 to $1,095,000. The contracts were in respect of 2 sites intended for major real estate development. The recipient shareholder of the bonus issue was the applicant. The value of the shares, namely $1million, was considered by the respondent to be assessable under the former paragraph 44(1)(a) of the Act. At the time, s 44(2) excluded from the category of assessable dividends those that were paid wholly and exclusively out of profits arising from the sale or revaluation of assets not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit. It was the applicant's case that the bonus issue was exempt from tax under this provision.

15. In his evidence the Proprietor said -

"In early 1985 I was looking for properties suitable for

development into a medium scale shopping centre and office

complex of high quality. I intended to establish a public

company which would buy under developed land, develop it by

building high quality shopping centres and office complexes,

hold them as long term investments and earn rent from the

leasing of the shops and offices. I intended to have the

company listed on the stock exchange. I intended that (the

applicant) would own most of the shares in this company and

that the rest would be taken up by the public. I decided

that (Group Limited) would carry out this project."

16. The public company negotiated in respect of 2 adjacent sites. A contract was entered into on 28 June 1985 in respect of the first site. It provided in clause 15 -

"Liability of vendor. The property hereby sold shall be at

the risk of the vendor from the date hereof until the contract

becomes unconditional and thereafter shall be at the risk of

the purchaser and the vendor whilst continuing in possession

will use the same with reasonable care."

17. The condition referred to related principally to a proposal to obtain development consent from the local council "for the erection of a commercial and/or residential and/or retail complex on the subject land". A date was set out within which that condition was to be satisfied. There were obligations on both the purchaser and vendor to pursue the application. If the condition had not been satisfied or waived within the prescribed period, the contract was to be at an end and "thereupon neither party shall have any further claim or action against the other on the contract". The second contract for the adjoining site was made with the council itself as vendor. It simply provided under the heading "liability of purchaser" : "The property hereby sold shall be at the risk of the purchaser from the date hereof and the vendor whilst continuing in possession will use the same with reasonable care." It too was subject to a special condition that suitable development approval be obtained within a stipulated time.

18. The revaluation resolution was not available. Indeed no minutes of meetings of directors or shareholders of the applicant, or of Group or of any of the other companies whose affairs have been the subject of this audit were made available at the hearing. Nevertheless counsel for the applicant submitted that the only asset that was capable of revaluation was Group's interest in the 2 contracts. It was submitted that these interests were not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit because clearly in their own terms they were acquired for the purpose of obtaining development consent and thereafter facilitating the development of the site. It was submitted that there must be a legal identity between what is acquired and what is sold before the exclusionary provisions of sub-section 44(2) had effect. In fact the contract was subsequently completed. The development took place and the property was sold 5 years after its acquisition and some 3 years after the development had been completed.

19. It was the applicant's primary submission that at the time of the revaluation it had no interest in the property which was subsequently sold. In support of this submission, it relied upon 3 High Court cases, McWilliam v McWilliam Wines Pty Limited and Others [1964] HCA 6; 114 CLR 656, Brown v Heffer [1967] HCA 40; 116 CLR 344 and KLDE Pty Limited (In Voluntary Liquidation) v Commissioner of Stamp Duties [1984] HCA 63; 155 CLR 288. These cases were cited as authority for the proposition that in the case of a conditional contract, the applicant had no more than contractual rights against the vendor. In McWilliam at 660, McTiernan J and Taylor J said -

"The question, therefore, must be taken to be confined to a

consideration of the rights, if any, to which the respondent

company became entitled by virtue of the contract. Under a

contract for the sale of land unaffected by statutory

provisions such as those to be found in the Crown Lands

Consolidation Act the vendor assumes an obligation, subject

to the terms of the contract, to execute a conveyance of the

land sold to the purchaser or as he may direct. Further,

where the purchase money specified in the contract has been

paid the purchaser becomes entitled in equity to the land

and the vendor thereafter becomes a bare trustee. But the

contract in this case was subject to an implied condition

that the sale of the farm land in question was subject to

the consent of the Commission being obtained (Egan v. Ross

(1928) 29 SR (N.S.W.) 382; 46 WN 90 and Butts v. O'Dwyer

[1952] HCA 74; (1952) 87 CLR 267) and, that being so, it could not be contended

that the respondent company became entitled by force of the

contract to an equitable interest in the land. Nevertheless,

upon the authority of the last two mentioned cases, the contract

imposed upon the vendor an obligation to do all such things as

might be necessary to obtain the consent of the Minister..."

20. This was followed in Brown at 349 -

"The argument submitted for the appellant in the present appeal

relied upon cases such as Egan v. Ross (1928) 29 SR (N.S.W.) 382

for the proposition that under such contracts as those with

which we are here concerned specific performance will be granted

in favour of the purchaser even before the Minister's consent has

been obtained, and that therefore the land is notionally converted

into money at the date of the contract notwithstanding that unless

the consent be given no effectual conveyance or transfer under

the contract can ever be made. The first part of the proposition,

however, is too widely stated and the second cannot be maintained.

The specific performance which will be granted before the

Minister's consent has been obtained is not specific performance

of the obligation to convey or transfer, for that obligation has

not yet arisen : McWilliam v. McWilliam Wines Pty. Ltd. (1964)

[1964] HCA 6; 114 CLR 656, at p 661. As Harvey CJ in Eq. made clear in Egan v

Ross (1928) 29 SR (N.S.W.) 382, at p 388 the decree that will

be made will go no further than directing that the proper steps

be taken for the purpose of obtaining the Minister's consent and,

"if that is obtained", to transfer the land to the purchaser : see

also the more explicit form of order made by this Court in Kennedy

v. Vercoe (1960) 105 ALR 521, at pp 530, 531. Accordingly

until the consent has been obtained the purchaser's interest,

being "commensurate only with what would be decreed to him",

does not extend to ownership of the land and the interest of

the vendor is not yet converted into a right to receive money in

place of the land. Many authorities on the topic are discussed

in the valuable judgment of Callan J. in In re Rudge; Curtain v

Rudge (1949) NZLR 752." .

21. Both cases were discussed by a Full Bench of the High Court in Stern v McArthur [1988] HCA 51; 165 CLR 489. Whilst it is true that neither McWilliam nor Brown were overruled in this later decision, the court (particularly Deane and Dawson JJ) took a different view of the nature of the rights acquired under a conditional contract. The conditions applicable under Stern were not identical with those considered either in McWilliam or Brown, or for that matter the conditions set out in the contracts in the present application. Nevertheless, it seems to me that their Honours made it clear that an interest under contracts of that general conditional nature conferred a right in rem. The purchaser acquires an equitable interest in the land sold and to that extent, the beneficial interest of the vendor in the land is diminished. The extent of the purchaser's interest is to be measured by the protection which equity will afford to the purchaser (Stern at 522).

22. In the present case the "substance of the matter" (Stern at 524) is that Group had acquired an interest in the 2 properties which would have been protected by equity, and which gave it proprietary rights defensible against third parties who had notice, for example, through a caveat. That interest was intended to be, and in fact subsequently was, enlarged to the whole of the fee simple upon completion of the purchase. To determine whether the conditional rights were not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit, it is therefore necessary to look at the purpose of acquisition in relation to total ownership of the real estate.

23. The applicant's alternative submission was that if it should be found it had a right in rem, the evidence did not disclose that at the time it acquired the right there was a purpose of resale of the real estate at a profit. Both parties agreed that the tests to be applied in relation to s 44(2) were identical with those that have been considered on many occasions in relation to the former s 26(a).

24. The applicant submitted that one should consider only the "dominant purpose". It has been held on many occasions that the first part of the former s 26(a) was satisfied if profit making by sale was the dominant purpose which actuated the taxpayer in the acquisition of the property, even if it was not the sole purpose (Moruben Gardens Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4147 at 4151). It was further submitted that if the dominant purpose was for long term investment at the time the rights were acquired, that purpose was not displaced by a later sale (Eisner v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4022). One should, to use counsel's words, "look at the purpose which floats to the top".

25. I accept that these are the correct tests to be applied. Nevertheless the evidence before me by no means discharges the applicant's onus to show affirmatively that the dominant purpose of Group was not to resell the development at a profit.

26. Evidence was given by the Proprietor that his was the directing mind in Group and indeed in all other companies whose affairs have been the subject of the audit. Nevertheless no other director of Group (a public company) was called to give evidence of purpose. Against the Proprietor's bare assertions that Group had the required investment purpose, there are a number of pieces of evidence pointing to the contrary.

27. Evidence was given of the trading activities of a number of companies in which the Proprietor was also the directing mind. Of 53 transactions listed in a schedule concerning the affairs of a number of companies, 34 referred to purchases, developments and sales of properties within 3 years. All of the 53 properties were sold within 5 years. It is permissible, in forming a view as to purpose, to take account of the activities of these other companies (Cole v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4792) even though, as counsel for the applicant pointed out, most of the other companies were private companies as distinct from Group, which had an element of public ownership. The pattern disclosed by this evidence indicates a mode of activity of companies in which the applicant was the directing mind, including the activity of Group. It was unfailing custom (with one exception) to acquire property, to improve it by further development and to sell it.

28. Evidence of purpose can be gleaned with reference to all of the evidence including evidence of later statements. In the Prospectus issued when Group was originally floated shortly after the revaluation the directors advised (page 6) that upon successful completion of the development, they intended to seek further funds from shareholders "to finance the company's further expansion". As to the future of the company, (page 7) the directors reported their belief "that there is growth potential in both Queensland and Australia (sic) generally over the next 5 to 10 years for a specialised high quality developer, that produces projects of originality and distinctive design which appeal to the more discriminating purchaser". This clearly indicates a plan on the part of the directors to sell the company's completed projects in the future to discriminating purchasers. There is no reason to doubt that this intention also underlay the subject development.

29. Although in its 1988 annual return, Group referred (page 9) to the subject development as being held as part of the company's long term investment plan, it was in fact sold shortly thereafter. In a letter (exhibit 11) dated some time in March or April 1987 the Proprietor, as chairman of Group, wrote to shareholders in conjunction with a proposed rights issue. In relation to the subject property, the letter indicated that "the directors anticipate profit of approximately $7.5million from this development". Although counsel for the applicant submitted that this was a reference to profits achieved by way of an increase in value, this seems to me an unrealistic way of construing the words. An ordinary shareholder would understand profit to mean a profit achieved by way of sale and realisation.

30. Feasibility studies (exhibit 10) prepared by Group for the information of financiers of several projects, including the subject development, all refer to a profit that could be anticipated from sale of the developments and offer calculations of the extent of that anticipated profit to the financiers. The evidence of the Proprietor also was that at all relevant times Group was highly geared. From this, one could infer commercial pressures to sell each development.

31. Having regard to these facts I have concluded that the applicant has not discharged its onus of proving affirmatively that the property was not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit.

INTEREST ON LOAN OF $2.8 MILLION

32. In 1986 it was proposed that Group acquire the entire issued capital of a company I will call Subsidiary in consideration of a payment of $4,800,000 to the applicant, which then owned Subsidiary. Once again, the minutes of meetings of directors or shareholders of Group were not made available at the hearing. The text of the resolution passed at the Extraordinary General Meeting of shareholders intended to be held on 18 September 1986 is assumed to be the same as the text set out in the Notice of Meeting.

33. That proposed resolution provided that part of the total acquisition price of $2million was to be satisfied by an allotment of shares in Group to the applicant. The balance of $2,800,000 was to be provided to the applicant by "companies associated with (the Proprietor) for a period of not less than 3 years at 14 per cent per annum".

34. What followed is not clear. Although the resolution referred to the $2.8million as intended to be provided by a company associated with the Proprietor, the balance sheet of the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1988 indicates that it was the applicant itself that lent the money. Why the applicant, which was owed $2.8million by Group after the sale, should also advance that money to Group remains unanswered. The directors' report of Group for the year ended 30 June 1987 confirms that the Extraordinary General Meeting took place and reports the acquisition of Subsidiary for the consideration outlined above. It does not identify the source of the loan, but does confirm that a loan was made for 3 years at an interest rate of 14 per cent per annum.

35. No deduction was claimed for the interest by Group but deductions were claimed by Subsidiary in 1987, 1988 and 1989 for amounts that would correspond to interest payable at the rate stated. That interest was debited to Subsidiary apparently because it had sufficient rental income to cover the outgoing, although when properly analysed, it would appear that Subsidiary, by doing so, would have been financing the purchase of its own shares. Although interest was claimed as a deduction by Subsidiary in each of the years in question, it has not been shown in accounts as income by any of the companies in the group, including the applicant. In the absence of any satisfactory explanation, the respondent included the payments of interest as assessable income of the applicant, on the basis that the loan was shown as an asset of the applicant and on the basis that in that event one would expect that the applicant would be the ultimate recipient of any such interest.

36. Counsel for the applicant submitted firstly that the interest should not be assessable in the applicant's hands as it had not been shown that it had received the moneys and that interest was not assessable until received. In support of this proposition, he relied upon the cases cited by Dr JP Hannan in "Principles of Income Taxation" (1946). Whether or not such old authorities are still relevant, the fact is that the applicant could not have received any interest in money as it had no bank account. There is no reason to suppose however that it was incapable of deriving income through interest debited to a payer and credited to it in some other accounts.

37. There is no evidence to support the suggestion made by counsel for the applicant, who endeavoured to make sense of the various entries in the accounts, that in fact the interest was credited to Developments. The basis upon which he reasoned was purely speculative.

38. The established facts are that the shares in Subsidiary were sold for $4.8million by the applicant to Group and that $2million was satisfied by the issue of shares in Group. The balance of $2.8million was left outstanding. The resolution indicated that this was to be supplied by way of loan from a company associated with the Proprietor. The applicant is a company associated with the Proprietor. The sum of $2.8million has not been repaid. In 1988 it was shown as an asset in the applicant's balance sheet. Interest was expensed to Subsidiary but its receipt by any other company has not been clearly established. There is a complete absence of evidence that Developments was the lender. The item of interest shown as income in its return does not, in any event, correspond with interest which would have been payable on a loan of $2.8million. Whilst it is true that there is no evidence that the applicant received the interest, there is no direct evidence to the contrary except from the Proprietor. What evidence has been put before me is not sufficient, in my view, to displace the onus which the applicant has of proving affirmatively that the inclusion of interest in the applicant's assessment under the circumstances resulted in an excessive assessment.

ONUS OF PROOF

39. The former s 190(b) cast on the taxpayer the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive. In all material respects, the later s 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act is in the same terms. The task of the applicant is to show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by the present Notices of Assessment exceeds its actual substantive liability. It does not prove its taxable income was less than the amounts shown in the assessment merely by showing that in some respects the Commissioner erred in the way in which he attributed income to the taxpayer, or otherwise dealt with the material available to him (Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; 168 CLR 614).

40. The applicant must prove affirmatively that the assessments are excessive. In McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; 143 CLR 284 at 303, Gibbs J considered the nature of the onus of proof in relation to a s 26(a) case. His observations are of general application in all matters where the applicant carries the burden of proof. The earlier view of Barwick CJ in Gauci v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 135 CLR at 87 was disapproved and the dissenting judgment of Mason J in that case was affirmed. This view has since been followed in other cases (for example in Allard v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 92 ATC 4897 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Australia and New Zealand Savings Bank Limited 94 ATC 4844 at 4849) and illustrates the nature of the burden which the taxpayer applicant must discharge -

"...The burden may be discharged by drawing inferences from

the evidence. In some cases in which all the relevant facts

are known, and there is no material upon which it might

properly be concluded that the property was acquired for the

relevant purpose, the inference may properly be drawn that the

property was not acquired for the relevant purpose. But it is

not enough, even when all the facts are known, that there is no

material upon which it may be concluded that the property was

acquired for the purpose mentioned in s. 26(a). If a taxpayer

can succeed, simply because there is no evidence from which it

can be concluded that the relevant purpose existed, that must

mean that the burden of proving the existence of that purpose

lies on the Commissioner. That in my respectful opinion would

be to invert the onus of proof. The taxpayer will succeed if

the proper inference from the evidence is that the property was

not acquired for the relevant purpose, but if there is no

evidence as to the purpose for which the taxpayer acquired the

property the appeal must fail.

I would respectfully agree with the statement of Mason J in his

dissenting judgement in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81 at pp 89-90:

"The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show

that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any

statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained

or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement

would be inconsistent with s. 190(b) for it is a consequence of

that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that

the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.... The crux of the

matter is that when in a s. 26(a) case an appellant seeks to

overcome the onus created by s. 190(b) by adducing evidence as

to his intentions with a view to establishing the purpose of the

acquisition was not a s. 26(a) purpose and that evidence is not

accepted, he has not discharged the onus which he bears. At best,

from the appellant's viewpoint, the evidence stands in a

situation in which it is equivocal, neither establishing a s.

26(a) purpose nor denying the existence of such a purpose. At

worst, the judge may, in the circumstances, be able to infer

the existence of a s. 26(a) purpose. In either event the

appellant fails to discharge the onus and the appeal fails."

41. In all 3 of the elements of the assessments considered in these reasons, the applicant has failed to discharge its onus. No evidence was offered on the question whether, and if so how much, additional taxation should have been imposed by these assessments. For reasons which I have canvassed, the decisions under review are therefore affirmed.
