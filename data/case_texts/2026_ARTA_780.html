Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

  1. Mr Palmer (the Applicant) applied to the Tribunal on 29 January 2024 for review of an objection decision issued by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) on 23 January 2024 (the Objection Decision).
  2. The Objection Decision relates to ‘default assessments,’ being assessments issued under section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA 1936) (the Default Assessments) and assessments of administrative penalties (the Penalty Assessments) issued to the Applicant in relation to the income years ended 30 June 2008 to 30 June 2016 (the Relevant Years). The Respondent has stated that the Applicant did not lodge an income tax return in any of the Relevant Years. The Respondent has also identified amounts which it says are income of the Applicant in each of the Relevant Years. The Default Assessments reflect the Respondent’s contentions.

Factual background and chronology of events

  1. The Respondent began an audit of the Applicant in July 2017. As part of this audit the Respondent issued notices under Division 353 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 1953) to various financial institutions and PayPal, seeking information and documents about the Applicant, two companies, a superannuation fund and two foundations.
  2. Following receipt of this material the Respondent identified certain accounts with banks and PayPal which it said were held by the Applicant (alone or with other individuals) or entities that it believed were associated with the Applicant, or in relation to which the Applicant had been identified as an account signatory. In the Default Assessments the Respondent included as assessable income amounts (or a portion of amounts) received into those accounts during the Relevant Years, describing those amounts as ‘unexplained deposits.’ The Respondent said that the total of these assessable amounts across the Relevant Years was $1,711,506.
  3. More particularly, the Respondent identified multiple PayPal accounts and bank accounts[1] which it said had some relevance to the determination of the Applicant’s taxable income in the Relevant Years. As will become apparent, a central area of dispute between the parties is what might be described generally as the relationship of the Applicant to the accounts and his entitlement to any amounts in those accounts. The Applicant submitted to the Tribunal that he was not entitled to amounts in those accounts, or at least should not be treated as entitled to the entirety of amounts where there were other signatories to the accounts or the account holders were companies or foundations. The following accounts and entities were identified for the purposes of the audit position, Objection Decision and submissions filed with the Tribunal:
    (a) In relation to accounts with financial institutions:[2]
    (i) Two accounts with Westpac in the name of the Inspiring Images Foundation, one account number ending in ‘3526’ and the other in ‘2534’.

    (ii) Two accounts with Westpac for the Know Your Rights Foundation, one account number ending in ‘9684’ and the other in ‘9692’.

    (iii) Three accounts, two with Westpac and one with Bendigo Bank for the Golden Tara Foundation, with account numbers ending in ‘8962’, ‘3477’ and ‘3485’.[3]

    (iv) Two accounts with Westpac in the name of BSR Foundation, with account numbers ending ‘0084’ and ‘0092’.[4]

    (v) One account with National Australia Bank (NAB) for ‘Mike Palmer trading as Success Coaching International’.

    (vi) One account with NAB for Libra Consulting Services Pty Ltd (Libra Consulting).

    (vii) One account with NAB for Virgin Investments Pty Ltd (Virgin Investments).

    (viii) One account with Bendigo Bank for Freedom Foundation.

    (ix) Four further accounts with Westpac in the Applicant’s name.[5]

    (b) In relation to accounts with PayPal:[6]

    (ii) Two accounts in the name of the BSR Foundation, one account number ending in ‘8019’ and the other ‘6489’.[7]

    (iii) One account in the name of Libra Consulting trading as Aussie Speeding Fines.

    (iv) A further account in the name of Aussie Speeding Fines.

  4. In this decision for ease of reading the Tribunal will refer to the various account holders collectively as the ‘Entities’ although the legal status (or even existence) of some of the account holders is a matter of contention between the parties and in some instances was unclear to the Tribunal.[8]
  5. Between July 2017 and December 2017 the Respondent issued the notices under Division 353 to the four banks and PayPal, and the banks and PayPal provided material including copies of account statements, summaries of account holder information and copies of documents provided when accounts were opened.[9] Specific documents are referred to in more detail below in relation to particular Entities or accounts.
  6. The Respondent wrote to the Applicant on 5 July 2017 and 25 July 2017 asking to have a telephone discussion with him to confirm his lodgement status for the Relevant Years.[10] A letter was sent to the Respondent marked ‘Evidence A’ from ‘MP in being – the Person’s Personal Representative of all derivations of the person MICHAEL GARETH PALMER’ which stated that the Respondent’s correspondence of 5 July 2017[11] had been ‘considered’ and the writer was not aware of any lawful requirement to assist the ‘purported Australian Taxation Office’ with its record keeping or ‘perform any other task that we have with the non-legal entity known as the Australian Taxation Office or ATO.’ An officer of the Respondent then sent a further email to the Applicant[12] asking him to confirm his contact details so that the Respondent could discuss with the Applicant ‘what is happening.’ A response to that email was sent by ‘Mike’ denying that he had a residential address or postal address and requesting the correspondence of July 2017 be sent to him again because he had not received it.[13] There was further email correspondence from late November 2017 to December 2017, with the Respondent requesting to speak with the Applicant and these requests being rebuffed on various bases.
  7. The Respondent issued audit reasons for decision on 19 February 2018,[14] advising that the Default Assessments[15] had been raised.
  8. The Penalty Assessments[16] were issued in relation to amounts arising under the Default Assessments[17] on the basis that ‘[the Applicant] failed to provide a document as required.’ This reflected the audit reasons for decision where the Respondent said that it was imposing a penalty on the basis of the Applicant having failed to lodge an income tax return in any the Relevant Years. The amount of the penalties was increased by 20% in each of the Relevant Years other than the first Relevant Year (being the 2008 income year).[18]
  9. The Applicant lodged an objection dated 10 November 2023[19] (the Objection) against the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments. In his Objection the Applicant stated that the Default Assessments were excessive, not made on reasonable grounds and were unfair. The Applicant stated that the administrative penalty, including the application and non-remission of uplift factors, should not have been imposed.[20]
  10. On 8 December 2023 the Respondent wrote to the Applicant seeking additional information, including details of other parties who were joint account holders of identified accounts or office holders in the named companies and foundations, details of any deductions the Applicant sought to claim and any other information or supporting evidence that might assist resolving the objection.[21]
  11. The Applicant replied by email to the Respondent on 10 January 2024 advising that he did not intend to provide further information. He referred to his Objection as lodged and said that he had already provided more than enough information or evidence in his Objection.[22]
  12. The Respondent finalised and issued its Objection Decision on 23 January 2024. The Respondent allowed the Applicant’s Objection in part for the 2015 and 2016 income years but disallowed his Objection in full in relation to the remaining years.

He said that:
    (a) The PayPal accounts belonged to multiple people and the Respondent had not made any effort to determine how much of those funds were actually transferred to him.

    (b) Funds from a separate company were also incorrectly attributed as his personal income.

    (c) The Respondent had included the same amounts twice as his income, being funds from PayPal accounts that were then transferred into bank accounts.

    (d) The Respondent had not made attempted to factor any reasonable expenses into the amounts they attributed to him as income. The Applicant said that it was simply not possible to earn the amounts the Respondent said he had earned as income without his having incurred some expenses.

    (e) These incorrect figures had then been adopted by another government agency for a different purpose, which resulted in a Departure Prohibition Order being made against him.[23]

  2. The Applicant filed four Statements of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFICs) as well as amended or updated versions of his SFICs with the Tribunal between October 2024 and July 2025. The content of these are discussed in greater detail in the Tribunal’s interlocutory decision of 20 October 2025.[24] The Tribunal refused to grant the Applicant leave to expand his grounds of review to cover numerous additional contentions and issues set out in those SFICS, some of which had been described by the Tribunal in a previous interlocutory decision as ‘unorthodox.’[25] As the Applicant did not actively pursue these grounds at the final hearing the Tribunal does not believe it is necessary to address these matters further.[26]
  3. The Applicant had previously made applications to the Tribunal seeking the issue of a summons to the Respondent to produce certain documents, seeking the attendance of the Commissioner of Taxation and/or various taxation officers at the substantive hearing of the proceeding, seeking orders for the provision of documents by the Respondent to the Tribunal (and Applicant) under section 26 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (the ART Act) and seeking leave to rely on additional grounds before the Tribunal to those contained in the Applicant’s objection dated 10 November 2023 (the Objection). These applications, which were opposed by the Respondent, were the subject of two separate interlocutory hearings and were dismissed by the Tribunal.
  4. This application was originally listed for a hearing in November 2025 which was then relisted for early December 2025 following the interlocutory matters. On 22 October 2025 the Applicant wrote to the Tribunal requesting an adjournment of this hearing date. He said that he had only just received the Tribunal’s written reasons in the second interlocutory proceeding which had the effect of limiting the scope of the grounds and evidence he could rely on at the substantive hearing.[27] He advised the Tribunal that he had lodged a Freedom of Information (FOI) request with the Respondent on the same date as this interlocutory decision and it would take at least 30 days for the Respondent to respond to the FOI request, and then he would require time to consider the outcome of the FOI request.
  5. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that he was considering whether to file a further application seeking leave to rely on an additional ground of review, being that he was not a resident of Australia for the purposes of Australian income tax laws during the Relevant Years, and in particular whether the facts established the necessary legislative connection between him and Australia’s taxing jurisdiction. He suggested that this was different to the Tribunal’s previous consideration of residence arguments made by him, and contended that a ‘person may reside in Australia in the ordinary sense yet not be a “resident for the purposes of the Act” within the meaning of the income-taxing provisions.’
  6. The Tribunal held a further case management directions hearing on 11 November 2025.[28] At that directions hearing the Respondent advised that it had recently located PayPal transaction documentation that had not previously been filed with the Tribunal and which it wished to file by way of supplementary Tribunal documents. The Tribunal decided that it was appropriate to adjourn the final hearing to allow the Applicant time to receive and consider this additional material. The Applicant also said that he would narrow his FOI request to exclude this material. The Applicant was also given leave to make submissions to the Tribunal as to why he believes he was not a resident of Australia for the purposes of Australian income taxation law in the income years the subject of this proceeding.[29]
  7. The hearing was relisted for three days commencing on 2 February 2026. A further case management directions hearing was listed for 20 January 2026 to ensure that the matter was ready for hearing on the relisted dates. This directions hearing was a lengthy one as certain procedural matters were raised.
  8. The Applicant had filed further submissions dated 15 January 2026 for the directions hearing, the purpose of which he described as being to:
    (a) clarify the scope of the documents relied upon by the Applicant for the purposes of this proceeding;

    (b) identify the live correctness issues that must ultimately be determined under Part IVC; and

  9. His submissions were accompanied by his draft of orders which he requested the Tribunal make. These orders included the filing by the Respondent within 21 days of certain types of documents and other materials relating to PayPal and bank accounts, or a sworn statement by the Respondent that it did not hold such material; liberty to apply for directions ‘permitting third-party production (including by subpoena)’ of certain documents from financial institutions to the extent that such material was not held by the Respondent; liberty to file further evidence or submissions following any such disclosure; and the listing of a further directions hearing by the Tribunal. The Applicant also requested orders that the Tribunal note certain matters for the purposes of case management, hearing planning and identification of material reflecting what the Applicant viewed as threshold issues and sequencing of issues before the Tribunal.

OVERVIEW OF ISSUES IN CONTENTION

  1. The Applicant set out three positions, each of which he submitted formed a basis for the Tribunal setting aside the Objection Decision and thus the underlying Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments:
    (a) His primary position was that it had not been established on the evidence that he was a resident of Australia in any of the Relevant Years. The Tribunal therefore could not be satisfied that the scope of assessable income adopted in the Default Assessments was correct. He also described residency as a ‘foundational element’ or precondition to the statutory pathway for making the Default Assessments. This was referred to as the ‘residency’ issue by the parties.

    (b) Alternatively, if the Tribunal did not accept his primary position on residency, his secondary position was that the Tribunal could not be satisfied on the evidence that the gross amounts received into the bank and PayPal accounts of the Entities (that is the ‘unexplained deposits’) were beneficially derived by him, nor did the evidence provide a reliable basis for allocating any proportion of those amounts to him. This was referred to as the ‘attribution’ issue for the purposes of the hearing.

    (c) Alternatively, and only if the Tribunal did not accept both his primary position on residency and his secondary position on attribution, his tertiary position was that the Tribunal should have regard to the reconstruction and analysis of the financial material before the Tribunal which he prepared and provided to the Respondent and Tribunal, as indicative of his taxable income in the Relevant Years. These financial outcomes were summarised in an overview document referred to by the Applicant as ‘Exhibit A’.[36] This was referred to as the ‘quantum’ issue for the purposes of the hearing.

  2. The Applicant also submitted that the penalties set out in the Penalty Assessments should not have been imposed, but if the Tribunal found that penalties did arise he said that all or a substantial part of those penalties should be remitted.
  3. The Tribunal understood the Respondent’s position to be as follows:
    (a) The Applicant had not discharged his burden of proof under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953 to prove that any of the Default Assessments were excessive and what the correct amount of his taxable income in the Relevant Years should have been.

    (b) Similarly, the Applicant had not discharged his burden of proof under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) in relation to the Penalty Assessments.

    (c) The Applicant had not discharged his burden of proof under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(ii) in relation to the exercise of the power to remit any of the penalties.

    (d) The Respondent characterised the Applicant’s submissions on residency as the Applicant seeking to challenge the validity of the Default Assessments, a matter which was not within the Tribunal’s jurisdiction as an administrative body conducting a merits review, rather than a court exercising powers of judicial review. In any event, the Respondent had made a finding as to the Applicant’s residency prior to issuing the Default Assessments.

    (e) The Respondent also viewed the Applicant’s submissions on attribution as an apparent challenge by the Applicant to the validity of the Default Assessments, which was not relevant to the Tribunal’s review under the ART Act. The Applicant’s submissions on attribution also sought to reverse the statutory burden of proof, by suggesting that the Respondent was required to establish that the unexplained deposits were attributable to the Applicant as income. Under paragraph 14ZZK(b) it was for the Applicant to satisfy the Tribunal that those amounts were not attributable to him.

    (f) The Respondent rejected the Applicant’s reconstructed financial statements and calculations as demonstrative of what his taxable income should have been in any of the Relevant Years. The Respondent said that these statements and calculations did not represent a calculation of taxable income, but were directed towards determination of net profit or loss or income for accounting purposes. Further, the Respondent believed that there was evidence indicating these statements and calculations were not reliable.

  4. The Respondent said that the administrative penalties set out in the Penalty Assessments were properly imposed and that there were no circumstances supporting remission of all or part of those penalties.

RESIDENCE OF THE APPLICANT

Relevant law

  1. Income tax is payable by an individual for each financial year and is worked out by reference to the taxable income of the individual for the relevant income year, which has regard to the assessable income and deductions of the individual: sections 4-1, 4-10 and 4-15 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 1997).
  2. The assessable income of an Australian resident will include the ordinary income and statutory income they derived from all sources, whether in or out of Australia, during the income year: subsections 6-5(2), 6-10(4).[37] The assessable income of a foreign resident includes ordinary income and statutory income derived from Australian sources, together with ordinary income that any other provision of the taxation legislation includes in assessable income on some basis other than having an Australian source: subsections 6-5(3), 6-10(5).
  3. An ‘Australian resident’ is a person who is a resident of Australia for the purposes of the ITAA 1936: section 995-1 of the ITAA 1997.[38]
  4. Subsection 6(1) of the ITAA 1936 provides that a ‘resident’ or ‘resident of Australia’ means, in relation to an individual:
    a) a person, other than a company, who resides in Australia and includes a person:

    (i) whose domicile is in Australia, unless the Commissioner is satisfied that the person’s permanent place of abode is outside Australia;

    (ii) who has actually been in Australia, continuously or intermittently, during more than one‑half of the year of income, unless the Commissioner is satisfied that the person’s usual place of abode is outside Australia and that the person does not intend to take up residence in Australia; or

    (iii) who is:

    (A) a member of the superannuation scheme established by deed under the Superannuation Act 1990; or

    (B) an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976; or

    (C) the spouse, or a child under 16, of a person covered by sub‑subparagraph (A) or (B)

  5. Terms such as ‘reside’, ‘permanent place of abode’ and ‘usual place of abode’ are not further defined in either the ITAA 1936 or ITAA 1997. There is a body of case authority which has set out principles for determining whether a person meets the elements of the definition of a resident, with these matters being heavily reliant on the particular circumstances of the person.

Relevance of Applicant’s residency status to the Default Assessments

  1. Having been given leave by the Tribunal to rely on this ground, the Applicant filed written submissions ‘regarding residency as a foundational precondition’ dated 15 January 2026,[39] in addition to his outline of submissions for the hearing. He also addressed the topic of residency in his oral submissions to the Tribunal at the hearing, and in his closing written submissions dated 26 March 2026 and a response to the Respondent’s closing submissions.
  2. The Applicant filed a short affidavit sworn by him on 15 January 2026 in which he stated that:
    (a) He was not a resident of Australia in any of the Relevant Years for the purposes of Australian taxation laws.

    (b) The Respondent had not identified, and the Applicant was not aware of, any affirmative evidence relied upon by the Respondent establishing that he was a resident of Australia in any of the Relevant Years for taxation purposes.

    (c) He did not admit that he satisfied any test of residency and expressly disputed that residency had been established as a foundational fact supporting the assessments.

  3. The Applicant sought to rely on this affidavit by way of having ‘put residency in issue on oath.’[40] He confirmed that no further witness or other evidence was being put by him to the Tribunal in relation to residency matters.[41]
  4. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he was not asking the Tribunal to determine whether he was a resident in each of the Relevant Years, including whether he met residence criteria such as physical presence, domicile or living arrangements.[42] However he submitted that the question of whether he was a ‘resident of Australia’ in any of the Relevant Years was a foundational element or statutory precondition to the issuance of the Default Assessments by the Applicant.[43] He contended that where the Respondent had failed to establish the Applicant’s residency, factually or legally, and proceeded on an assumption as to his residency, the requirements for the assessment pathway, which he also described as a legal gateway, under section 167 of the ITAA 1936 have not been met. He said that on the evidence before the Tribunal his residency for taxation purposes had not been established in each of the Relevant Years. On that basis the Tribunal could ‘not be satisfied that the scope of assessable income adopted in the [Default Assessments], including any reliance on residency-based assumptions is correct’[44] and the Default Assessments could not stand.
  5. More particularly the Applicant said:[45]
    (a) Section 167, whilst authorising the Respondent to make an assessment where a person has not furnished a return, does not dispense with the need for the Respondent to form an opinion as to the taxpayer’s correct liability under taxation laws. That opinion must be formed by reference to the relevant statutory regime said to apply to the taxpayer, including whether the taxpayer is a resident for taxation purposes.

    (b) Where the Respondent proceeds on the basis that the taxpayer is a resident, the ability to make an assessment under section 167 on that basis requires residency to have been established as a ‘foundational fact’ rather than on the basis of an assumption that the taxpayer is a resident. Unless (Australian) residency has been established, the consequences of such residence do not arise.

    (c) Residency is determined by findings of fact directed to the relevant statutory criteria, for each income year. In this case, the Respondent had not identified for each of the Relevant Years which statutory test of residency was said to apply to the Applicant or identified evidence directed to such tests. The Tribunal had to be satisfied on the evidence before it that the Respondent had identified and relied on a lawful statutory basis for treating the Applicant as falling within the taxing regime for the purposes of issuing the Default Assessments.

    (d) Section 161 of the ITAA 1936 provides that a person must, if required by the Respondent by legislative instrument, give to the Respondent a return for the year of income specified in that instrument. The Respondent had not identified any legislative instrument said to apply to the Applicant, the class of persons into which the Applicant was said to fall or any analysis establishing that the Applicant was subject to the ‘resident’ taxation regime rather than the ‘non-resident’ regime. The Respondent had therefore failed to establish the legal basis upon which the Applicant was treated as subject to tax as a resident of Australia.

    (e) There was an absence of evidence addressing the threshold question of residency of the Applicant, which meant that the Tribunal could not be satisfied that the Default Assessments were correct. The Applicant said this was a matter of the correctness of the Default Assessments, and not a matter relating to the validity of or jurisdictional basis for the Default Assessments.

  6. Although the Applicant had said that he was not asking the Tribunal to determine whether he was a resident in any of the Relevant Years, he made some submissions as to which factors might (or might not) be relevant to a determination of residence, such as physical presence or living in Australia or the conduct of income generating activities within Australia.[46]
  7. The Respondent submitted that:
    (a) Despite the Applicant’s submissions to the contrary, his arguments about residency went to matters relating to the validity of the Default Assessments.

    (b) By virtue of section 54 of the ART Act, the Tribunal has all of the powers of the original decision maker for the purposes of its review of the reviewable decision. However, these powers can only be exercised within the jurisdiction conferred by the relevant statue. The Tribunal does not have a wider or more general review jurisdiction.

  8. While the Tribunal appreciates that there is some initial logic in the Applicant’s contentions, he faces a number of difficulties with his submissions. The Tribunal makes the following observations:
    (a) The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s submission that the Tribunal’s powers of review of taxation objection decisions may only be exercised within the jurisdictional scope of Part IVC of the TAA 1953. This is made clear by the words of section 54 of the ART Act which provide that ‘for the purposes of reviewing a reviewable decision’ the Tribunal may exercise the power and discretions conferred on the decision-maker ‘by an Act or an instrument made under an Act.’ The Full Court of the Federal Court in Kennedy v Administrative Appeals Tribunal described the jurisdiction and powers of the predecessor to the current Tribunal as follows: [47]

    The Tribunal has jurisdiction to hear and determine the present review under Part IVC of the TAA because each assessment purports to have been made in exercise of powers conferred by that enactment. Whether or not the assessments were, as a matter of law, validly made does not attenuate this finding.’

    (b) The Tribunal has previously explained that it is not a court and does not exercise judicial powers. It is unable to conduct the type of judicial review which may be undertaken by a court under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth) or the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth).

    (c) In relation to the Applicant’s submissions on section 161, the Tribunal views this as seeking to introduce a further or new ground of objection, which the Applicant has not been given leave to raise before the Tribunal. Nevertheless, it can be dealt with fairly quickly. The Tribunal has previously explained, by reference to a number of longstanding authorities, that it is not part of the Tribunal’s function to review the basis upon which the Respondent issued the Default Assessments or validity of such assessments, including in the specific context of an argument that there was a ‘statutory precondition’ arising under section 161 for the Respondent to prove the existence and application of legislative instruments made for the purposes of that provision.[48] The Applicant has not provided any new evidence or analysis that would alter this conclusion. The Tribunal does not agree that the making by the Respondent of an assessment under section 167 is dependent on the Respondent having provided evidence of legislative instruments referred to in section 161.

    (d) Further (and logically) the Applicant was either a resident of Australia or a non-resident of Australia in each of the Relevant Years. Each of those options potentially attracts a different taxation treatment. Broadly, if a taxpayer is a resident they are taxed on their income from sources in and outside Australia, whereas a non-resident will be taxed on Australian source income. The Tribunal accepts in principle that the question of whether the Applicant was a resident might go to the ‘correctness’ of the Default Assessments for the purposes of paragraph 14ZZK(b). However the Applicant has not demonstrated why or how any of the Default Assessments are excessive or incorrect having regard to his residency status. He simply asserts that the Respondent should not have ‘assumed’ that he was a resident and that the Respondent should have demonstrated ‘some evidentiary and analytical basis for that position.’[49] The Tribunal does not agree that the Respondent was required to do so. Section 167 says that where the Respondent ‘has reason to believe’ that a person who has not furnished a return has taxable income, the Respondent ‘may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his or her judgment income tax ought to be levied.’ (emphasis added). Even if validity had been in issue, on the basis of the audit reasons for decision, the Objection Decision and the Default Assessments alone the Tribunal believes that the Respondent has a ‘reason to believe’ and has made such a ‘judgment’ including in relation to the matter of the Applicant’s residency.

  9. For the sake of completeness, the Tribunal also notes that the Applicant told the Tribunal that there was no articulation of residency in the Objection Decision and no identification of which residency test was applied to him by the Respondent in any of the thousands of pages of documents filed with the Tribunal, such that his Australian residency had simply been ‘assumed’ by the Respondent without any reasoning or analysis.[50] From the materials filed by the parties and the oral evidence given at the hearing, the Tribunal notes that:
    (a) At paragraph 26 of the Objection Decision the Respondent set out its finding that ‘you are an Australian resident for income tax purposes.’

    (b) The Objection Decision was made in relation to the Default Assessments which were issued to reflect the Audit Reasons for Decision dated 19 February 2018,[51] being the Default Assessments against which the Applicant objected. At paragraphs 10 and 11 of the Audit Reasons for Decision the Respondent stated, within the ‘facts and evidence’ underlying its decision, that the Applicant was an Australian resident for tax purposes and identified a particular address in a suburb of Melbourne which it believed to be his residential address based on various sources including information obtained from financial institutions, VicRoads and the Department of Immigration and Border Protection. Appendix D to the Audit Reasons for Decision is a three-page table which sets out three addresses in suburban Melbourne and the basis[52] upon which the Respondent believed that these addresses were linked to the Applicant, and in particular why addresses were his residential addresses at particular times across the Relevant Years.

    (c) The Applicant told the Tribunal that correspondence was sent by the Respondent to the address stated in the Audit Reasons for Decision, however for at least part of period of the Relevant Years he did not physically live at that location. Residential address data held by agencies such as VicRoads might therefore not have been reliable. He explained to the Tribunal that due to the loss of his previous business, litigation which resulted in eviction from his home and consequential financial problems, he found himself homeless and had to sleep at the business premises of a friend. The Applicant did confirm that those business premises were in suburban Melbourne.[53]

    (d) The documents filed by the Respondent in this proceeding included material obtained from banks during 2017 in response to the Division 353 notices, such as copies of applications to open accounts with those banks. Within one of these applications, which is dated March 2015 and appears to bear the signature of the Applicant, the response to the question ‘Are you a resident of Australia for tax purposes’ is ‘Yes.’ The ‘permanent residential address’ of the Applicant since 2000 is stated to be an address in suburban Melbourne.[54]

  10. Even if the Applicant had been correct in arguing that a finding as to his residency status was a statutory precondition to the Default Assessments of which the Tribunal needed to be satisfied based on the evidence, the Tribunal would have been willing to find that the Respondent had made such a finding based on evidence, as set out in the Audit Reasons for Decision. The Applicant did not provide evidence to challenge the material in the Audit Reasons for Decision. The Audit Reasons for Decision were affirmed in the Objection Decision, the Applicant not having challenged residency matters during the objection process. The affidavit he filed with the Tribunal on residency in this proceeding merely contained a series of assertions that he was not a resident. His oral evidence to the Tribunal was that he may not have actually been living at the address identified by the Respondent during each of the Relevant Years, but might have been sleeping at another address in suburban Melbourne. The Tribunal believes that a conclusion could have been formed that the Applicant was a resident of Australia on the basis that he ‘resided’ in Australia, that is, on ordinary concepts. The Tribunal does not believe that it was necessary in these circumstances for the Respondent to articulate particular paragraphs of the subsection 6(1) definition of a ‘resident.’ Although reference was made to various overseas trips for holiday or business purposes, the Applicant did not provide any evidence which would indicate that he did not reside in Australia in any of the Relevant Years, or that he had a permanent place of abode outside Australia in any of those years.
  11. The filing by the Applicant of an affidavit in which he merely makes assertions that he was not a resident, did not admit to being a resident and disputed that residency had been established does not alter these conclusions. Further, in light of the matters set out in paragraph 52 above the Applicant should have exercised caution before swearing in an affidavit that the Respondent had not identified, and he was not aware of, any evidence relied upon by the Respondent establishing that he was an Australian resident for taxation purposes in any of the Relevant Years.
  12. Finally, for the reasons given below, even if the Tribunal were to accept that the Applicant had demonstrated that any of the Default Assessments were ‘incorrect’ (on the basis of residency or otherwise) the Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant has shown what that Default Assessment should have been.

ATTRIBUTION OF INCOME TO APPLICANT AND THE DEFAULT ASSESSMENTS

Relevant law and case authorities

  1. The Tribunal had previously set out in its earlier interlocutory decisions key principles relating to assessments made under section 167 of the ITAA 1936, the scope of the Tribunal’s role when it undertakes a review of an objection decision relating to such assessments and the burden of proof which an applicant seeking such a review must satisfy. Unfortunately many of these matters arose again within submissions for the substantive hearing. The Tribunal therefore repeats and elaborates below on reasoning that may previously have been provided to the parties.[55]
  2. In relation to an application for review of a taxation objection decision relating to assessments, including default assessments:
    (a) If a person makes default in furnishing a return, the Respondent is not satisfied with the return furnished by a person or the Respondent has reason to believe that a person who has not furnished a return has derived taxable income, then the Respondent may make an assessment of the amount upon which, in the Respondent’s judgement, income tax ought to be levied and that amount will be the taxable income of the person: section 167 of the ITAA 1936.
(b) The validity of an assessment is not affected by reason that any of the provisions of the ITAA 1936, ITAA 1997, Part IVC of the TAA 1953 or Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 have not been complied with: section 175 of the ITAA 1936.[56]

(c) Further, a notice of assessment is conclusive evidence that the assessment was properly made. It is also conclusive evidence that the amounts and particulars of the assessment are correct, except in Part IVC proceedings upon a review or appeal relating to that assessment: Item 2 in the Table in subsection 350-10, Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953.

(d) On a review of an objection decision relating to an assessment, an applicant has the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been: subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953. An applicant taxpayer must satisfy the Tribunal of this on the ‘balance of probabilities.’[57]

(e) In this context the Tribunal cannot review the processes by which the Default Assessments were made or the validity of the Default Assessments. The Tribunal is able to review the amounts and particulars of the Default Assessments, and in particular whether the amounts of the Default Assessments are excessive.[58]

(f) The role of the Tribunal is to make the correct or preferable decision as to whether the Applicant has discharged his burden of proving that the Default Assessments are excessive or incorrect, and what they should have been. [59]

(g) There is no burden on the Respondent to show that the Default Assessments were correct. The High Court has said that the manner in which taxpayers may discharge their burden of proof in relation to assessments may vary depending on the circumstances of the particular case, however absent any agreement between the parties to confine the appeal against the assessment to a specific point of law or fact, there is no onus on the Respondent to show that the relevant assessments were correctly made and the Respondent is entitled to rely on ‘any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount assessed.’[60] The High Court also accepted the view that there was no statutory requirement that the assessments be sustained or supported by evidence.[61]

(h) The Federal Court has indicated how a taxpayer might discharge their burden of proof in seeking to establish that a default assessment was excessive. In Ma v Commissioner of Taxation, Burchett J said that where a taxpayer who denies an undisclosed source of income ‘provides acceptable evidence’ and ‘demonstrates a reasonable explanation’ for the relevant matters, he will ‘generally discharge his burden of proof unless some positive reason is shown why he is to be disbelieved.’[62]

(i) However an approach of attacking elements of the Respondent’s calculations and facts in respect of which the taxpayer chooses to lead evidence is not sufficient for the taxpayer to discharge their burden.[63]

(j) Further, the process for a (default) assessment under section 167 of the ITAA 1936 differs from that under section 166. Consistent with the Respondent ‘being entitled to judge an amount as taxable under section 167 ... it is insufficient for a taxpayer simply to show error on the [Respondent’s] part.’[64]

(k) The High Court in Dalco referred to the possibility of ‘exceptional’ circumstances in which an error might have been made on the part of the Respondent, such that the error affected not only the correctness of the amount assessed but ‘vitiated the purported exercise of the power to assess.’ The Court warned that care must be taken to ‘maintain the distinction between an error in exercising the power to make an assessment and an error which deprives a purported assessment of validity.’ Nevertheless, in Dalco the Court confirmed that ‘the Commissioner’s bona fide judgment as to the amount of the taxpayer’s taxable income and the power to make the assessment was validly exercised. The assessments being valid, the burden was on the taxpayer to prove that the amounts assessed were excessive.’[65]

  1. In the context of this proceeding both parties acknowledged and referred to subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953 as applicable. It is not controversial that under this provision an applicant has more than one task. It is not sufficient for the Applicant to satisfy the Tribunal that the Default Assessments are incorrect, or unlikely to be correct, in some way or to some extent. The burden of proof in this proceeding is on the Applicant to satisfy the Tribunal, on the balance of probabilities, that the Default Assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what those Default Assessments should have been.[66]
  2. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he accepted that he bore the burden of proof set out in section 14ZZK. He also acknowledged that he bore the burden of proving that the Default Assessments were excessive, and that it was insufficient to show that the Default Assessments were ‘wrong’ with the Applicant being required to demonstrate what amount should have been assessed. He acknowledged that his burden of proof required more than a mere negative assertion.[67]
  3. Although these principles were supposedly not controversial, and although the Applicant and Respondent generally cited the same case authorities in support of their contentions as to whether the Applicant had satisfied his burden of proof, the parties ultimately took different approaches as to how these principles applied in this proceeding.

Attribution and derivation of income

  1. A key issue in dispute between the parties was whether there was any basis for the Applicant being treated as having derived any of the ‘unexplained’ amounts in the first place, being the ‘attribution’ issue. The Respondent attributed to the Applicant as his income amounts from bank and PayPal accounts relating to the Applicant (including a business conducted by him as a sole trader), two companies (including a business name registered to one of those companies), five entities described as ‘private foundations’ by the Applicant and two other ‘entities’ or names whose nature was unclear.
  2. Subsection 6-5(2) of the ITAA 1997 provides that:
    If you are an Australian resident, your assessable income includes the *ordinary income you *derived directly or indirectly from all sources, whether in or out of Australia, during the income year.
  3. Subsection 6-5(4) provides that:
    In working out whether you have derived an amount of *ordinary income, and (if so) when you derived it, you are taken to have received the amount as soon as it is applied or dealt with in any way on your behalf or as you direct.
  4. An Australian resident must also include within their assessable income their ‘statutory income’ (being income other than ordinary income but which are included in assessable income by legislative provisions) from all sources: subsection 6-10(4).
  5. The term ‘ordinary income’ as used in section 6-5 refers to ‘income according to ordinary concepts’: subsection 6-5(1). This in turn draws on principles drawn from case authorities which have identified a number of factors which indicate an amount is to be characterised as income.
  6. The concept of ‘derivation’ is also not further defined within the ITAA 1936 or ITAA 1997, and case authorities have looked to a nexus between amounts and income producing activities.
  7. The manner in which different entities are recognised and treated for Australia taxation purposes depends on a number of factors, but generally speaking:
    (a) It is not controversial that an individual operating a business in the capacity of a ‘sole trader’ will be subject to taxation on the taxable income arising from the activities or conduct of that business.[68]

    (b) It is also not controversial that for taxation purposes companies are ordinarily treated as separate entities to their shareholders (and officeholders) and it is the corporate entity which is subject to taxation on its taxable income. The shareholders may be subject to tax on dividends, including amounts deemed to be dividends,[69] paid (or taken to be paid) to them by the company. The officeholders may be subject to tax on amounts of remuneration or benefits paid or provided to them by the company for their services.[70]

    (c) An ‘entity’ described as a ‘foundation’ may have the legal form of a trust, a corporation or unincorporated association or a co-operative and the taxation treatment of the entity will follow accordingly.

  8. However, in the present case the statutory provisions and case authorities relating to income and derivation must be considered in the context of assessments having been issued under section 167 and the Applicant’s burden of proof under paragraph 14ZZK(b). For the purposes of this review by the Tribunal the Respondent is not required to provide evidence demonstrating derivation of income by the Applicant, and the Applicant will not discharge his burden of proof by asserting that the Respondent has not shown how he derived amounts included within the Default Assessments. The Applicant must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that such amounts should not have been included in the determination of his taxable income.

Contentions of parties in relation to attribution and quantum of amounts

  1. The Applicant made the following submissions:
    (a) The Applicant identified three premises upon which he said the Respondent had based the Default Assessments[71] and the evidence put forward at the hearing did not permit the Tribunal to be satisfied that those premises had been made out. This was not mere criticism of the Respondent’s reasoning.[72]

    (b) The Applicant said he was not raising these matters by way of challenges to the validity of the Default Assessments, but said that in the context of a Part IVC merits review these premises must be established on the evidence before the Tribunal, so that the Tribunal could determine the ‘correct or preferable amount of the Applicant’s taxable income.’[73] If those matters (the ‘premises’ identified by the Applicant) could not be established on the evidence, this was not a question of the validity of the assessments but went directly to whether the Default Assessments were excessive and ‘what amount, if any, can be properly determined as the Applicant’s taxable income.’[74]

    (c) The Applicant said that the Respondent had not ‘identified any transaction-level analysis by which the financial data relied upon was examined to distinguish income from non-income amounts or to attribute those amounts to the Applicant.’ Without such an analysis the Tribunal could not be satisfied that the ‘amounts’ (presumably those in or underlying the Default Assessments) represented the Applicant’s taxable income. The Tribunal must be satisfied that specific amounts were derived by the Applicant and properly attributable to the Applicant as income, on the basis of the evidence before the Tribunal.[75]

    (d) The Applicant said that he was not challenging the legal validity of the Default Assessments, was not suggesting that the Default Assessments be set aside because of flaws in the Respondent’s methodology and was not suggesting that his burden under section 14ZZK could be discharged by identifying error alone. However he said that there were ‘factual matters necessary to determine whether the amounts assessed can be accepted on the evidence’ and the Respondent’s ‘approach to the evidence’ was flawed. Further, the ‘evidentiary foundation’ of the Default Assessments was affected by issues with how information had been obtained and relied upon,[76] which bore directly on the ‘reliability and weight of the material said to support’ the Default Assessments, and an ‘assessment that is not supported by the evidence cannot be accepted as correct.’ He submitted that the Respondent had wrongly contended that the Default Assessments must stand unless the Applicant could provide ‘a precisely quantified alternative calculation.’[77]

    (e) He contended that where a taxpayer established that an assessment was excessive or incorrect, the assessment was legally displaced.

  2. The Applicant identified two key issues for the Tribunal to consider (in circumstances where it did not accept his arguments relating to residency), with the second of those issues only becoming relevant if the Tribunal did not accept his arguments in relation to the first. The first of those issues was whether there was any basis upon which the Respondent could attribute to him as income amounts which had been received into the bank or PayPal accounts of the Entities. He submitted that the Respondent attributed amounts to him as income on the basis of assumption rather than evidence. He referred the Tribunal to several case authorities in support of the proposition that beneficial ownership or personal derivation of these amounts by him had to be established,[78] and this was particularly so in the context of a merits review. He said that it was not sufficient to merely establish that he was involved with, facilitated, accessed or had control of or authorised user status in relation to the accounts. The Applicant also said that the fact that an account bore his name, that he held a particular office or that no alternative beneficiary could be identified did not permit attribution of amounts ‘by default’ to him.[79]
  3. In this regard the Applicant also reiterated his concern that he had not been permitted to obtain further information, including account opening data and information identifying multiple other authorised users or office holders.[80] He acknowledged that documents already filed with the Tribunal, such as Articles which established the foundations, did contain some information about other office holders or authorised account users. However his view was that this material did not show, for example, that other users could sign or operate the accounts, the allocation of receipts between users or otherwise how amounts were derived by him personally.[81]
  4. In summary he said that where a basis for the attribution of the amounts to him had not been established, the Tribunal could not be satisfied that the Default Assessments were not excessive, it was not required to reconstruct an alternative assessment and it could not ‘speculate’ as to the outcome.[82]
  5. The second key issue identified by the Applicant was the basis for determination of the quantum of income attributed to him. The Applicant submitted to the Tribunal that if the Tribunal rejected his contentions as to residency, and also rejected his submissions in relation to whether any amounts said to be received by one or more of the Entities should be attributed to him as his income, then the Applicant in the alternative asked the Tribunal to consider the ‘financial outcomes’ arising from financial statements and calculations reconstructed by him for the Relevant Years. The Applicant wished to be clear that he was not conceding that these financial outcomes represented his personal taxable income in any of the Relevant Years,[83] but said that these financial outcomes reflected the ‘outer limits’ of what the accounting material available to him could demonstrate.
  6. In this regard the Applicant provided to the Tribunal a series of financial statements, accounts and working notes which had been reconstructed by the Applicant[84] containing calculations for some of the Entities which he said excluded non-income items, removed duplicated items, recognised identifiable deductions and marked outgoings which would not be characterised as deductible.
  7. The Respondent submitted in brief that the matters raised by the Applicant appeared to be directed to a challenge to the validity of the Default Assessments or were an attempt to reverse the burden of proof provided under section 14ZZK.[85]
  8. The Respondent’s position was that the Applicant had not discharged his burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessments. The Respondent contended that the Applicant had adopted an incorrect approach as to what was required for him to discharge his burden.
  9. The Respondent also rejected the Applicant’s reconstructed financial statements as being probative of what the Default Assessments, or quantum of the Default Assessments, should have been. The Respondent viewed these reconstructed statements and the calculations in them as representing the net accounting profit or loss of the entities to which they related, rather than a calculation of taxable income or amount to be assessed.[86]

Tribunal’s view on contentions

  1. Although the Tribunal appreciates that the Applicant would strongly disagree, the Tribunal’s view of some of his submissions was that he was effectively seeking to reverse or alter the burden of proof, such that the Respondent was required in this proceeding to put forward evidence positively supporting not only the correctness of the Default Assessments but the quantum of those assessment.[87] The Applicant’s submissions appeared to suggest that where the Respondent did not do so, the Applicant could not or would not provide evidence to address the Default Assessments. This was apparent in the Applicant’s approach to the issue of his residency status and the treatment of deposits which had been identified by the Respondent as attributable to the Applicant.
  2. The Applicant had been advised that such an approach was not correct in the previous interlocutory decisions handed down by the Tribunal. The Applicant indicated that he understood this, and said that his submissions for the purposes of the final hearing were in relation to statutory or jurisdictional preconditions, or threshold or foundational matters, underlying the assessments, rather than a reversal of the statutory burden of proof.
  3. The Tribunal does not agree with the Applicant that its task was to determine the ‘correct or preferable amount’ of the Applicant’s taxable income. Nor does the Tribunal agree with the Applicant’s contention that if demonstrated that a Default Assessment was excessive or incorrect, that assessment was ‘legally displaced.’ The Tribunal must decide, and in this context make the correct or preferable decision, whether the Applicant has discharged his statutory burden of proof as set out in subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i). That statutory burden of proof expressly requires an applicant taxpayer to not only prove an assessment to be excessive or incorrect, but where the taxpayer has done so additionally show what the assessment should have been.
  4. The Applicant could of course discharge his burden of proof by providing to the Tribunal evidence that countered the Respondent’s position on the correctness and quantum of the Default Assessments, such evidence being sufficient for the Tribunal to be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the Default Assessments were incorrect and what they should have been. That is, the Applicant might put forward evidence or analysis which demonstrated that the Default Assessments were not consistent with reliable evidence, such that the Default Assessments were not correct and therefore at least the first task under subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) was complete.
  5. However where the Applicant instead chose to argue that the Respondent had not provided satisfactory evidence in support of the elements or amounts of the Default Assessments, without more, the Tribunal must find that he had not met his burden of proof.
  6. The Respondent acknowledged that the Default Assessments may not be a completely accurate representation of the Applicant’s taxable income for the Relevant Years. An assessment made under section 167 is, by its nature, likely to be made in circumstances where the Respondent does not have complete and/or reliable information about the elements of the taxpayer’s taxable income.
  7. In relation to the case authorities cited by the Applicant in support of his contentions on derivation of income, and therefore whether there is a basis for attribution of income, the Tribunal acknowledges that each of Stone, Montgomery and Zobory set out principles relating to when amounts will be treated as ‘income’ of the recipient. The Tribunal believes that the High Court’s decision in Stone is of limited assistance to the Applicant, as the factual circumstances of the taxpayer in that case are distinguishable from those of the Applicant. In that respect, Stone is consistent with the Applicant’s submission that questions of derivation and the characterisation of receipts are fact dependent, and that derivation requires some factual connection between taxpayer and receipt. However that case did not consider questions of entitlement to or beneficial interest in a receipt. Similarly, the lease incentive payment the subject of Montgomery involved a different factual inquiry. Contrary to the Applicant’s submissions it was not decided on the basis of possession of or beneficial entitlement to an amount.
  8. However in Zobory Burchett J did make the observation that the provisions of the Australian taxation legislation which assess amounts of ordinary income are ‘directed to income to which a taxpayer is beneficially entitled’, citing earlier High Court authority.[88] Zobory dealt with the issue of whether a taxpayer was to be assessed on income which arose from the investment of money stolen by the taxpayer, with the taxpayer arguing that the funds were held on a constructive trust for the company from which he had stolen the money and thus not assessable to him.
  9. The Tribunal does accept that it might have been open to the Applicant to demonstrate what the amounts of the Default Assessments should have been by reference to ‘limits’ on certain items, including concessions by him as to amounts of deductions, which made up the calculation of overall taxable income in any of the Relevant Years. For example, the Applicant might have submitted on the basis of evidence that he could provide to the Tribunal that his taxable income in a particular year should be based on $100 of assessable income and $50 of deductible expenses, even though he might also believe that with further evidence he would have been able to prove a lesser income or greater deductible amount.
  10. In assessing the approach and evidence of the Applicant the Tribunal has been mindful that the Relevant Years occurred some considerable time ago and, that there were genuine reasons why the Applicant might not have retained complete records or documentary evidence from that period or it might be difficult to locate witnesses to those transactions or events. The Tribunal was also conscious that the Applicant was self-represented in this proceeding, although he did demonstrate a familiarity with legal processes and concepts based on other litigation with which he had been involved. To the extent that the Applicant did put forward evidence in support of his contentions the Tribunal has sought to give weight to this evidence with regard to these practical challenges.[89] The Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicant undertook significant work to prepare reconstructed statements relating to his financial position in the Relevant Years, to the best of his ability and to assist the Tribunal in understanding his submissions.
  11. However the Tribunal must be satisfied that there is evidence of sufficient reliability, in whatever form that may take, before the Tribunal can find that the Applicant has discharged his burden. The Tribunal accepts that it was not unreasonable for the Applicant to not recall very small or one-off amounts that were received over ten years ago. However the Tribunal believes it was reasonable for the Applicant to recall at least some basic information about Entities with which he acknowledged he had an association, even if he disputed the nature or extent of that association. The Tribunal does not accept the Applicant’s contention that it must necessarily determine matters on the basis of material that can ‘reasonably be reconstructed’ due to the absence or loss of any historic records. The weight to be given by the Tribunal to evidence put forward is a matter for the Tribunal to assess in the circumstances. The Tribunal does not believe that ‘exceptional circumstances’ of the sort alluded to in Dalco have arisen in the present proceeding.
  12. The Tribunal also acknowledges that the Applicant holds a strong view that he should have been given further opportunities to undertake processes for obtaining additional documentary material, including from the Respondent and third parties, on the basis of his belief that such material might support his contentions. Over the course of this application the Applicant had sought orders to obtain further material from the Respondent and third parties, including financial institutions and PayPal. The previous interlocutory decisions dealt with some of these requests. The Tribunal has also set out at paragraphs 22 to 25 above its response to the Applicant’s further request shortly before the date of the hearing.
  13. The Tribunal does not accept the Applicant’s argument that he was prevented from providing such information (and in particular information about the identity and role of other signatories, account holders or account users) to the Respondent or Tribunal, to the extent that he already had this information, by privacy laws. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant is not a lawyer and would not expect him to provide an analysis of Australia’s privacy legislation. However there was no articulation of why he thought there was a privacy law issue. In any event, it was not entirely clear to the Tribunal as to whether the Applicant was confirming that he had any such information. If the Applicant did not have information to provide or was unwilling to provide the information to the Tribunal, then that is a matter that goes to whether he could discharge his burden of proof.
  14. To the extent that it was suggested that the Respondent might be required to prove that the information it obtained from the banks and PayPal under the Division 353 notices was correct and complete, the Tribunal rejects this proposition. Having obtained such information and thereafter formed a view on matters such as, for example, the Applicant being the only account owner for the PayPal accounts, after the issue of the Default Assessments it was a matter for the Applicant to prove that the Respondent’s position was not correct. The Tribunal does not propose to repeat the Tribunal’s findings as made in the interlocutory proceedings. The Respondent was not required to provide further evidence to prove that other office holders or account users did not exist, or that if such office holders or account users existed they did not have particular powers or capacity, simply because the Applicant suggested that they might. If the Applicant had evidence that other office holders or account users did exist and could access the accounts, he should have provided this evidence, preferably at the objection stage but at least for the purposes of this proceeding.
  15. Although the Tribunal has rejected aspects of the Applicant’s submissions as to the manner in which he might discharge his burden of proof, and has also rejected his submissions relating to the legal basis for attribution and income derivation, the Tribunal does not automatically reject his reconstructed financial statements and outcomes. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent that the Applicant is required to demonstrate what his taxable income was and therefore what the assessments should have been. The Tribunal also agrees that accounting profits or losses do not necessarily represent assessable income or taxable income.
  16. However the Tribunal was willing to consider the evidence the Applicant provided (and in particular his reconstruction of his financial statements for the Relevant Years and the supporting material relating to that reconstruction), to determine whether he had nevertheless demonstrated that any of the Default Assessments were excessive or incorrect, and what they should have been, regardless of the particular labels given to the financial outcomes. The Applicant referred to the financial outcomes as reflecting the ‘outer limits’ of what the records available to him by the time of the hearing could demonstrate. In Trautwein, Latham CJ referred to the requirement for the taxpayer to demonstrate ‘what correction should be made in order to make [the assessments] right or more nearly right.’[90] The Tribunal’s view is that the Applicant’s attempts at reconstruction of financial statements or outcomes, unlike his other critiques of or challenges to the Default Assessments, goes beyond merely pointing at errors on the part of the Respondent. The Tribunal must therefore examine whether the Applicant’s reconstruction attempts are sufficiently reliable and probative in the context of meeting his burden of proof under section 14ZZK.
  17. The Applicant filed copies of a substantial number of tax invoices, receipts and credit card statements in support of his submissions that certain expenses and outgoings should have been taken into account in the determination of his taxable income in the Relevant Years.[91] As a general observation the Tribunal is willing to accept that some of the tax invoices or receipts evidence outgoings that would, on the balance of probabilities, represent deductible amounts. For example, the Tribunal would be likely to accept the tax invoices for printing services provided by Pax Printers to the Know Your Rights Foundation and Aussie Speeding Fines, which itemise the printing of seminar books and business cards, as evidence supporting a claim for such items.[92] Similarly receipts issued to Success Coaching International by local councils, Consumer Affairs Victoria, domain name providers and social media platforms for business and domain name registrations and online advertising would be regarded by the Tribunal as probative of deductible business expenditure.[93] The Tribunal might also accept tax invoices or receipts issued to the Applicant (rather than to any particular business or Entity) where the items described in the invoice or receipt had a clear ‘business’ character, such as venue hire or event related expenditure.[94] In the context of business activities described by the Applicant as including seminars and educational events, such expenditure might be accepted as deductible.
  18. The Tribunal was also open to accepting, on the basis of the oral evidence given by the Applicant under oath, that he may have received financial assistance in the form of smaller loans from his mother to cover his living expenses during periods of hardship and such amounts should not be regarded as assessable income of the Applicant.
  19. However:
    (a) A number of the tax invoices, receipts or credit card items filed by the Applicant describe amounts of a more general nature, which could reflect amounts of a personal or other non-deductible nature, particularly where those invoices or receipts were issued to the Applicant directly, were paid from a credit card in the name of the Applicant’s mother or did not identify the recipient of the goods or services. This includes expenditure on airfares, hotel accommodation, airport car parking, car rental, food, vehicle repairs and maintenance, petrol, freeway tolls, telephone/internet and IT or digital accessories of a generic nature such as chargers or cases.[95]

    (b) The characterisation of amounts recorded as loans from Mrs Palmer to the corporate Entities, or amounts described as ‘loans’ between Entities or the Applicant and entities, was less clear.

Treatment of amounts said to arise from bank and PayPal accounts

  1. Having regard to the propositions above, the Tribunal makes the following observations in relation to the various Entities identified, firstly in relation to the issue of ‘attribution’ and then if required, in relation to the issue of ‘quantum.’

Success Coaching International

  1. A bank account was identified by the Respondent held in the name of ‘Mike Palmer trading as Success Coaching International.’ The Respondent obtained a series of bank statements from NAB[96] from which it formed the view that the Applicant was the sole account holder of and signatory to this bank account. Significant amounts were identified as received into this bank account across each of the Relevant Years.[97]
  2. Absent details of any other account holders or any other information about these accounts, the Respondent assessed the Applicant on the gross amount of deposits into this bank account in the Relevant Years.
  3. This ‘Entity’ differs from some of the others under consideration in that there is no suggestion that a separate entity, such as a company or foundation, was established. The Applicant acknowledged that he was operating as a sole trader in relation to the Success Coaching International Business.[98] However the Applicant submitted that even where an account was described as a ‘sole trader’ or as an individual ‘trading as’, the Tribunal nevertheless was required to identify on the evidence which receipts were derived by that sole trader or individual personally, as opposed to which receipts were those of ‘a business or another entity.’ Therefore, in relation to a bank account held by the Applicant trading as Success Coaching International, he said that the account description or label did not establish that the gross inflow to that account was income derived by him personally.[99]
  4. There was no evidence before the Tribunal that amounts received into the bank account of Success Coaching International were held by the Applicant as nominee or trustee for another person or entity.[100] There was no suggestion that the Applicant was a partner in a partnership that conducted the Success Coaching International business (and this would be directly inconsistent with the bank statements provided for Success Coaching International).
  5. The Applicant is correct in saying that a receipt or the gross amount of a receipt into a bank account in his name (including in his capacity as a sole trader) is not necessarily assessable income to him. Hypothetically, an amount paid into a bank account could be a loan or a gift or some other non-assessable item. However that is a matter of characterisation of the nature of the amount received rather than an attribution question. Further, and more importantly, in the present circumstances the Applicant has the burden of proving that any such amount should not be assessable to him.
  6. On the basis of the bank statements provided by NAB which clearly state that the account for Success Coaching International was held by the Applicant ‘trading as’ Success Coaching International, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant was conducting this business in his capacity as a sole trader. He has not provided any evidence to the Tribunal that indicates otherwise. The Tribunal does not agree with his submissions that some further tracing exercise was required on the part of the Respondent (or Tribunal) before amounts received in that capacity were ‘attributable’ to him. He has also not provided any evidence to the Tribunal which would demonstrate that amounts received into this account were to be held for another person and/or were of a non-assessable nature. To the extent that those amounts were of an income nature, they would be taken to have been (personally) derived by him.
  7. The Tribunal therefore has no basis for displacing the position that amounts received into this Success Coaching International bank account in the Relevant Years were income derived by the Applicant.
  8. However the Tribunal also agrees that the Applicant must be given the opportunity to demonstrate that the amounts of the Default Assessments were excessive (and what they should have been) by way of providing evidence of any expenses deductible in relation to the Success Coaching International business. This is the ‘quantum’ aspect identified by the Applicant.
  9. The Respondent’s counsel asked the Applicant questions about whether payments from the bank account of Success Coaching International to a vehicle finance company were made in relation to a vehicle or vehicles driven by the Applicant or whether cash withdrawals at supermarkets and retail centres were for business or personal expenses. The Applicant explained that he had a preference to pay business related bills (including utilities and postage) in cash and would do so unless direct debit was required by the provider.[101] The Tribunal accepts this explanation as plausible. The Tribunal has already indicated that it accepts the ‘attribution’ of amounts received by Success Coaching International to the Applicant on the basis of his sole trader status, so the question of whether any such amounts were used for his personal purposes does not further the analysis of this issue. However this discussion is relevant to the issue of ‘quantum’ of amounts to be recognised in determining his taxable income.
  10. The Respondent’s counsel also asked the Applicant about his use of vehicles, and he confirmed that he had both a personal vehicle and multiple business vehicles at various times during the Relevant Years.[102] The Applicant also identified purchases from retail outlets such as Kmart, Target and Costco as potentially being for printing or items of stationery. The Tribunal accepts that since these purchases were made more than a decade ago, it was difficult for him to recall the detail. The bank statements from 2009 also showed a number of transactions made in the USA. The Applicant explained that he was also a professional photographer and had travelled to the USA on a photography trip. This was early activity for the ‘Inspiring Images’ business, which subsequently was separated out from the Success Coaching International business (and which is discussed further below).[103]
  11. The Applicant had prepared and provided to the Tribunal for the purposes of the hearing a set of ‘financial outcomes’ for Success Coaching International in the form of profit/loss statements for the Relevant Years, and accompanying working notes, including monthly incoming and outgoings summaries.[104] The Applicant explained to the Tribunal the methodology he adopted in preparing this material, which included identification of amounts received into the NAB bank account (per the statements) which he did not view as being of an income nature,[105] as well as a breakdown of outgoings into business and personal expenditure. The Applicant contended that this approach would produce a more accurate picture for taxation purposes than simply treating all gross inflows into accounts as income.[106]
  12. The Tribunal was open to accepting this material as demonstrating the quantum of assessable income, deductible expenses and therefore taxable income that should have been attributed to him in relation to Success Coaching International in the Relevant Years, for the purposes of proving what the Default Assessments ‘should have been.’ The Tribunal agrees that a proper characterisation of amounts as assessable income or deductible expenses is more relevant to a correct determination of taxable income. However whether the Tribunal does accept this material for this purpose is dependent on whether the Tribunal accepts the financial outcomes put forward by the Applicant as sufficiently reliable on the evidence.
  13. The Tribunal accepted that the Applicant might have difficulty locating all supporting documentation underlying his financial outcomes, such as receipts and invoices, due to the significant time that had passed since the Relevant Years. The Applicant had also flagged prior to the hearing that he had some of this material in storage at his mother’s home in New Zealand, and had asked the Tribunal for guidance as to how he might present the material. By way of confirmation of the existence of such material the Applicant provided photographs said to be of boxes of receipts, invoices and similar documents.[107] The Respondent raised a concern about whether the Tribunal could place any evidentiary weight on such material. The Tribunal agrees that it needed to approach this material with some caution, but also needed to be mindful of the risk that significant delay would have arisen in progressing this application if potentially thousands of pages of additional documents needed to be scanned and/or shipped.[108]
  14. The Tribunal again reiterates that it is for the Applicant to prove that any or all of the Default Assessments were excessive or incorrect, and what they should have been. It is not sufficient for the Applicant to identify some (or even many) amounts which the Respondent may have treated incorrectly in making the Default Assessments.
  15. In preparing his financial outcomes and account summaries for Success Coaching International the Applicant has properly acknowledged that amounts were received in the bank account that should be regarded as income. This includes ‘general sales’ as well as income the Applicant received from other work, such as with the Victorian Racing Commission. The Applicant has also properly acknowledged that some payments from that account should not be treated as deductible expenditure.[109]
  16. The Tribunal does accept the Applicant’s characterisation of certain other receipts into the bank account for Success Coaching International (such as Medicare refunds) as not being of an income nature. The Tribunal also accepts that some outgoings identified by him, such as bank fees,[110] are on the balance of probabilities likely to be deductible expenses. Similarly, some invoices from third parties referencing expenditure by or for Success Coaching International were provided which the Tribunal would accept as probative of expenditure relating to that business.[111]
  17. The Tribunal is also willing to accept the Applicant’s verbal evidence, given under oath, that his mother lent him varying amounts of money at different times during the Relevant Years when he needed financial assistance and he repaid those amounts as and when he could.[112] The Tribunal believes this is plausible in the context of his personal circumstances and the evidence of his mother’s involvement in other enterprises he conducted. The Tribunal acknowledges the Respondent’s concern that there was a lack of documentation relating to such loans, and the Applicant agreed that he did not keep a ‘dollar for dollar ledger’ but the Tribunal considers that this is explicable in the context of a close family relationship. Such receipts from Mrs Palmer would not be assessable income (nor would amounts repaid to her be deductible to the Applicant).
  18. However there are other items proposed by the Applicant in his financial outcomes which the Tribunal does not agree with, or does not believe are supported by evidence. Certain receipts into the bank account of Success Coaching International were described as ‘loans from LCS’. During cross-examination the Applicant was asked to explain what these amounts were. He said that they were ‘probably’ from Libra Consulting and acknowledged that he had no records for such loans, nor could he indicate a breakdown of interest or principal amounts for repayment of such loans.[113] The account summaries prepared by the Applicant also included as deductible amounts of expenditure (such as repairs and maintenance, tolls) relating to multiple motor vehicles, as well as sale proceeds from at least one vehicle. The Applicant said that he had excluded from his calculations amounts relating to one vehicle which he agreed had been for his personal use. However his evidence about which motor vehicles might relate to any of the Relevant Years for particular business or entities was vague, as he told the Tribunal that he had bought and sold ‘lots’ of cars and ‘maybe’ there were multiple vehicles used by Success Coaching International at that time.[114] There was a reference to a trading settlement in the 2008 year. The Applicant could not recall whether this was a reference to share trading or otherwise what it involved.[115]
  19. The Applicant also indicated that cash withdrawn from Success Coaching International’s bank account might have been used to pay off a Diners Club card and/or Virgin Money Card, which in turn had been used for a mixture of business and personal expenditure.[116]
  20. Even where the Applicant was unable to provide all documentation for historic transactions, the Tribunal may have been willing to find that he had discharged his burden of proof for a Default Assessment where he was able to provide sufficient evidence to support an assessment that was of an amount which was less than what the Respondent had assessed him to (and even if the Applicant held a personal view that the assessment should have been nil or a much lesser amount). This is broadly consistent with the Applicant’s submission that the Tribunal consider an ‘outer limit’ of what an assessment should have been.
  21. However the Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant has provided evidence to support a finding that any of the Default Assessments should have been of a particular amount that is less than what the Respondent had assessed. Although the financial outcomes and account summaries provided detail of his contentions as to the elements of a correct assessment, a significant proportion of these elements were not supported by satisfactory evidence. In particular, the Tribunal could not be satisfied with the Applicant’s contentions relating to loans from Libra Consulting, transactions relating to motor vehicles and ‘trading’ transactions.

Libra Consulting Services

  1. A bank account was identified by the Respondent held in the name of Libra Consulting Services with National Australia Bank (NAB). Amounts were identified as received into this bank account in each of the Relevant Years.[117]
  2. The Respondent said that the Applicant was the sole director, secretary and shareholder of this company. Therefore the Respondent said in the Objection Decision that it would assess the Applicant on the full amount of any deposits into this bank account. The Respondent acknowledged that, based on information obtained from NAB, the Applicant was a co-signatory to this account, but did not believe this changed its position on attribution. The Respondent also referred to information obtained from the bank that the Applicant was the ‘sole authorising party’ to this bank account.[118]
  3. A company search obtained by the Respondent in May 2017[119] indicated that the Applicant was a director of Libra Consulting Services from July 2004 to 1 February 2012 and then was reappointed on 20 March 2012. He was appointed as secretary in March 2012. He remained in these roles as at the date of the company search. It is correct therefore that, with the exception of a few weeks in February and March 2012, the Applicant was the sole director and secretary of this company in the Relevant Years.[120]
  4. A PayPal account was also identified by the Respondent as held in the name of ‘Libra Consulting ... trading as Aussie Speeding Fines.’ This is discussed below with the other PayPal account for Aussie Speeding Fines.
  5. The Applicant said that Libra Consulting used to operate a transcription business and he received a wage from this company at that time, around the 1990s or early 2000s, but he had not received remuneration from it since then.[121] The Applicant also told the Tribunal that Libra Consulting might have been a trustee for a further entity called the Libra Consulting Services Trust that had been established by his mother.[122]
  6. The Applicant confirmed that his mother was also a signatory to Libra Consulting’s bank account with NAB and she would have had access to that bank account, but he could not recall if or when she had ever accessed it.[123]
  7. The Applicant contended that no amounts received by Libra Consulting should be attributed to him as assessable or taxable income. The Applicant referred to the fact that Libra Consulting was a company and a separate entity, such that receipts of that company should not be treated as derived by him. Even if he was the sole shareholder, director or secretary or otherwise had control of that company, that was not a basis for attributing amounts received by Libra Consulting to him as his personal income. There was no evidence of extraction of funds from the company by him. He accepted that amounts paid by Libra Consulting to him such as salary, dividends, loans or some other ‘personal application’ could be attributed to him as income. However he said that the Respondent had not provided evidence of any such tracing or allocation of amounts to him.[124]
  8. The Respondent made an overarching submission that the Applicant had not proven the source and character of the ‘unexplained’ deposits received by Entities including Libra Consulting. The Respondent referred the Tribunal to certain instances where it appeared that the Applicant was accessing accounts of these Entities for non-business purposes, and the Applicant acknowledged that he might have used company funds for personal expenses, but only to a very limited degree. The Respondent also submitted that the Tribunal could not be satisfied on the evidence that the Applicant did not have taxable income arising from dividends or deemed dividends from Libra Consulting. From the limited information available the Respondent said that it was not possible to confirm whether the Applicant had used funds in the account of Libra Consulting (and other Entities) such that those amounts were effectively distributions or remuneration to him.[125]
  9. The Applicant agreed that Libra Consulting had not filed a tax return for any of the Relevant Years.[126] The Tribunal does not treat the absence of tax returns for Libra Consulting as necessarily indicative of the Applicant having derived or received particular amounts. However had contemporaneous and reliable accounting and tax records or lodgements for Libra Consulting for each of the Relevant Years been provided to the Tribunal, this might have assisted the Applicant in discharging his burden of proof, by potentially demonstrating a basis for attribution consistent with his submissions. The Tribunal repeats that the Respondent does not have the burden of ‘proving’ the accuracy of the Default Assessments, whether in terms of the attribution of amounts to the Applicant or the quantum of such amounts. It is therefore not required to provide evidence of tracing or allocation.
  10. The Applicant provided a set of Accounts for Libra Consulting for each of the Relevant Years.[127] For the 2008 to 2011 these Accounts included a balance sheet, trading statement and profit and loss statement which explained to the Tribunal had been prepared by his mother using accounting software for the purposes of lodgement with regulators. Therefore, the Accounts for the 2008 to 2011 years appear to be contemporaneous with those years. From the 2012 to 2016 years the Applicant had prepared a profit and loss style reconciliation of amounts identified in the bank account statements, with the Applicant identifying amounts which he believed were properly characterised as income, those which should be regarded ‘non-income’ and expenses against income. The Accounts for the 2008 to 2011 years identify ‘undistributed profits’ which were ‘not allocated’ in each of those years, with the amount of such undistributed profits increasing across those years. Loans from ‘directors’ are also identified as a liability of Libra Consulting. They also identify loans made to Virgin Investments. The Tribunal has reviewed this information and although it might indicate that cash dividends were not paid to shareholders in those years it does not provide any assistance in relation to deemed dividends such as those arising from loans with entities or businesses associated with the Applicant. The subsequent Accounts for the 2012 to 2016 years do not address retained profits, shareholder funds or similar matters.
  11. The Tribunal agrees with the proposition that ordinarily the existence of the ‘corporate veil’ means that a shareholder or officeholder would not be treated as deriving amounts received into the corporate entity. However in circumstances where the Applicant bears the burden of proof, he has not provided satisfactory evidence about his access to and the use of the bank account of Libra Consulting into which amounts were received, nor elaborated on matters which might give rise to dividend or deemed dividend amounts, including amounts labelled as distributions or loans from ‘LCS’ to businesses or entities associated with him.[128] Attribution on that basis would not necessarily be inconsistent with ‘beneficial’ entitlement to amounts or application of amounts for the benefit of the Applicant as described in case authorities cited by him, or entitlement deemed by statute. The Applicant has not demonstrated any basis for non-attribution of amounts arising in relation to Libra Consulting for the purposes of any of the Default Assessments.
  12. The next question is then whether the Accounts may assist in an understanding of the ‘quantum’ of the Applicant’s taxable income in any of the Relevant Years. In some of the Relevant Years the Applicant indicated that a loss would have resulted in Libra Consulting. In other Relevant Years the Applicant’s Accounts suggested a ‘profit’ in Libra Consulting that was less than the amount attributed to him by the Respondent in the relevant Default Assessment. In the 2013 year the profit for Libra Consulting suggested by the Applicant’s calculated appeared to exceed the amount attributed to him by the Respondent for that year.
  13. A question arose as to the basis on which the Accounts for the 2008 year showed ‘nil’ profit or loss with income and expenses being identical. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he appreciated this seemed unusual but he had re-checked the figures.[129] The Tribunal observes that this only occurred in the 2008 year and therefore does not draw any adverse inference on the reliability of the Applicant’s Accounts based on this matter alone.
  14. However there were other issues identified which raised questions about the reliability of the Accounts in the context of demonstrating what the quantum of Applicant’s taxable income should be in any of the Relevant Years. First, certain amounts were described as ‘loans’ between Libra Consulting and other Entities or businesses including Virgin Investments and Success Coaching International. The Applicant acknowledged that he did not have loan or other documentation evidencing or explaining those loans, other than the description of these items in the Accounts or reconstructed financial outcomes for those other Entities.[130]
  15. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that he recalled a company car which he used for business purposes such as deliveries and not for private use.[131] The Tribunal has previously noted that although the Applicant had provided tax invoices, receipts or credit card statements identifying vehicle related expenditure such as repairs and maintenance and petrol, the nexus between that expenditure and any business conducted by the Applicant or an Entity with which he was associated was not clear. There were invoices for freeway tolls issued to Libra Consulting and the Tribunal would be willing to place some weight on those invoices in relation to the characterisation of those tolls as deductible in nature. However in most instances, even where a vehicle was identified by reference to its registration details, the Applicant had difficulty recalling which vehicles were used for any particular purpose, when they were used or when they might have been acquired or disposed of, making it difficult for the Tribunal to relate such expenditure to any of the ‘MVs’ [motor vehicles] referenced in the Accounts.[132]
  16. Again, the Tribunal is unable to find that the Applicant has provided evidence to support a finding that any of the Default Assessments should have been of a particular amount that is less than what the Respondent had assessed. The Accounts provided or prepared by the Applicant, although addressing some details relevant to a determination of taxable income, were not supported by satisfactory evidence.

Aussie Speeding Fines

  1. A PayPal account was identified by the Respondent in the name of Aussie Speeding Fines, into which substantial amounts were received in each of the Relevant Years. A second PayPal account was identified by the Respondent in the name of Libra Consulting ‘trading as Aussie Speeding Fines’ into which amounts were received in the 2013, 2014, 2015 and 2016 years.[133]
  2. On the basis of information obtained from PayPal in response to a Division 353 notice, the Respondent formed the view that the Applicant was the account owner for each of these accounts, and in the absence of information about any other account owners the Respondent would assess the Applicant on the full amount of deposits into those accounts.[134]
  3. The documents provided by PayPal indicate the following:
    (a) A copy of an ASIC ‘business name extract’ confirms that ‘Aussie Speeding Fines’ was a business name registered to Libra Consulting commencing from July 2012.[135]

    (b) In relation to account ‘7643’ this account was held in the name of Libra Consulting trading as Aussie Speeding Fines. The named account user is ‘Mike Palmer’ with the same date of birth as the Applicant, and he has been nominated as the Primary Authorised User and contact person. Various addresses are provided for different periods in the contact details, but the suburban Melbourne address identified by the Respondent as the residential address of the Applicant was stated multiple times.[136]

    (c) In relation to account ‘7290’ this account was held in the name of ‘Aussie Speeding Fines’. Numerous contact addresses were provided for different periods in the contact details, in Australia, the USA and UK. Those contact addresses also refer to multiple individuals other than the Applicant, although it is not clear in what capacity they are named. This account appears to have been created in 2007, which is some time prior to the registration by ASIC of the Aussie Speeding Fines name to Libra Consulting.[137]

  4. Also included with this section of the bundle of material from PayPal is a statement from June 2012 for a bank account with NAB in the name of the Applicant’s mother, into which amounts have been transferred from a PayPal account. The relationship between this bank account and Aussie Speeding Fines (if any) was not made clear to the Tribunal.[138]
  5. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the activities of Aussie Speeding Fines included providing information, DVDs, CDs, books, courses and other online forums for members of the community. He said that not all payments received into its PayPal accounts represented sales income, but could also include donations or other amounts by way of assistance.[139] As mentioned, Libra Consulting had previously operated a transcription business but subsequently moved into the activities of the Aussie Speeding Fines business.[140]
  6. The Applicant explained that there were two PayPal accounts for Aussie Speeding Fines, with one being the main account and the other being a members account.[141]
  7. In addition to the Accounts prepared for Libra Consulting, the Applicant provided copies of monthly PayPal sales reports for Aussie Speeding Fines[142] and his notes summarising PayPal transaction information for 2015 and 2016, including sales, refunds and credits, fees and fee refunds.[143] These notes also identified subsequent transfers of amounts to Libra Consulting’s bank account.
  8. The legal status of Aussie Speeding Fines for the 2008 to 2012 income years was not clear to the Tribunal. The ASIC business name extracts indicate that this business name was only registered to Libra Consulting from 1 July 2012. The PayPal account ‘7290’, being the account identified by the Respondent as being the repository for substantial amounts attributed to the Applicant, appears to have been opened well before July 2012.
  9. The Applicant was either unable or unwilling to provide further clarification of the materials provided to the Tribunal as they related to Aussie Speeding Fines, or explanation about the activities or status of Aussie Speeding Fines, particularly before 2012. In the absence of any evidence or explanation provided by the Applicant, the Tribunal cannot find that he has discharged any burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessments insofar as they incorporate amounts arising from the Aussie Speeding Fines PayPal accounts. For the 2013 year and subsequent Relevant Years, the Tribunal makes findings aligned to its other findings for Libra Consulting. For the Relevant Years prior to the 2013 year the Tribunal does not have any information which would displace the Respondent’s position relating to attribution of amounts to the Applicant, or the quantum of amounts attributed to him.

Virgin Investments

  1. A bank account with NAB was identified by the Respondent held in the name of Virgin Investments. Amounts were identified as received into this bank account in the 2008, 2009 and 2010 years.[144]
  2. The Respondent said that the Applicant was the sole director, secretary and shareholder of this company. The Respondent’s position in the Objection Decision was that the Applicant controlled this bank account. Therefore the Respondent assessed the Applicant on the full amount of any deposits into this bank account.
  3. Information obtained from NAB under the Division 353 notice did not identify any signatory to the bank account, which had been closed in February 2010. NAB did provide copies of bank statements for the account ranging in date from June 2007 to February 2010. One statement records transfers of $11,000 being ‘per Mike.’[145]
  4. A company search obtained by the Respondent in May 2017[146] indicated that Virgin Investments was deregistered in July 2012, and that the Applicant had been the sole director and secretary of that company from October 2004 until its deregistration in July 2012. Therefore the Applicant was the sole director and secretary of Virgin Investments in the first five of the Relevant Years (2008 to 2012).[147]
  5. The Applicant told the Tribunal that due to the number of years that had elapsed he had difficulty recalling details of this company, including dates relating to his involvement, although he did believe that Virgin Investments had previously filed its own tax returns at some time and intended to file returns if required to. The Respondent’s counsel put to the Applicant that Virgin Investments had not filed any tax returns between 1 July 2007 and 30 June 2016, and the Applicant indicated that was probably right. [148]
  6. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he had not received a wage or other remuneration from Virgin Investments.[149]
  7. As with Libra Consulting, the issue of the ‘corporate veil’ arises in relation to Virgin Investments. The Tribunal understands the Applicant’s submissions in relation to Virgin Investments to be similar to those made in relation to Libra Consulting, being that his status as a shareholder or officeholder of Virgin Investments was not a basis for attribution of amounts to him. Again, in addition to submitting that the Applicant had not proven the source and character of ‘unexplained’ deposits in the bank account of Virgin Investments, the Respondent said the Tribunal could not be satisfied on the evidence that the Applicant did not have taxable income in the form of dividends or deemed dividends from this company, of which he was the director and shareholder.
  8. The Respondent’s counsel asked the Applicant a number of questions relating to his access to the bank account. The Respondent contended that the Applicant had used funds in the bank account of Virgin Investment and had acknowledged that some of this was for private use. The bank statements provided by NAB show this account being used to pay a Diners Club card.[150]
  9. There was also discussion about payments made from the bank account of Virgin Investments in 2017 to an entity ‘Bluestone Group.’ The Applicant confirmed that he had ‘access’ to that account but said that these amounts related to mortgage payments on the property in suburban Melbourne. That property was the one said by the Respondent to be his (now former) residential address. The Applicant said that Virgin Investments ‘had the house’ and rented it out to a third party but otherwise it did ‘nothing.’[151] The Applicant noted that litigation between Virgin Investments and a financier relating to foreclosure under a mortgage of the property was a matter of public record.[152]
  10. The Applicant expressed a strong view that ‘involvement’ in such a company was not the ‘statutory test for attribution.’[153] The Tribunal acknowledges that Virgin Investments, as a company, is a separate entity to the Applicant. However the Applicant has been placed in circumstances where he bears the burden of proving the Default Assessments incorrect. He was not able to explain to the Tribunal’s satisfaction his apparent access to and use of the bank account of Virgin Investments over a number of years, in circumstances where he said that he did not receive any recognised form of remuneration, dividends or other benefits from that entity. The Tribunal again notes transactions described as ‘distributions’ to Virgin Investments from Libra Consulting in the 2008 and 2009 years which the Applicant was unable to explain.[154]
  11. As with Libra Consulting, in circumstances where the Applicant bears the burden of proof, he has not provided satisfactory evidence about his access to and the use of the bank account of Virgin Investments into which amounts were received, nor elaborated on matters which might give rise to dividend or deemed dividend amounts. The Applicant has not demonstrated any basis for non-attribution of amounts arising in relation to Virgin Investments for the purposes of any of the Default Assessments.
  12. The Applicant prepared a Profit & Loss Statement and Annual Accounts for Virgin Investments for the 2008,[155] 2009[156] and 2010[157] years. He explained to the Tribunal that he had prepared these from bank statements and sales reports he had been able to retrieve. He agreed that one amount of $96,833 recorded as an expense for this company in the 2008 year represented mortgage interest only payments for the suburban Melbourne property.[158] He also identified in the Profit & Loss Statements other expenses referable to a rental property, such as insurances, rates, cleaning, repairs and maintenance and security. Also recorded in the Profit & Loss Statements were ‘distributions’ from Libra Consulting Services and reversals of bank charges and debit. He said that on this basis Virgin Investments had made a substantial loss in the 2008 year and a more modest profit in the 2009 year.
  13. The Respondent contended that there was a lack of evidence about the mortgage repayments said to have been made by Virgin Investments, and in particular what portion of those payments were interest and whether they might include capital repayments.[159] The Applicant referred to having adduced specific evidence about the interest only nature of the mortgage loan repayments made by Virgin Investments, such evidence not having been displaced by any contrary evidence from the Respondent.[160] The Tribunal’s view is that the Applicant made assertions about the nature of these payments, said that matters relating to the property were on the public record and told the Tribunal that he should be able to obtain supporting evidence. However there was no evidence before the Tribunal of these matters. Nor was the Respondent required to provide evidence to displace these assertions and statements.
  14. In any event, the matters relating to mortgage repayments are subsumed by the broader question of whether the Applicant has met his burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessments.
  15. The Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicant took significant steps towards meeting his burden of proof, insofar as the Default Assessments related to amounts attributed from Virgin Investments, by providing information in the form of the Profit & Loss Statements and Annual Accounts about amounts received and expended by Virgin Investments for the 2008, 2009 and 2010 years. The Tribunal also agrees with the Applicant that some of the debate during cross examination at the hearing over selected items, often of smaller amounts, did not advance a firm understanding of what the correct taxable income figure should be. However the Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant’s reconstructed financial outcomes were sufficient to discharge his burden of proof. Limited evidence of payments having been made or received by an entity is not enough of itself. Even where the Tribunal was satisfied that there was evidence of those payments having occurred, the Tribunal would require evidence or analysis about the nature and character of the payments sufficient to displace the treatment given by the Respondent in the Default Assessments.
  16. In the case of Virgin Investments, the Tribunal could not be satisfied from the profit and loss statements and annual account summaries provided by the Applicant as to whether any amounts had been distributed to him or he had otherwise received a benefit from that company which might be regarded as a deemed dividend. In relation to the 2008 and 2009 years, the Tribunal can accept that some expense items identified by the Applicant are likely to have been deductible,[161] offsetting receipts attributed to him by the Respondent. The Tribunal was not provided with satisfactory evidence or analysis relating to the characterisation for taxation law purposes of certain significant items, such as the mortgage payments, distributions from other entities,[162] rent said to have been paid by Success Coaching International or amounts described as relating to legal matters or bank disputes. No supporting or explanatory information was provided about the 2010 year.
  17. The Tribunal accepts that it is likely that certain of the Default Assessments were ‘incorrect’ insofar as they related to the detail of amounts attributed from Virgin Investments, but cannot be satisfied that they were excessive or as to what they should have been.

Freedom Foundation

  1. A bank account was identified by the Respondent held in the name of Freedom Foundation. Significant amounts were identified as received into this bank account for the 2013 to 2016 years.[163] A PayPal account was also identified in the name of Freedom Foundation with amounts being received into this account in the 2015 and 2016 years.[164]
  2. Based on information obtained from the bank, the Respondent said that the Applicant was the sole account holder and signatory to the bank account. Therefore the Respondent had assessed the Applicant on the (full) amount of any deposits into this bank account in the Relevant Years.
  3. Information provided by PayPal to the Respondent in August 2017 identified a ‘Michael Palmer’ with the same birth date as the Applicant as the primary authorised user for the PayPal account in the name of Freedom Foundation. Three addresses were recorded in the contact details for different periods, however they included the suburban Melbourne address previously identified by the Respondent as the Applicant’s residential address and a GPO Box associated with him.[165]
  4. The Respondent told the Tribunal that Freedom Foundation had previously self-assessed as tax-exempt, but that the Respondent did not hold any other information about that Entity. The Applicant said that Freedom Foundation was a ‘separate entity’ and that his understanding was that, according to the Respondent, private foundations were tax-exempt.[166] He also told the Tribunal that he ‘didn’t have a lot to do with the Freedom Foundation’ in terms of its day-to-day operations.[167]
  5. Account information obtained from Bendigo Bank in July 2017 indicated that the Applicant was the sole signatory to the bank account and the ‘account owner’ as at that date.[168] The bank also provided a copy of the ‘Customer Application’ form for the bank account, dated around August 2012, which had been completed and signed by the Applicant as ‘Applicant 1’ (for the bank account) and by Ms A as ‘Applicant 2.’ That form contained an acknowledgement term to the effect that where an account was in joint names, the money in the account was owned jointly by those persons. There was also provision for ‘signing instructions’ with the default position being that either of the joint account owners could sign for withdrawals from the account unless otherwise specified. In this case the box ‘one to sign’ was ticked. It was not made clear to the Tribunal whether this represented an election by the Applicant and Ms A that only the Applicant was able to sign, or whether it was intended to convey that only one of the two account owners (that is, either of the Applicant or Ms A) were able to sign, as per the default position.[169]
  6. Also included with the documents provided by Bendigo Bank was a copy of the ‘Know Your Customer’ information form which had to be completed at the time of opening the bank account. Attached to this form was an undated copy of the Articles of Association of the Freedom Foundation which named the Applicant and Ms A as the Founding Principal Participants, and a copy of minutes of a Principal Participants Meeting on 1 April 2012. A copy of minutes of a further Principal Participants Meeting on 14 October 2013 indicates that Ms A was removed as a Principal Participant and that the bank was directed to remove Ms A from the foundation’s bank account. The minute then recorded that ‘in order to comply with legal requirements’ the Applicant’s mother was to be ‘added as a second, silent participant’ but was to have no access to the bank account of the foundation or any responsibility or liability in relation to the foundation.[170]
  7. The Articles of Association and minutes of meeting state that the Freedom Foundation is a ‘Private Foundation, not-for-profit, tax-exempt, non-government organisation’ or a ‘humanitarian not-for-profit, non-government-organisation.’ Its terms provided that the Principal Participants had the ‘sole discretion and responsibility to determine all the activities, enterprises and operation’ of the foundation: Article Six. Article Eight provides that the foundation operated in privacy and would not reveal any private information relating to is operation to any public or government agency or any purported legal authority. Article Nine provides that any account held with any bank or financial institution into which assets of the foundation were deposited was to be operated by the Principal Participants[171] and held in the name of the foundation. Article Ten provides that:
    ‘No part of the funding or net earnings of the Foundation shall inure to the personal benefit of, or be distributable to its Principal Participants, members, office holders or other natural people, except that the Principal Participants shall be authorized and empowered to pay reasonable compensation and provide sustenance for all people who render services to the Foundation or its enterprises, whether they be Members, Participants, volunteers or contractors. These compensations and sustenance are at the discretion of the Principal Participants, and may include, but are not limited to living costs, travel costs, food expenses, office expenses, donations and so forth...’
  8. Article Ten goes on to specify that complete and accurate accounting and financial records of the foundation are to be kept and made available by the Principal Participants to specified persons but must not be made available to any public or government body or agency.
  9. The Tribunal accepts that at least when the Bendigo Bank account was first opened in 2012, amounts in that account were described as being owned jointly by the Applicant and Ms A, either in their own capacity or on behalf of the foundation. In July 2017, the Bendigo Bank identified the Applicant as the sole signatory and account holder. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the bank’s information in its letter to the Respondent of July 2017 was ‘absolutely categorically incorrect’ and ‘false and misleading’ which is why he had subsequently sought to obtain the ‘actual bank opening statements’ to prove this matter.[172] However the Tribunal believes that the forms relating to the opening of that bank account had been provided by Bendigo Bank. These are the Customer Application Form and the Know Your Client Information form dated from August 2012, together with the copy of the Articles of Association and minute of the April 2012 for the Freedom Foundation.[173] Further, the bank provided a copy of the minute of the October 2013 meeting of the foundation, at which Ms A was removed as a signatory and account owner and no replacement or additional signatory or account owner was nominated, with it being expressly stated that Mrs Palmer was not to take on these roles. The Tribunal believes relevant documentary evidence has been provided, and it is consistent with the position that in the 2015 to 2016 years, and for the significant part of the 2014 year, the Applicant was the sole signatory and nominated ‘account owner.’ However the Tribunal also believes that this material is consistent with the conclusion that Ms A was a joint signatory and account owner for the 2012 and 2013 year, and for a small part of the 2014 year. The Respondent does not appear to have attributed any amount from the Freedom Foundation’s bank account to the Applicant in the 2012 year, but did so in the 2013 and 2014 years.
  10. Aside from his assertion that the material provided by the Bendigo Bank relating to the Freedom Foundation was incorrect or incomplete, the Applicant did not provide any further evidence or explanation of the ownership or operation of the bank account. In the context of forming any further understanding of the Freedom Foundation and the Applicant’s relationship with it, the Tribunal could not find any further documentation relating to the establishment of the Freedom Foundation within the material filed other than as set out above. The Tribunal did not believe it was appropriate to undertake its own research, such as Google searches of websites referenced by the Applicant, on evidentiary or factual matters.
  11. The Tribunal has considered the Articles of Association establishing the Freedom Foundation. It is difficult to neatly categorise this entity as a trust, unincorporated association or other entity based on the Articles, however such an exercise may not be particularly useful for the purposes of the Tribunal’s decision in this matter as this application does not involve the taxation treatment of the Freedom Foundation.[174] The Tribunal observes that while the Articles provide that no part of the funding or earnings of the foundation is to inure to the personal benefit of or be distributed to a Principal Participant, nevertheless the Principal Participants have a wide ranging discretion to pay amounts including ‘compensation’ and ‘sustenance’ to any person who provides services to the foundation including a Participant. This is expressly stated to include amounts for the living expenses and other expenses of a personal nature. The Applicant agreed that some cash withdrawals from the Freedom Foundation bank account were for his personal expenses, but said that he could not recall exact dates or amounts due to the significant amount of time that had elapsed since those transactions. He also did not disagree that other expenses paid from the Freedom Foundation account such as rent and motor vehicles might be for personal purposes, although again he said it was difficult to recall the details of amounts in the bank statements.[175] However he believed that the use of amounts from that account for his personal purposes was occasional only. He also believed it was possible that other people had withdrawn funds from the account, but did not indicate who they might be.[176]
  12. From October 2013 until at least July 2017 when Bendigo Bank responded to the Division 353 notice, the Applicant was the sole (active) Principal Participant and sole signatory and account holder for the bank account. No evidence or explanation was provided by the Applicant of other Participants, Members or persons in receipt of financial support by the Freedom Foundation, despite the Applicant’s assertion that such people existed. As a Principal Participant of the Freedom Foundation, if such evidence existed it should have been accessible to him. The Tribunal is not convinced by references to privacy law constraining his provision of information.[177] If the Applicant felt constrained by the non-disclosure provisions in the Articles of Association, that is a matter for him to manage or raise with the Tribunal. The Tribunal rejects the Applicant’s contention that the Respondent should be responsible for providing further evidence of such other persons.
  13. The Tribunal largely agrees with the Applicant’s contention that ‘it’s not the tribunal’s role to consider who may have been other beneficiaries. We’re here to test attribution on the evidence.’[178] It is for the Applicant to demonstrate that the Default Assessments are excessive or incorrect and what they should have been. If the Applicant had provided evidence of other persons who might be owners of the bank account, or who might have been paid or benefited from (directly or indirectly) the bank account or the PayPal account the Tribunal might have accepted that the attribution by the Respondent of amounts from those accounts to the Applicant was incorrect. However the Applicant did not provide such evidence, and did not address the evidence that had been filed with the Tribunal in relation to Freedom Foundation. The Tribunal has no basis for overturning the Respondent’s attribution of amounts.
  14. It is therefore necessary for the Tribunal to consider whether the Applicant had otherwise demonstrated that any of the Default Assessments were incorrect and what they should have been through his alternative analysis of an appropriate ‘quantum’ of taxable income. The Applicant prepared certain statements of financial outcomes for the Freedom Foundation for the 2013 to 2016 years in the form of accounts summaries and expense sheets, together with copies of PayPal monthly sales reports.[179] By reference to amounts in the bank account, the Applicant identified which amounts received could be characterised as income (such as amounts from the conduct of events and from coaching activities, as well as rent received) and those which should not be treated as income (such as loans from Mrs Palmer and refunds of rent). The Applicant also identified which amounts paid could be characterised as deductible expenses (such as bank fees, rent paid and motor vehicle expenses). This was done on a year-by-year basis. The Applicant then said that any excess of income over expenses should be split between the two Principal Participants such that his taxable income in any of the 2013 to 2016 years was half of that excess.
  15. An initial issue identified by the Tribunal is that the basis for the Applicant only including half of any excess of income over expenses in his taxable income is doubtful. The Tribunal has found that although there were originally two Principal Participants of the Freedom Foundation, Ms A ceased to be a Principal Participant in October 2013. At that time Mrs Palmer was added as a ‘silent participant’ who had no access to the bank account of the foundation and was stated not to have any responsibility or liability to the foundation.
  16. A second issue is that the financial outcomes amounts provided by the Applicant essentially summarise amounts in the bank statements and sales reports. Minimal supporting information was provided in relation to the expenses identified. There are some items that the Tribunal is willing to accept as being, on the balance of probabilities, characterised as assessable income, non-assessable amounts or deductible expenses. Examples of this would include income from events and coaching services being assessable, small loans from the Applicant’s mother being non-assessable and bank fees being deductible. The Tribunal is not satisfied that there is sufficient evidence to support the deductibility of items labelled ‘MV [motor vehicle] expenses’ in circumstances where the Applicant could not recall or explain with any level of confidence which motor vehicles were concerned and what they were used for. Similarly there were multiple amounts received for ‘rent’ or ‘refund of rent’ and payments for ‘rent’ with no supporting evidence and the Applicant having difficulty explaining these amounts.[180] Even though the Tribunal can accept a nexus to ‘VRC event’ income, the Tribunal is also not satisfied that there is evidence supporting the deductibility of ‘VRC expenses.’ An amount of overseas travel is identified as an expense that would be deductible for the 2016 year, but no evidence was provided as to its nexus to any business or income earning activities.[181]
  17. The Tribunal is willing to accept, based on the evidence provided by the Respondent in the form of the material obtained from Bendigo Bank under the Division 353 notices, that the Default Assessments for the 2013 and 2014 years may be ‘incorrect’ in that they may include the entirety of amounts received into the bank account of the Freedom Foundation at a time when the Applicant was not the sole signatory or account holder. However, in the context of the Default Assessments in each of those years including amounts arising in relation to Entities other than the Freedom Foundation, it is not possible for the Tribunal to make any finding as to what either of those Default Assessments ‘should have been’ as a result of this matter alone. Nor has the Applicant provided evidence or explanation satisfactory to the Tribunal of what amounts should have been included in his taxable income relating to the Freedom Foundation, whether that amount should be ‘nil’ on the basis of attribution to him being incorrect or some other amount.

Put simply, the Tribunal was provided with very limited material in relation to the Know Your Rights Foundation and any transactions undertaken by it giving rise to the amounts in the bank statements and PayPal account summaries. The financial incomes amounts provided by the Applicant did little more than summarise amounts in those bank statements and sales reports, and no supporting information was provided in relation to the expenses identified. In these circumstances the Tribunal does not have a basis for displacing any of the Default Assessments insofar as they relate to amounts attributed from the Know Your Rights Foundation.

Golden Tara Foundation

  1. Three bank accounts were identified by the Respondent held in the name of Golden Tara Foundation. Amounts were identified as received into two of these bank accounts in the 2015 and 2016 years.[193]
  2. The Respondent said that it obtained information from the bank indicating that the Applicant was ‘the signatory’ to these bank accounts. Absent details of any other account holders or any other information about these accounts, the Respondent assessed the Applicant on the full amount of deposits into those bank accounts in the Relevant Years.[194]
  3. The Applicant told the Tribunal that Golden Tara Foundation was nothing to do with him. He said that he had helped another person (Ms B) set up the bank accounts, and that was why he was named as an account holder.[195]
  4. In response to a Division 353 notice sent by the Respondent, Bendigo Bank provided account information for one bank account held in the name of Golden Tara Foundation including a copy of a document titled the ‘Articles of Association’ of the Golden Tara Foundation.[196] These Articles of Association named Ms B and the Applicant as the ‘Founding Principal Participants’ and was apparently signed by both of them. A Minute of a ‘Principal Participants Meeting’ dated 16 July 2014 and which was stated to have been held in Frankston in Melbourne noted that it was agreed by the Principal Participants that Ms B and the Applicant would be the authorised signatories on the bank accounts of the Golden Tara Foundation. This minute was also apparently signed by the parties.[197]
  5. Also in response to a Division 353 notice, Westpac provided account information for two bank accounts held in the name of Golden Tara Foundation. It confirmed that the Applicant was a signatory on both of those accounts.[198] The Applicant was asked about a deposit of $5,000 into one of these bank accounts in June 2016. He was adamant that he had not deposited that amount and otherwise had nothing to do with the account, having only assisted Ms B to set up the account as a minimum of two people were required.[199]
  6. The Articles of Association for Golden Tara Foundation are in a similar form to those of the Freedom Foundation. They (and the minute of the Principal Participants Meeting in July 2014) clearly identify the Applicant as one of two Founding Principal Participants and a co-signatory to the bank accounts. The Articles of Association also provide for ‘compensation’ and ‘sustenance’ to be provided to various people, including Participants, which may cover living and other costs of a personal nature. However the bank statements issued by both Westpac and Bendigo to Golden Tara Foundation identify a street address and PO box address that had not otherwise been identified as a residential address of the Applicant. Very few transactions are recorded on the Westpac account and with the exception of some larger transactions in June 2016 and January 2017, they were of small amounts. The Bendigo Bank account was largely inactive during the periods shown in the bank statements.
  7. Based on the documentary and oral evidence provided the Tribunal was less convinced of the basis for attributing amounts received into Golden Tara Foundation’s accounts to the Applicant. This is not because the Tribunal would have required the Respondent to ‘prove the correctness’ of the assessments as they related to this entity, but because:
    (a) The documents provided by Bendigo Bank in response to the Division 353 notice include the copy of the Articles of Association and minute of the Principal Participants Meeting which identify two Founding Principal Participants who were also the designated account signatories. Unlike Freedom Foundation there was no further documentation indicating any alteration of that position such that the Applicant had since become the sole participant or account signatory.

    (b) The Tribunal might have been able to place more weight the Applicant’s evidence on oath that any involvement he had with this entity was of an administrative nature, largely at the time that the entity was established, such that it could be found, on the balance of probabilities, that none of the amounts received by this Entity should be attributed to him (that is, a nil amount of income).

  8. The Tribunal’s view is that only half of the amounts received by Golden Tara Foundation in any of the Relevant Years should be attributed to the Applicant, on the same basis that the Respondent previously applied in the Objection Decision in relation to Know Your Rights Foundation. However although the Tribunal might have some doubts or questions about the basis for attribution, it did not have sufficient information to find, on the balance of probabilities, against the Respondent’s position beyond this apportionment. Further as noted above, the Default Assessments encompass a range of amounts beyond those relating to Golden Tara Foundation, such that even if the Tribunal determined that the Applicant had demonstrated that nil amounts should be attributed to him in relation to Golden Tara Foundation, he would need to do more to discharge his burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessments generally.

He did not offer any explanation beyond the reference to the existence of a website as above.[210]

Again the Tribunal notes that the amounts involved are relatively minor.

  1. The Applicant raised a concern that the Respondent had effectively included the same amounts twice in the Default Assessments. That is, an amount received into a PayPal account in the name of an Entity which was then transferred to a bank account in the name of that Entity represented only one amount received and the Applicant should not be attributed with income from both the PayPal and bank account. The Applicant asked whether the Respondent had evidence to show that amounts were not counted more than once in the same year, or in different years.[229] The Applicant had contended that the Respondent had admitted to double counting amounts and this was evidence of ‘fraudulent accounting’ by the Respondent, unlawful and went to the correctness of the Default Assessments. The Applicant also filed ‘Annexure C – PayPal Data Gap Inventory’ and ‘Annexure D – Illustrative Double-Counting Mechanism’ as annexures to his Submissions for Substantive Hearing[230] as well as a document containing his notes on potential ‘double accounting’ which appeared to refer to certain transfers of funds between Entities.[231]

The Respondent’s counsel also took the Applicant and Tribunal to statements of accounts in the name of Libra Consulting by way of example to explain why the Respondent did not believe there had been double counting on its part.[232]
  3. The Tribunal does not agree that the Respondent ‘admitted’ any double counting of amounts. In this regard the Applicant referred to a paragraph of the Respondent’s SFIC.[233] It is clear to the Tribunal that the paragraph of the Respondent’s SFIC does not contain any admission or concession by the Respondent, but is the Respondent’s summary of its understanding of the Applicant’s position or contentions. To suggest that this represented an admission of error or even fraud by the Respondent is misleading and the Applicant is reminded of his obligations under section 56 of the ART Act.
  4. In the context of the Default Assessments and the Applicant’s burden of proof the Respondent is not required to provide evidence to disprove the Applicant’s allegations and the Tribunal is not satisfied that the Applicant has provided evidence or analysis supporting his claims. The ‘Annexure C’ document is a two page document summarising the Applicant’s contentions or complaints that the Respondent did not provide him with all of the PayPal data to which he believes he was entitled. The ‘Annexure D’ document criticises aspects of the Respondent’s approach in reaching the quantum of the Default Assessments, including the Respondent having not provided a ‘reconciliation’ of amounts. Annexure D also contains an ‘illustrative example’ stating that if a customer paid $500 into a PayPay account and that amount was then transferred into a linked account, the Applicant or Entity should not be treated as having received or derived $1000.
  5. The Tribunal agrees with this proposition as a matter of logic, however the Applicant did not demonstrate how this applied in his circumstances. The Tribunal has looked at particular transactions identified by the Applicant in relation to Freedom Foundation[234] but is not satisfied that this proves double counting either in relation to that Entity or more widely. For example, in relation to the 2013 and 2014 years, the Applicant suggests that amounts were double counted when it is clear that the Respondent did not seek to attribute separately or additionally to the Applicant as income any amounts received into the PayPal account of Freedom Foundation (account ended ‘9105’).[235] The gross or ‘All PayPal accounts’ amount recognised as income for each of those years (as added to bank account amounts) related to PayPal accounts in the name of other Entities or persons.[236] In relation to the 2015 and 2016 years, the Applicant refers to bank statements confirming double counting but it is not clear which particular items or amounts he believes evidence double counting.

ADMINISTRATIVE PENALTIES AND THE PENALTY ASSESSMENTS

Relevant law and guidance

  1. Subdivision 284-C in Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 contains provisions for administrative penalties relating to ‘statements.’ This includes the imposition and calculation of penalties for making of false or misleading statements made by a taxpayer to the Respondent or in circumstances where the Respondent determines a tax-related liability in circumstances where the taxpayer has not provided required documents: section 284-70. Section 284-75 sets out various bases upon which a person will be liable for a penalty. Sections 284-85 and 284-90 contain provisions for working out the amount of the penalty to be imposed. In particular, the Items in Table in subsection 284-90(1) establish how the ‘base penalty amount’ is to be determined for the particular circumstances in which penalties may be imposed.
  2. The base penalty amount may be increased or reduced in accordance with Subdivision 284-D. This includes circumstances where penalties have been imposed on the taxpayer across multiple years: see subsection 284-220(1).
  3. However under subsection 298-20(1) in Schedule 1, where a penalty has been imposed on a person ‘The [Respondent] may remit all or a part of the penalty.’ In the context of a review by the Tribunal of an objection decision relating to a penalty assessment, the Tribunal may exercise that power to remit.
  4. The TAA 1953 does not prescribe particular matters which the Respondent (or Tribunal) must take into account when considering whether to remit all or a part of a penalty, although there is case authority which suggests that the Tribunal should consider whether it is appropriate in ‘all the circumstances’ to remit all or part of the penalty[237] and further that the discretion to remit must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 1953.[238]

Imposition of an administrative penalty

  1. The Tribunal is unable to provide legal advice or assistance to a party to a proceeding, including in circumstances where a party does not have separate legal representation and is not legally qualified themselves. However the Tribunal ‘may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it considers appropriate’ and must also ensure that each party to a proceeding in the Tribunal is given ‘a reasonable opportunity’ to present their case, make submissions and adduce evidence.[239] The Tribunal believed that the Respondent’s SFIC and written opening submissions, especially when read together with the Objection Decision, set out the Respondent’s position on penalties in fairly clear and ‘plain English’ terms.[240] However as the hearing progressed it became unclear to the Tribunal whether the Applicant did not understand the position and submissions of the Respondent relating to penalties, or whether he had made a conscious choice to approach the issue of penalties in a different manner. For that reason before the parties made their oral arguments on the topic of penalties, the Tribunal explained to the Applicant certain matters to which the Tribunal would give consideration. In particular the Tribunal said to the parties:[241]
    So there are sort of two ways of approaching, two things that I need to consider in relation to the penalties: ‘Was the Commissioner right the first place when they imposed them in those amounts?’ and the second thing is, ‘Even if we accept that the penalties were correctly imposed, is there a basis for them being remitted?’ So that’s what I’m listening for when I listen to the two sets of submissions here. So Ms Wong, have I mischaracterised anything or does my summary just cause you any concerns? I just want to make sure that we sort of have clarity around the two different aspects of penalties that we need to look at, which I think were set out quite clearly in the outline of submissions but I just want to draw them to Mr Palmer’s attention.
  2. The Respondent had put forward two alternative bases for the imposition of administrative penalties under Division 284, Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953. Those two alternative bases result in the same amount of penalty being imposed on the Applicant. The Tribunal also wanted to confirm that the Applicant appreciated that two alternative bases were being put for imposition of administrative penalties, which were found in different statutory provisions.[242]
  3. The two bases were as follows:
    (a) Subsection 284-75(3) provides that a person is liable to an administrative penalty if they ‘fail to give a return, notice or other document to the [Respondent] by the day it is required to be given’, the document is necessary for the Respondent to determine a tax-related liability of the person accurately and the Respondent determines the tax-related liability of the person without the assistance of the document. The Respondent says that the Applicant failed to lodge an income tax return in any of the Relevant Years, such that the Respondent had to issue default assessments under section 167 of the ITAA 1936.

    (b) Alternatively, subsection 284-75(1) provides that a person is liable to an administrative penalty if they ‘make a statement to the [Respondent] ... and ... the statement is false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it.’

  4. In relation to the amount of such penalty, the Respondent submitted that:[243]
    (a) Item 7 of the Table in subsection 284-90(1) provides that where a penalty is imposed under subsection 284-75(3), the base penalty amount is 75% of the tax-related liability concerned. That base penalty amount was to be increased by 20% in each of the Relevant Years other than the 2008 year because the Applicant had been previously liable to a penalty under subsection 284-75(3): see paragraph 284-220(1)(e).

    (b) Item 1 of the Table provides that where there is a shortfall amount as a result of a false or misleading statement as described in subsection 284-75(1) and that shortfall amount (or part of the shortfall amount) resulted from ‘intentional disregard of a taxation law’, the base penalty amount is 75% of the shortfall amount (or part thereof). In this context, and in addition to the Applicant not having lodged returns, the Respondent referred to the Applicant having indicated to the Respondent that it was not necessary for him to lodge income tax returns in the Relevant Years, which in turn suggested that he did not carry on certain activities and did not have any taxable income.[244] The Respondent viewed the Applicant’s behaviour in relation to each of the Relevant Years as demonstrating ‘intentional disregard’ rather than less culpable behaviour such as ‘recklessness’ or ‘failure to take reasonable care’ which would have attracted a base penalty amount of 50% or 25% respectively under Items 2 and 3 of the Table. The base penalty amount was to be further increased by 20% in each of the Relevant Years other than the 2008 year because a base penalty amount had previously been worked out for the Applicant under Item 1: see paragraph 284-220(1)(c).

  5. The Respondent submitted to the Tribunal that the Applicant also bore the same burden of proof under section 14ZZK in relation to the Penalty Assessments as he did in relation to the Default Assessments.[245]
  6. The Applicant submitted that:
    (a) Penalties imposed under Division 284 are calculated by reference to a shortfall amount. A shortfall amount arises where the tax-related liability (as properly determined) exceeds the amount of tax returned or assessed. Where there is no shortfall amount there is no basis for the imposition of a penalty.[246]

    (b) On the basis that the evidence before the Tribunal it was not possible to conclude that the Applicant was a resident of Australia, that amounts were beneficially derived by the Applicant or that taxable income exceeding the tax-free threshold was demonstrated for any of the Relevant years. Therefore, no tax would have been payable and there was no shortfall amount arising to the Applicant in any of the Relevant Years.[247]

    (c) The Respondent had not identified clear particulars as to how the Applicant made a false or misleading statement, including a deemed statement arising from a failure to lodge an income tax return or how this produced a shortfall, for the purposes of subsection 284-75(3). The Respondent is required to establish the statutory elements for a penalty, including the existence of a shortfall amount, that the statement was false or misleading in a material particular and that it resulted in a (properly quantified) shortfall.[248]

    (d) The Applicant acknowledged that he bore the onus to demonstrate that the Penalty Assessments were excessive. However the Tribunal had to reach findings ‘on the evidence in relation to factual matters said to justify the imposition of penalties, including the elements of the relevant penalty provisions.’ The Applicant cited certain case authorities which he said supported the proposition that evidence was necessary to establish the factual elements supporting the imposition of penalties, and that an assumption relating to these elements was not sufficient.[249]

    (e) The evidence did not support any finding of recklessness or intentional disregard on the part of the Applicant.[250]

  7. The Tribunal agrees that subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) places the burden of proof on the Applicant in relation to the assessments issued to him imposing administrative penalties. That is, the Applicant was required to prove, on the balance of probabilities, that each Penalty Assessment was excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what that Penalty Assessment should have been.
  8. The Tribunal agrees in general with the Applicant’s contention that to the extent that a ‘shortfall amount’ does not exist, there would be no administrative penalty. For the purposes of subsection 284-75(1) and Items 1, 2 and 3 of the Table in subsection 284-90(1), a person may be ‘liable’ to a penalty for making a false or misleading statement, but there must be a ‘shortfall amount’ resulting from such a statement before a base penalty amount can be calculated.[251] However subsection 284-75(3) and Item 7 speak in terms of a ‘tax-related liability’ having been determined (rather than a shortfall amount resulting), although the Tribunal accepts that practically this still requires a deficiency between tax that has been paid and tax that should have been paid.
  9. It is also relatively uncontroversial that the Tribunal has to be satisfied on the evidence that the elements of the relevant penalty statutory provisions have been met before finding that a penalty was imposed correctly. This is subject to the qualification that the Applicant bears the burden of proof in this regard.
  10. However the difficulty for the Applicant is that he did not address the elements of either of subsections 284-75(1) or 284-75(3) satisfactorily. In particular, he did not address the elements of subsection 284-75(3) beyond contending that the Respondent had failed to property articulate a false or misleading statement or had failed to demonstrate how a failure to lodge a tax return gave rise to such a statement. To be clear, subsection 284-75(3) does not require a person to have made a false or misleading statement. False or misleading statements are the subject of a separate provision imposing administrative penalties, that being subsection 284-75(1).[252] What is required for subsection 284-75(3) to operate is that:
    (a) A person failed to give a return, notice or other document to the Respondent by the day it was required to be given. In the present case the Applicant the Tribunal is satisfied on the evidence that the Applicant did not file an income tax return in relation to any of the Relevant Years.

    (b) That document was necessary for the Respondent to determine a tax-related liability of that person accurately. The Tribunal is satisfied that the lodgement of an income tax return by the Applicant in each of the Relevant Years was necessary for the Respondent to determine the amount of any income tax payable by the Applicant in that year.

    (c) The Respondent determined the tax-related liability without the assistance of that document. The Tribunal is satisfied that the Respondent issued an assessment under section 167 of the ITAA 1936 to the Applicant for each of the Relevant Years, being the Default Assessments, from which the Penalty Assessments resulted.

  11. For these reasons the elements of subsection 284-75(3) have been met in each of the Relevant Years, such that the Applicant is liable to an administrative penalty under this provision in each of the Relevant Years.
  12. The Applicant told the Tribunal ‘I did not lodge income tax returns for eleven years.’[253] Although it is the fact of non-lodgement that is relevant rather than the intention behind it the Tribunal observes that despite the numerous filings and three days of hearing, it was never made entirely clear to the Tribunal why the Applicant did not file an income tax return in any of the Relevant Years. It was not clear when (or even if) the Applicant had formed a view that he was not a resident of Australia for taxation purposes. Even if he had believed he was not a resident of Australia, he would still have been required to disclose as assessable any income with an Australian source and his status as a sole trader in relation to Success Coaching International, at a minimum, would have been relevant in this regard.
  13. The Tribunal is satisfied that even if the Applicant had not appreciated prior to the hearing the elements of subsection 284-75(3) and how it differed from subsection 284-75(1), the Respondent’s counsel explained these matters clearly during the hearing, at the Tribunal’s suggestion. After that explanation the Member summarised what she had heard from the Respondent’s counsel and suggested to the Applicant that it would be helpful if he could address the two alternative bases when he made his oral submissions. The Tribunal offered the Applicant a short break to gather his thoughts before making his submissions, and the hearing was adjourned briefly for this purpose.[254] Unfortunately he did not address the key elements of subsection 284-75(3), either in his oral submissions on penalties or in his closing written submissions (for which he also had the benefit of a copy of the transcript of the hearing).
  14. The Tribunal does not propose to reagitate matters which were dealt with in the previous interlocutory hearings, including topics which the Tribunal determined could not or would not be the subject of this review. However the Tribunal will refer to a couple of matters raised by the Applicant previously which might touch on subparagraph 284-75(3)(a). The Applicant had said that the Respondent had not demonstrated that he was within a class of persons required to lodge a tax return. The Tribunal rejected his argument that the Respondent was required to prove this matter, or that it was a matter relevant to a merits review by the Tribunal of an objection decision relating to an assessment under section 167.
  15. The Applicant also said he had not agreed to or authorised the Respondent or any other person to lodge an income tax return for him in any of the Relevant Years. This appears to be a reference to the income tax returns generated by the Respondent within its systems as part of the process for the issuance of the Default Assessments under section 167. The Respondent’s permission or authorisation is not required for the issue of assessments under section 167, or for any of the steps involved in issuing those assessments. Where the Respondent believed the Default Assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect he had rights of review under Part IVC of the TAA 1953, which he has exercised resulting in the current Tribunal proceeding. The Applicant returned to this point during the hearing, when he submitted that he had not made a false or misleading statement because the Respondent relied on returns it had prepared for the Relevant Years, rather than any returns which he had prepared (because he had not lodged any returns for those years).[255]
  16. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the Respondent was not correct in describing as ‘automatic’ the imposition of a penalty for non-lodgement at the rate of 75%. Once a person is found to be liable to a penalty under subsection 284-75(3), Item 7 of the Table in subsection 284-90(1) provides that the base penalty amount ‘is’ 75% of the tax-related liability concerned. This is different to the way in which the base penalty amount is calculated under Items 1, 2 or 3 in relation to a false or misleading statement, which then requires further consideration of whether the person demonstrated intentional disregard, recklessness or a failure to take reasonable care, and whether the shortfall amount or part of the shortfall amount resulted from a statement in those circumstances.[256] The Tribunal accepts that the use of the term ‘automatic’ by the Respondent’s counsel was intended to convey this difference between a penalty imposed under subsection 284-75(3) and one imposed under subsection 284-75(1).
  17. The Tribunal therefore finds that the Applicant was liable to an administrative penalty under subsection 284-75(3) in each of the Relevant Years, with the base penalty amount of such penalty being 75% of the tax-related liability concerned in the 2008 year and increased by 20% in each of the subsequent Relevant Years. The Tribunal is not satisfied that the Applicant has demonstrated, on the balance of probabilities, any basis for this conclusion being incorrect.
  18. Based on the finding that the Applicant is liable to an administrative penalty in each of the Relevant Years under subsection 284-75(3) for failing to lodge an income tax return in each of those years, it is not necessary for the Tribunal to consider the Respondent’s alternative basis for imposition of a penalty, being shortfall amounts resulting from making of false or misleading statements for the purposes of subsection 284-75(1) in circumstances of intentional disregard of a taxation law.
  19. However based on the Tribunal’s finding that the Applicant should only be assessed on one half of the amounts previously attributed to him as income in relation to Golden Tara Foundation and Inspiring Images Foundation, the ‘tax related liability’ upon which the base penalty amount is calculated in each of the 2015 and 2016 years will be reduced accordingly.

Should the administrative penalty be remitted?

  1. Finally, the Respondent submitted that there was no basis for remission under section 298-20, Schedule 1 of the TAA 1953 of any part of the penalty imposed in any of the Penalty Assessments. The Respondent said that the Applicant had not provided any evidence of circumstances which would support remission.
  2. The Applicant submitted, in the alternative, that if administrative penalties were to be imposed, they should be remitted in full or substantially having regard to the following matters:[257]
    (a) The age of the assessments.

    (b) The complexity of the issues, including the attribution issues.

    (c) The absence of clear beneficial ownership documentation.

    (d) The absence of ‘intentional disregard’, recklessness or deliberate evasion, concealment or obstruction on the part of the Applicant.

    (e) The Applicant’s participation and conduct in these proceedings, including his cooperation, provision of detailed supporting material and his structured reconstruction of the financial records available to him (across multiple entities and multiple years).

    (f) The Respondent’s partial reductions and inconsistencies.

    (g) Delays on the part of the Respondent in managing his review, audit and objection processes. The Applicant said that the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments were only issued around 2017, whereas they related to income years reaching back to 2008. In circumstances where records are ordinarily only required to be kept by law for up to five years, that delay was material to the Applicant and contributed to evidentiary limitations in this matter.

  3. The Applicant repeated matters raised in relation to his submissions on attribution of amounts as being relevant to the issue of penalty remission, including the Respondent’s position being based on ‘inferences drawn from incomplete or aggregated material’ rather than satisfactory evidence, the Respondent’s reliance on financial activity of separate legal and accounting entities which were not separately assessed, perceived deficiencies in the Respondent’s approach to beneficial derivation of amounts by the Applicant, the Applicant not being able to disclose information relating to other parties due to privacy laws (and which the Respondent should have been able to obtain), and ‘methodological defects’ in the Default Assessments.[258] These matters go to the question of whether the Applicant has discharged his burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessments and have been dealt with above. The Tribunal does not view them as circumstances supporting remission of penalties.
  4. The Applicant noted that the Respondent had previously given him a partial reduction of penalties. The Tribunal understands this to be a reference to the partial allowance of the Applicant’s Objection in relation to the 2015 and 2016 years. Based on information received from the relevant bank which indicated that the Applicant was a co-signatory (with one other person) to the bank accounts for the Know Your Rights Foundation, the Respondent’s objection team formed the view that only 50% of amounts received into those bank accounts should be attributed to the Applicant (rather than the entirety). The Respondent therefore decided that the assessable income in the Default Assessments for those two years should be reduced by approximately $9,000 in aggregate, which had a consequential effect of reducing the amounts in the Penalty Assessments for each of those years. As set out above the Tribunal has also found that the evidence supports a further (small) reduction of amounts attributed to the Applicant in relation to Golden Tara Foundation and Inspiring Images Foundation, on the same basis. However the Tribunal does not believe that this is a circumstance that tends in favour of remission of all or a substantial part of the remaining penalty. It was a partial allowance of the Objection in a relatively modest amount (compared to the total of the Penalty Assessments across the Relevant Years) and not indicative of any basis for viewing the broader penalties imposed as being unjust or unreasonable.
  5. The Applicant referred the Tribunal to the Respondent’s internal instructions to its staff in relation to the imposition and remission of penalties under subsection 284-75(3), Practice Statement Law Administration (PSLA) 2014/4.[259] The Applicant said that the Respondent had not followed this guidance when imposing penalties in his case.[260] The Tribunal is not bound by internal guidance or instructions of the Respondent, however it is generally appropriate for the Tribunal to have regard to such material in the interests of consistency of decision-making. Such material can also assist the Tribunal in understanding the basis upon which the Respondent made the decision for review by the Tribunal. In any event, the Tribunal considers that the matters set out in paragraphs 27 to 45 of PSLA 2014/4 are examples of ‘circumstances’ which might be relevant for the Tribunal’s consideration when asked to exercise the discretion to remit a penalty regardless of the existence of that guidance document. Therefore, to the extent that the Applicant seeks to raise such circumstances, the Tribunal will consider them.
  6. The Tribunal has considered the Applicant’s contentions relating to delay on the part of the Respondent, and the age of the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments. It is correct that the Respondent’s review and audit processes commenced in 2017, which was a number of years after the earliest of the Relevant Years. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant may not have had access to full records relating to those earlier years by the time that the Respondent’s audit commenced. As set out previously in these reasons, the Tribunal took this concern into account in assessing the reliability of the Applicant’s evidence. In some instances (for example, in relation to loans he said had been made to him by his mother, or his lack of involvement with the Golden Tara Foundation) the Tribunal was willing to accept his oral evidence as plausible without requiring direct corroborative documentary evidence.
  7. However the elapse of time between the Relevant Years and commencement of review and audit processes must also be viewed in the context of this proceeding, and the Penalty Assessments in dispute, arising from the failure of the Applicant to lodge income tax returns in any of the Relevant Years, across an extended period of years, such that the Respondent was required to issue assessments of income tax under section 167 in the absence of any other basis for assessment. The Tribunal has determined that those Default Assessments should be upheld (except to the extent of some relatively small amounts in two years). In these circumstances the Tribunal does not view the period that passed before audit processes were commenced as weighing significantly in favour of penalty remission, as the Applicant has contributed to this delay.
  8. The Tribunal has also considered the time that elapsed between the commencement of the review and audit processes, the issuing of the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments and the finalisation of the Objection Decision. The Respondent wrote to the Applicant in July 2017 asking to speak with him about his lodgement status for the Relevant Years. The Respondent issued the Division 353 information requests to various banks and PayPal and those third parties provided information between July 2017 and December 2017. A letter was sent to the Respondent, apparently by the Applicant, in July 2017 essentially indicating that the Applicant did not intend to engage. In late 2017, further email correspondence followed between the Respondent and a person who appears to have been the Applicant (or at least emailing from an address associated with the Applicant) with the Respondent seeking to speak with the Applicant, without any success. The Respondent issued its audit reasons for decision in February 2018, followed by raising of income tax returns for the Relevant Years[261] on its systems and issuance of the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments in the same month.
  9. The Tribunal does not propose to set out in detail of the text of the correspondence between the parties in late 2017, including reasons given by the Applicant for not engaging in the audit process. The Applicant’s political, religious or other personal beliefs are not relevant to the Tribunal’s decision. For present purposes it is sufficient for the Tribunal to find that there was no apparent delay in the Respondent progressing its review and audit processes, and that the Applicant was given opportunities to engage with the Respondent during this process and prior to the issue of the Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments.
  10. The Applicant’s Objection was lodged in November 2023. The Tribunal acknowledges the Applicant’s belief that he had objected in 2018 and disagreed with the Respondent’s apparent position that earlier communications from him disputing the assessments was invalid as an objection. The Tribunal also understands that there was litigation in the County Court of Victoria involving the Applicant and Respondent at some point between 2018 and 2023, and the Applicant said that this affected his ability to lodge a valid objection prior to November 2023.[262] The Tribunal is willing to accept this, but observes that this does not represent delay on the part of the Respondent, and that the Respondent granted the Applicant an extension of time to lodge his Objection. As previously noted, the Respondent wrote to the Applicant in December 2023 asking him to provide further information in support of his Objection, which the Applicant declined to do in January 2024. The Respondent thereafter finalised its Objection Decision in late January 2024. Again, the Tribunal cannot discern any delay on the part of the Respondent.
  11. The Applicant asked that the Tribunal have regard to his participation and conduct in these proceedings, including the provision by him of a significant volume of material, information and reconstructed financial accounts. In the context of penalty remission the Tribunal does not place significant weight on the provision of material after an application was lodged with the Tribunal, including provision of material in accordance with the Tribunal’s directions, and does not believe there is evidence of extensive co-operation or engagement by the Applicant prior to the Tribunal proceeding. The Tribunal views the focus of engagement, active participation, co-operation and/or provision of information as being at the stage of review, audit and objection by the Respondent, being the processes which give rise to the imposition and assessment of the penalties. This application has its origins in the failure of the Applicant to lodge tax returns over a number of years. The Tribunal has previously referred to correspondence to the Respondent from the Applicant, or believed to have been from the Applicant, where there was a clear refusal to engage.[263] The Applicant was given the opportunity to provide further information in support of his Objection, but declined in very adamant terms to do so.[264] In any event, the Applicant needed to file significant persuasive material with the Tribunal as a consequence of the burden of proof being with him. Further, had the Applicant not complied with the Tribunal’s directions his application would likely have been dismissed. The Tribunal also notes that during the hearing the Applicant objected to answering or refused to answer questions put to him by the Respondent’s counsel about certain of the Entities or his involvement with them on the basis of his view of what was relevant for the Tribunal.
  12. The Tribunal has not made any findings in relation to whether the Applicant demonstrated ‘intentional disregard’ or ‘recklessness’ as this was not required where liability for a penalty was found under subsection 284-75(3). The Tribunal has set out above its view that the Applicant did not fully engage or co-operate with the Respondent’s audit and objection processes. The Tribunal has not made any findings that the Applicant obstructed or concealed material from the Respondent, however the Tribunal would not place significant weight in support of remission on the absence of a finding of obstruction or concealment. A taxpayer is required by law not to obstruct the Respondent in carrying out its duties.
  13. Although not expressly raised by the Applicant as a ground for penalty remission, the Applicant had previously told the Tribunal, in the context of his residency submissions and explanation of difficulties in locating records, about having experienced the loss of a business, significant financial challenges and a period of homelessness. Without wanting to raise matters which might cause distress to the Applicant, the Tribunal invited him to elaborate on whether any of these personal circumstances might also be relevant to the question of penalty remission. The Applicant noted that he had experienced homelessness for a couple of periods of six to twelve months.[265] He also said that his mother had been trying to assist him with his financial record keeping around this time but her partner had become seriously ill.[266] The Tribunal understands that at least some of this time overlapped with the period of review or audit activity conducted by the Respondent, and potentially during the time when tax returns would otherwise have been due for lodgement.[267] This Tribunal was not clear as to whether or how this affected the Applicant’s ability to lodge tax returns in any of the Relevant Years (or take advice on whether he was required to lodge returns). The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s statements that he experienced significant financial difficulties, including the loss of his home, but does not have sufficient information to form a conclusion that remission of any part of the penalties is warranted on this basis.
  14. Although the Applicant requested only a full or substantial remission of all penalties imposed, the Tribunal has considered whether in the alternative it should remit part of the penalty amount, or a penalty relating to a particular year but has been unable to form the conclusion that it would be appropriate to do so based on the material before the Tribunal about the Applicant’s circumstances.
  15. The Applicant correctly submitted that it was necessary to consider whether penalty remission was appropriate for each of the Penalty Assessments, rather than the Penalty Assessments collectively. The Tribunal has considered whether there were circumstances that applied or which carried more weight in relation to a Penalty Assessment for any particular Relevant Year compared to other Relevant Years. The Tribunal has not been able to discern any such difference in circumstances.[268] For ease of reading the Tribunal has therefore outlined its reasons in summary, on the basis of its view that the same circumstances apply and carry the same weight in relation to each of the Penalty Assessments.

POST HEARING MATTER

  1. On 22 April 2026 the Tribunal received a copy of a letter sent by the Respondent’s solicitors to the Applicant querying whether three of the case authorities named by the Applicant in his written closing submissions stood for the propositions he had set out in those submissions.[269] The Respondent referred to the obligations of the parties under section 56 of the ART Act and asked that the Applicant confirm that he intended to cite those cases, particularly as they related to the issue of administrative penalties. The Applicant replied to the Respondent’s solicitors by email on 27 April 2026, with a copy to the Tribunal, confirming that he had intended to refer to those authorities and noting that they were provided in support of the proposition that findings of fact underlying the imposition of administrative penalties needed to be supported by appropriate evidence. He explained his view as to how they did so.
  2. The Tribunal observes that the three cases are known Australian taxation law authorities and do canvass matters relating to evidence and proof. However the Tribunal did not find them to be of any additional assistance to the Applicant’s case, particularly in circumstances where those cases involved quite different facts and substantive provisions of taxation legislation to the issues currently before the Tribunal. Two of the cases addressed penalties. The Tribunal has referred above to the Dixon decision which was the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court on appeal from one case cited by the Applicant.[270] The other case that considered penalty issues focussed on the concept of ‘recklessness’ and whether a position was ‘reasonably arguable.’[271] The Tribunal has found that penalties were correctly imposed on the Applicant under subsection 284-75(3) which does not look at these concepts.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal finds that except to the extent of a couple of small amounts in the 2015 and 2016 income years the Applicant has not satisfied his burden of proof in relation to the Default Assessment for any of the Relevant Years, because he has not demonstrated on the balance of probabilities that any of the Default Assessments were excessive and what they should have been. The Tribunal makes the same finding in relation to each of the Penalty Assessments, again noting that there should be a small reduction in the amount of penalty in each of the 2015 and 2016 years to reflect the reduced amount of ‘tax related liability’ in each of those years. The Tribunal will therefore vary the Objection Decision under review but the effect of this variation is to affirm the significant portion of the Objection Decision.
  2. The Tribunal appreciates that the Applicant will be unhappy with this decision. The Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicant did extensive work in preparing the financial outcomes summaries and his submissions, in circumstances where he was self-represented and did not have ready access to historic records. The Applicant made certain submissions with which the Tribunal agreed and identified some matters where the Tribunal agrees that it was likely that the Respondent was incorrect. However this does not provide a sufficient basis for the Tribunal to set aside the Objection Decision and the underlying Default Assessments and Penalty Assessments. The Applicant has also made choices at various stages in relation to what level of information and explanation he was willing to share with the Respondent and the Tribunal.
  3. Following the second interlocutory decision the Applicant had requested information on how he might lodge an appeal with the Federal Court from a decision of the Tribunal. The covering letter from the Tribunal which accompanies these reasons for decision will contain some basic information about appeals to the Federal Court and a link to the Court’s website.
