VT85/2133 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 379; (1988) 19 ATR 3211; 88 ATC 282 (22 January 1988)

Last Updated: 22 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - whether expenses incurred in study deductible - whether expenses incurred in relocation for purposes of employment deductible

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.51

Taxation Board of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 s.189

Case U91 (1987) 87 ATC 525

Ronpibon Tin N.L. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

Federal Commissioner Taxation v Hatchett (1971) 71 ATC 4184

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mrs. R.A. Balmford, Senior Member

1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent disallowing objections by the applicant to the inclusion in the respondent's assessment of his income for the year ended 30 June 1984 of certain amounts claimed as deductions pursuant to sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. The applicant's tax agent, on 7 May 1985, requested that the matter be referred to a Board of Review. The material before the Tribunal does not indicate when that request was complied with by the respondent. By virtue of Section 223(1) of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 and sub-section 189(2) of the Act that referral of the decision on the applicant's objection is deemed to constitute the making by the applicant of an application to the Tribunal for review of the decision.

3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to Section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as modified by Section 14ZG of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 and numbered as folios 1-15, together with one other document supplied by the respondent at the hearing. Evidence was given by the applicant, who was unrepresented.

4. The amounts in issue fall into two categories, which may be described as "the relocation expenses", amounting to $3059 and "the education expenses" amounting to $547.

5. The applicant relied on the first limb of sub-section 51(1) which reads:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income ... shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

6. The relocation expenses amounted to $3059. The applicant said that he had originally been required by his employer, the then Public Works Department of the State of Victoria, to live within a ten mile radius of the Post Office in a Victorian coastal town and he accordingly purchased a house in that area where he lived with his family. He then received a considerable promotion, within the same Division of the Department, to a position the advertisement for which had included the following statement:

"For the satisfactory performance of standby duties applicants must be prepared to reside in the metropolitan area [of Melbourne]".

7. On being appointed to that position, the applicant sold his house and purchased another house within the metropolitan area. He produced an itemised statement of the expenses involved, amounting to $8306.54, of which $5247.06 had been reimbursed by his employer, pursuant to the relevant Determination of the Victorian Public Service Board, leaving a balance of $3059.48. The amounts of the expenditure, and their relevance to the relocation required by his employer, were not challenged by the respondent and are not in issue.

8. In my view the facts in this matter are indistinguishable from those in Case U91 (1987) 87 ATC 525. That case concerned a Commonwealth public servant, who moved from a State capital to Canberra at the request of his superiors. Deputy President Todd held that the costs and expenses of an unsuccessful auction were not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and were of a private or domestic nature. He said, at paragraph 8:

8. In Case B18, 70 ATC 78, which concerned bank employees who were transferred interstate and who claimed inter alia as deductions the costs incurred in selling their houses, Mr G.R. Thompson (Member) at p. 87 stated:

"12. The problem with which the Board is concerned, it seems to me, is one stage more remote from the income producing process than the payment of fares from home to place of employment, for here the items in question relate to the disposal of one home base and the acquisition of another. This takes the task of equating the losses or outgoings claimed by the taxpayer, and now under objection, with the process of gaining or producing assessable income out of the realm of mere difficulty into the area of impossibility. Deductibility under either limb of sec. 51(1) is thus denied without turning to the express exception of 'losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature' to be found in that section.

  1. If it were necessary to consider that exception, and it were necessary to describe the essential nature of commission and stamp duties relating to the sale and purchase of family homes, one could not find a more apt adjective than ' domestic', for the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary includes the following meaning to that word: 'Of or belonging to the home, house or household; household, home, family'".

While the applicant submitted that cases such as this (as well as Case G4, 75 ATC 14) could he distinguished on the footing that the expense which he incurred was contemporaneous rather than a prerequisite, I refer to and adopt Case B18 and the reasoning set out above.

9. Accordingly, the objection decision in relation to the relocation expenses will be affirmed.

10. The education expenses related to two separate courses, which will be referred to as "the French course" and "the business course".

11. The applicant was required by his employer to attend an international seminar, held over two weeks in Paris and two other locations in France, in a field for which he held a major responsibility in Victoria, and in which the French government is a world leader. He was the only Australian present. The French Trade Consulate in Melbourne, through which the arrangements for the seminar were made, recommended that, as the applicant had not studied French since his schooldays, he should attend a crash course in French conversation, conducted by the Alliance Francaise de Melbourne. He was not formally required to attend that course but fees and expenses in relation to the course amounted to $640, and their amounts and their relevance to the course were not challenged by the respondent.

12. Interpreters were present at the seminar for the formal presentations, but the applicant said that much of the time was spent in observing operations and demonstrations of equipment which were explained in French, with one interpreter available for forty people. The study he had undertaken enabled him not only to understand the demonstrations better than he would otherwise have done, but also to ask his own questions and understand the answers. It added considerably to the benefit which he, and thus his employer, obtained from the seminar.

13. I am satisfied that, by attending the seminar on the basis of the recommendation described above, the applicant was doing no more than was his duty to his employer, in ensuring that he derived as much benefit as possible from his attendance. That being so, the expenditure was "incidental and relevant to" gaining or producing the assessable income, to use the words of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin N.L. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 and accordingly falls within the first limb of sub-section 51(1) as having been "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income".

14. The business course was a course at a technical college embracing the subjects of financial management, administrative and managerial systems, economics and marketing management. The applicant said that he undertook: the course because his only qualification was in a specialised professional field, as a ship's captain. The promotion which required him to reside in the metropolitan area brought him significant management responsibilities, and in order to perform his duties properly he needed to broaden his education in the management field. Again, the amounts of expenditure, totalling $157, and their relevance to the course, were not challenged by the respondent.

15. Further, the respondent, at the hearing, abstained from arguing that the business course was insufficiently connected with the gaining or producing of assessable income to justify the allowance of the deduction. In my view that abstention was appropriate. I am satisfied, on the evidence, that the connection was sufficient, and I need only refer to the well-known decisions of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hatchett (1971) 71 ATC 4184.

16. The Commissioner had already treated $250 of the education expenses as a rebatable amount in respect of the relevant year of income pursuant to Section 159U of the Act as it then stood, leaving a balance of $547 ($640 + $157 - $250) which was the subject of the claim for a deduction with which I am concerned. For the reasons given above, the objection decision as to the education expenses will be set aside and the objection in respect of the amount of $547 allowed in full.
