Paracel Holdings Pty Limited and Commissioner of Patents [1994] AATA 58; (1994) Aipc 91-053 (1994) 19 AAR 165 (9 March 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

PARACEL HOLDINGS PTY LIMITED v. COMMISSIONER OF PATENTS

No. N93/168

AAT No. 9358

Number of pages - 9

Patents

(1994) AIPC 91-053

[1994] AATA 58; (1994) 19 AAR 165

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

BJ McMAHON (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Patents - restoration application - failure to pay continuation fee - notice of opposition -definition of "person interested" - conditions for restoration - whether failure to pay was unintentional - enough that present to mind of person that fee is payable and select not to pay it - reasons for election irrelevant - whether failure was due to error or omission or circumstances beyond control of applicant - relevant time for considering failure

Patents Act 1952 (Cth) ss 47D, 47E, 152

Patents Regulations 1991 (Cth) Reg 23.8(1)

Rock Engineering Pty Ltd v A. Noble and Son Ltd [1990] APO 38; (1990) 20 IPR 525

Kaiser Aluminium and Chemical Corporation v Reynolds Metal Co [1969] HCA 7; (1969) 120 CLR 136

Land's Patent (1910) 27 RPC 481

Weir Pumps Ltd v Commissioner of Patents (1989) AIPC 90-537

HEARING

SYDNEY, 28 February 1994

9:3:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr R O'Neill

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr RJ Wright

Solicitors for the Applicant: Ian B Mitchell

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitors

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

BJ McMAHON This application comes to the Tribunal pursuant to s 151 of the Patents Act 1952. The applicant seeks a review of a decision made by a delegate of the Commissioner refusing to restore an application which had lapsed because of failure to pay a continuation fee.

2. By regulation 23.8(1) of the Patents Regulations 1991, the relevant provisions of the 1952 Act continue to apply in respect of this application. The relevant sections are as follows -

"47D(1) Subject to this section, an application for a standard patent

shall lapse if the applicant fails, within the prescribed time, to pay a

fee prescribed for the purposes of this section (in this section and

section 47E referred to as a "continuation fee").

(2) If an applicant fails to pay a continuation fee within the

prescribed time, he may apply to the Commissioner for an extension of

the time for making that payment, and the Commissioner shall extend the

time accordingly, but the time for making a payment shall not be

extended for more than 6 months.

47E(1) Where an application has lapsed under section 47C or 47D, the

Commissioner shall advertise that fact in the Official Journal.

(2) The applicant may make an application (in this section referred to

as "the application for restoration") to the Commissioner for the

restoration of the lapsed application.

(3) The application for restoration shall -

(a) in the case of an application that lapsed under section 47C - be

accompanied by a request for the making of an examination of the lapsed

application and the complete specification and contain a statement of

the circumstances that led to the failure to make the request within the

prescribed time; or

(b) in the case of an application that lapsed under section 47D - be

accompanied by the unpaid continuation fee and contain a statement of

the circumstances that led to the failure to pay the fee within the

prescribed time.

(4) If the Commissioner is satisfied that the failure to make the

request or to pay the continuation fee, as the case may be, within the

prescribed time was unintentional and resulted from an error or omission

on the part of the applicant or of his agent or attorney or from

circumstances beyond the control of the applicant and that there has

been no undue delay in the making of the application for restoration,

the Commissioner shall advertise the application for restoration in the

Official Journal, but if he is not so satisfied, he shall refuse the

application for restoration.

(5) A person interested may, within such time as is prescribed, give

notice to the Commissioner of opposition to the restoration and shall

serve a copy of the notice on the applicant.

(6) If notice of opposition is not so given, the Commissioner shall

restore the lapsed application.

(7) If notice of opposition is so given, the Commissioner shall hear the

case and either restore the lapsed application or refuse the application

for restoration.

(8) Where a lapsed application is restored under this section, such

provisions as are prescribed have effect for the protection or

compensation of persons who availed themselves, or took definite steps

by contract or otherwise to avail themselves, of the subject-matter of

the lapsed application after the lapse of the application was advertised

in the Official Journal and before the date of the first advertisement

of the application for restoration.

(9) Proceedings shall not be taken in pursuance of section 54C in

respect of any Act or thing done during the period from and including

the date on which the application for the patent lapsed to and including

the date of the restoration of the application."

3. The patent application is entitled "Recompression Chamber". Claim 1 describes the invention as a compression chamber suitable for the treatment of patients suffering barotrauma or the like. The chamber comprises a frusto-conical shell, an end wall closing the base of the shell and a sealable access hatch in the end wall. The application lapsed pursuant to s 47D due to the non-payment of the continuation fee payable on 20 May 1989 ("the prescribed time"). Lapsing was advertised in the Official Journal of 11 January 1990. On 19 December 1989 the applicant lodged an application for restoration pursuant to s 47E together with certain supporting declarations.

4. Before advertising the application for restoration, the Commissioner must be "satisfied" that the pre-conditions of sub-section (4) have been met. It is clear that the standard of satisfaction that is required is no more than a prima facie conclusion. In Rock Engineering Pty Ltd v A. Noble and Son Ltd [1990] APO 38; (1990) 20 IPR 525 at 528, it was observed that advertisement of an application for restoration is, in effect, a conditional acceptance of the application for restoration, subject to review in the light of further evidence which may be lodged on opposition. This is clearly correct. Although an argument was suggested by the applicant that the advertising of the application was conclusive of the fact that the applicant had complied with sub-section (4) to the satisfaction of the Commissioner, it was not put with much conviction. There would be no point to the hearing required by sub-section (7) if this argument were correct. It can not be suggested that on the hearing the Commissioner has, as it were, locked himself into a recognition that the pre-conditions of sub-section (4) have been met. All that has to be shown prior to advertising is an arguable case.

5. Notices of opposition were filed with the delegate by McIlwraith McEachern Operations Ltd ("MMOL") and by Robert L Sands. Sub-section (5) requires that the notice of opposition may be given only by "a person interested". The use of the word "so" in sub-section (6) confirms this. The attributes of "a person interested" were discussed in the leading case of Kaiser Aluminium and Chemical Corporation v Reynolds Metal Co [1969] HCA 7; (1969) 120 CLR 136 at 138-141. Kitto J quoted with approval the statement of Lord Buckmaster as Solicitor-General in New Things Ltd's Application (1913) 31 RPC 45 at 46 that an interest to oppose a patent application "must be a real definite and substantial interest and must not arise from something that the opponent proposes to do". Quoting from Clavel's Application (1928) 45 RPC 222 at 224, His Honour also quoted with approval an observation by the Comptroller-General that there must be reasonable grounds for apprehending that the grant of patent rights to the applicant would be "immediately or directly prejudicial to the interests of the opponent" at the time when the opposition is heard. His Honour also went on to say at 141 that these were but illustrations and by no means delineate the outer limits of the concept of "a person interested". There have been other criteria developed following Kaiser to decide whether an opponent's interest is sufficient. If the opponent can show a relevant manufacturing interest or a relevant trading interest then this will make him "a person interested". In the present application, the delegate found that MMOL had such interests but that Mr Sands did not.

6. On the hearing before this Tribunal, neither MMOL nor Mr Sands sought to be made a party, although both were given notice of the proceedings. There was some evidence that since the hearing before the delegate, the relevant Division of MMOL, which had the necessary interests, had ceased trading. It was submitted on the hearing that as a consequence, there was now no person interested, capable of giving a notice of opposition and consequently the provisions of sub-section (6) operated requiring the Commissioner to restore the lapsed application.

7. In my view, the terms of sub-section (5) make it clear that the section is enlivened by the giving of a notice of opposition which is valid at the time it is given. Evidence was put before the delegate that the activities of MMOL demonstrated that it had such an interest at the time. The careful examination by the delegate of this evidence and the arguments in support put by counsel for the objector are helpful and do not need to be repeated. The material raised by the applicant in the present case, to a large extent, was raised in the hearing before the delegate (page 11 of the delegate's decision). None of the material put before me persuades me that I should take a view different from that of the delegate, namely, that at the time the notice of opposition was lodged, MMOL had a relevant interest. The fact that there has been a change of circumstance subsequently, it seems to me, is irrelevant for the purpose of the present application.

8. No application was made to the delegate to strike out the notice of opposition. That could well have been because there was no apparent basis to support such an application. Even if the notice of opposition ought to have been struck out, however, sub-section (6) would not have the effect of entitling the applicant to restoration of the application, if material came to the attention of the delegate in the process of hearing the case, which indicated that the application should not be restored (Rock Engineering at 528.) This material deals with the real issues to be determined in this application, namely whether the pre-conditions of sub-section (4) have been met.

9. There are 5 pre-conditions to a successful application for restoration under this sub-section -

1. That there has been a failure to pay a continuation fee within

the prescribed time.

2. That there has been no undue delay in the making of the

application for restoration.

3. That the failure to pay the fee within the prescribed time was

unintentional.

4. That the failure to pay the fee within the prescribed time

resulted from an error or omission on the part of the applicant or

of his agent or attorney; or

5. That the failure to pay the fee within the prescribed time

resulted from circumstances beyond the control of the applicant.

10. It is generally acknowledged that the first 2 pre-conditions have been complied with. Compliance with the remaining 3 is contested.

11. All the evidence given by the parties to the delegate was in writing, pursuant to the relevant regulations. A decision was taken by both parties to continue that course before this Tribunal. This is not the usual way in which reviews are conducted by this Tribunal, particularly where there are contested issues of fact. However no oral evidence was given. A verified statement by Mr Coghill was tendered in evidence, supported to a minor extent by a verified statement by Mr Grimaldi. Neither witness was tendered for cross-examination, nor was any objection made by the respondent to the tendering of the written evidence without the benefit of testing it in cross-examination. I therefore have the same evidence that was before the delegate through the s 37 documents, and this additional written evidence. I must do the best I can with the evidence available.

12. On the documents Mr Coghill, who appears to be the principal officer of the applicant, has given contradictory versions of the reasons for failure to pay the continuation fee.

13. In his declaration in support of the original application, he said (relevantly) -

"2. On or about the 20th February, 1989 I was advised by Shelston Waters

(the applicant's Patent Attorneys) that the maintenance fee was due in

respect of this application. As our company was experiencing management

and cash flow problems at the time I decided to defer payment and it was

my understanding that the fee could be paid late with fine.

3. On or about the 20th May, 1989 I was again advised by Shelston Waters

that the application had lapsed but could be reinstated by payment of

the required fee and a fine. I again deferred instructing Shelston

Waters believing that the application could be reinstated by payment of

the appropriate fine.

4. On the 30th November, 1989 I telephoned Shelston Waters to ask what

was required in terms of late payment of the maintenance fee. I was

advised that the application had lapsed irrevocably as the period in

which a fine could be paid had expired on the 20th November, 1989. I

immediately instructed Shelston Waters to apply for restoration of this

application.

5. It was always my company's intention to maintain this application in

force and the lapsing resulted from my misunderstanding of the

provisions relating to late payment of the maintenance fee."

14. That declaration was made on 27 December 1989, close to the events it describes and close to the original application for restoration. In response to a letter from the Commissioner seeking further explanation, he made another declaration on 22 January 1990. Relevantly he said -

"3. At the time of lapsing of this application, myself and other

directors of the applicant company were under considerable pressure to

sort ourt (sic) the affairs of the company following a management

reorganisation. Accordingly, I was deferring all matters which in my

view did not require my urgent attention.

4. It was always my view and understanding of the procedure involved in

maintenance of this patent application that the renewal fee could be

paid late with the appropriate fine. I failed to understand the patent

application would lapse irrevocably if the fee was not paid within six

months of the due date. How I arrived at this view, I am unsure,

however, given that I am layman in respect of patent procedures and was

under great pressure at the time, I now believe that I did not fully

understand the advice provided by my Attorneys and/or did not spend

sufficient time in attempting to understand the requirements in relation

to maintenance of this application.

5. I again state that it was always my intention to maintain this

application in force and that the lapsing was unintentional and resulted

from my failure to fully understand the procedure required in

maintaining this application. This lack of understanding, in hindsight,

resulted from my paying minimal attention to the matter due to pressures

of other business."

15. Largely on the basis of this evidence, the delegate found that the failure to pay the continuation fee within the prescribed time was not unintentional and did not result from any other of the prescribed circumstances.

16. In the verified statement tendered in evidence before me as Exhibit C, the applicant said in a mixture of fact and submission -

"8. The Delegate's finding that I decided to intentially (sic) not pay

the Patent renewal fee of $136 and consciously put off paying it, is

untrue. Although I had received the two letters from Messrs Shelston

Waters that I referred to in paragraphs 2 and 3 of my declaration of

27th December 1989, the subsequent letters were not brought to my

attention. For the reasons set out above, the office arrangements at

North Sydney were somewhat chaotic between February 1989 and late 1989.

I was unaware of the consequences of failing to pay the fee on or before

20 November 1989 as I was under the belief that the fee could be paid

late with a fine. It is clear that I was under a misapprehension as to

the effects of non-payment of the fee by 20 November 1989 and this is

clearly an error or omission within the provisions of sub-section

47E(4). It is incorrect to conclude that my decision to defer payment

was a "deliberate decision not (sic) pay the continuation fee within the

prescribed time" - page 17 of the decision of the Delegate dated 1 March

1993."

17. The reference to correspondence from Shelston Waters is a reference to a series of letters from the applicant's Patent Attorneys that appear in the s 37 documents. The first letter is dated 20 March 1989 and is addressed to the applicant at premises at Chatswood. It was a standard form letter reminding the applicant that the renewal fee of $136 was due on 20 May. The letter went on to say that in the absence of the applicant's timely instructions, the case would be allowed to lapse and added "renewal after the due date may be impossible or more expensive". A note has been added to the pro forma letter in these words : "Please note that regardless of any previous arrangement we can not effect renewal of this case unless payment accompanies your instruction". This letter was sent prior to the prescribed date and was received by the applicant, as Mr Coghill acknowledges. He also received the second letter, dated 24 May 1989, although this time it was addressed to the applicant at its new premises in North Sydney.

18. By this time the application had lapsed. The letter stated : "This is to confirm that the above case has been allowed to lapse in default of your instruction to renew it. We remind you that renewal may be effected for up to 6 months after the due date, upon payment of penalty fees". The limitation of 6 months is clearly set out. I do not accept the evidence of Mr Coghill when he says that he was under the impression that the application could be restored at any time upon payment of a fine. The period of grace is clearly set out in the letter, the receipt of which he acknowledges.

19. Mr Coghill then says that he did not receive 3 subsequent letters written by the Attorneys on 31 May, 1 August and 15 September 1989 respectively. I also find it difficult to accept this evidence from Mr Coghill. All letters were sent to the same address as the letter of 24 May, which he received. There was some evidence in Mr Grimaldi's statement that the North Sydney premises were serviced offices, where facilities were shared on a temporary basis, and that the procedure for the mail system was for the serviced office personnel to receive the mail and distribute this mail to tenants on each floor. I accept that the serviced office personnel may have been unfamiliar with the various companies' requirements and business practices and, according to Mr Grimaldi, that at times documents and letters were lost. I can not accept however that not one, not two, but three letters all on an important subject, and all asking for money, were lost.

20. On 31 May, the attorneys wrote to International Innovations Limited (the company in whose care the letter of 24 May was addressed to) at the North Sydney address advising that several patent applications had lapsed because of failure to pay the annual maintenance fees. These were then summarised. They included the application currently under consideration. The letter said in relation to that application that the "absolute final date" was 20 November 1989. The letter went on : "Failure to pay these maintenance fees before the absolute final date will result in lapsing of these applications. In most cases lapsing is irrevocable. If you wish us to pay these fees we will require a full payment of our outstanding account, together with money on account to cover the cost of payment of these fees. In the absence of your instructions we shall assume you wish to allow these applications to lapse".

21. On 1 August 1989 the attorneys again wrote to International Innovations Ltd at the applicant's address, noting that they still had not received payment of the balance of their outstanding account in relation to the named applications including the present one, and reminding the applicant that the applications were in a state of lapse and required payment of renewal fees as well as any fines associated with late payment.

22. On 15 September 1989 a third letter was written to International Innovations Ltd at the applicant's address. This referred to a recent telephone conversation. As the letter is addressed to the attention of Robert Coghill, I assume that the telephone conversation referred to was one between Mr Coghill and the Attorneys. It is to be noted that this conversation took place at least 2 months prior to the "absolute final date" in November. The letter sets out the costs of reinstating the named applications that were in a "state of lapse" through failure to pay the maintenance fees and asked for early instructions as to which applications the applicant wished "to revive" and asked for money in advance to cover the above costs.

23. These letters, the 2 contemporary statutory declarations of Mr Coghill, the reference to the telephone conversation and the fact of non-payment of the continuation fee, lead me to conclude as a fact that this evidence is to be preferred to the later evidence tendered at the hearing before me, and that accordingly the failure to pay the continuation fee was not unintentional. I conclude that Mr Coghill, and therefore the applicant, knew that the fee was payable and that the applicant took a conscious decision to defer payment.

24. Such a decision is not one contemplated by the sub-section as an appropriate basis for the restoration of a lapsed application. An early decision of Parker J in the Chancery Division entitled in the matter of Land's Patent (1910) 27 RPC 481 has been followed on many occasions. Although it dealt with legislation which is now different in terms from the legislation before me, the reasoning of His Honour is still apposite. At page 483 he said -

"..... It is quite clear that the omission of the payment of the fee

is the omission which is referred to in Subsection 3, and the

Section only applies if the omission to pay the fee was

unintentional. In order that an omission to pay a fee should be

intentional, it is only necessary that it should be present to the

mind of the person who has to pay the fee, that the fee is payable,

and that he should deliberately elect not to pay it. His reasons for

that election are, it appears to me, absolutely immaterial....."

25. It matters not why the decision not to pay was deliberately taken. Even if I now accept the revised version of Mr Coghill's evidence that he was under a misapprehension of law as to the effect of non-payment of the fee, the result would not be different, as Parker J pointed out later in the judgment from which I have quoted. A decision to defer payment is analogous to the deliberate policy not to maintain a watch referred to in Weir Pumps Ltd v Commissioner of Patents (1989) AIPC 90-537 at 38,744.

26. My finding therefore that the failure to pay the fee was not unintentional is a finding that an essential pre-requisite of sub-section (4) has not been made out. This application must fail on that basis alone.

27. I would only add that even if Mr Coghill's evidence that the failure was unintentional were to be accepted, it could not be said that the failure resulted from circumstances beyond the control of the applicant. This part of the argument is based on the office conditions referred to by both Mr Coghill and Mr Grimaldi. Neither addressed the possibility that the so called "administrative problems" associated with the removal to North Sydney were capable of being controlled by the applicant. If letters did not come to the attention of Mr Coghill, then he said that he could not be held responsible and that as he was unaware that he was required to pay the fee, and was not aware of the consequences of non-payment, these circumstances were beyond his control. As I have said earlier I do not accept that he did not receive these letters. In any event, however, it was certainly within his control to organise the administration of his office in such a way that 3 different important letters (one written at his request following a telephone conversation) came to his attention. If, as he says, he did not see them, then it can not be said that the surrounding circumstances were beyond his control.

28. In any event, such an explanation does not deal with the situation contemplated by sub-section (4). The error or omission resulting from circumstances beyond the control of the applicant must have occurred prior to the prescribed date, that is, prior to 20 May 1989. Provision for amelioration is given only if it can be shown that the failure occurred prior to that date, and not during or after the so called 6 months period of grace.

29. For these reasons the decision under review is affirmed.
