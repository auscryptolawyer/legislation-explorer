Last Updated: 29 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - solicitor in partnership deductibility of costs of flying lessons - deductibility of costs' of flying publications used in partnership business apportionment - portion of partnership income assigned to trust.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

Ure v. F.C. of T. 81 A.T.C. 4100.

Applicant COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT86/7093

AAT Decision No 4469

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1 July 1988

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/7093

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 1 July 1988

Place : Sydney

Decision : The Tribunal varies the objection-decision under review and directs that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1984 be reduced by the sum of $10.

(Sgd.) PETER M. ROACH,

Senior Member.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/7093

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

1 July 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant in this reference is a solicitor. At all material times he has carried on business as a solicitor in private practice in equal partnership with others. Along with all other solicitors in private practice he knew that, no matter how talented he was, his abilities would be unproductive of income unless he had clients. In common with most of his fellows, he took the view that it was desirable that he should always increase his client base. He also must have known that the likelihood of new clients continuing to generate fees for the practice would depend upon the quality of his service and the acceptability of it to clients. In short, his capacity to attract the initial instructions of new clients would merely serve as an opportunity to demonstrate his talents. It might or might not lead to other retainers. To that extent he did not differ from the circumstances in which nearly all solicitors in private practice find themselves.

  1. Similarly, as with his fellows, he was to find that new clients personal to himself would come by reason of some personal association with himself or by reason of being recommended by other clients. As to the former group, those associations might have their foundation in a wide range of activities within the community. Those new clients might come from associations with church groups, gambling interests, yachting fraternities, political parties, the arts, service groups, and many other such groupings within the community.
  2. In addition, as with most practitioners, the interest group from which he was likely to find new clients was likely to be an influence on the further development of his practice. The active sportsman was likely to attract the business of sporting clubs and sportsmen. Their business being commonly sports orientated was likely to cause him to develop some expertise in that field, thereby tending with experience to lead to increased proficiency; and thereby to attract more clients from that field, presenting both associated and unrelated problems.
  3. In the case of the Applicant the particular interest group with which he came to be associated was the fraternity pilots and from within the aviation industry. Although at the time he had no significant experience, even as a passenger in light aircraft, his interest in an ill-defined area referred to as "aviation law" caused him to play a prominent place in the formation of an aviation law association: a body which, in the few years of its existence, has come to have a membership of some 250 persons in four States. Undetailed evidence was given that it holds two-day conferences on "aviation law" and that on occasion visitors attend from overseas. Several members of the association are both practising lawyers (either barristers or solicitors) and pilots: with varying degrees of flying qualifications.

5. The Applicant took the view that he would enhance his own reputation if he were to qualify as a pilot. To that end he obtained a student licence in January 1984 and, on 12 December of that year, qualified for and was granted a restricted private pilot's licence. Since then he has maintained his endorsement although in that period he has flown only 8.2 hours on 16 occasions while in command; and a further 9.4 hours on 15 occasions with another pilot in command. On another occasion, not recorded in his pilot's logbook, while flying on the holidays in an aircraft in charge of others, he did take control of the aircraft on some occasions. It is not suggested that at any time he has used the aircraft, or his skills as a pilot of aircraft, directly in relation to his practice. It is not suggested that he has used it to fly to distant Courts, or to travel to attend upon clients, or to transport clients. Having regard to the fact that he holds only a restricted private pilot's licence, limiting his flying activities to the surrounds of the aerodrome from which he is flying and the training area related to it, that is not surprising.

6. When the Applicant gained his student pilot's licence he was able to come into possession of manuals which had not previously been available to him. He instanced and produced in evidence materials published by the Department of Aviation (however so-called from time to time) such as a Visual Flight Guide; NOTAM'S (Class II) (notices to airmen); AIC's (aeronautical information circulars); En Route Supplement; Aerodrome Diagrams; FISCOMS (flight information service communications chart); Aerodrome Directory; and Air Navigation Orders. His evidence was that, having gained access to these materials, some of them were of immediate use to him in the course of his professional work. He gave one instance in which, in the course of his professional work, he needed to have recourse to Air Navigation Orders, not on the basis that they would certainly contain information relevant to the problem posed to him for solution, but rather on the basis that he could not properly advise without having considered the possibility that those Orders might contain some material relevant to the determination of the client's problem. I accept that such occasions occurred, but not that they were commonplace.

7. He produced evidence, which I accept, that from as early as March 1984, persons and organizations whose first association with him had come through his interest in the association and in flying, had come to him as clients. He had been able to identify $12,430 in fees as derived in the period March 1984 to May 1988 from eighteen distinct matters to which he attributed an "aviation" flavour and $2,849 from nine retainers from the same group of persons, but not said to have any aviation characteristics. In the latter class were such matters as advising on marital problems and acting in the sale of a private residence. In the former category were matters as diverse as the incorporation of an association; sales and leases of aircraft and of flying schools; and advice in relation to the use of helipads.

8. I accept that within his partnership he is regarded as the person most appropriate to deal with matters having any association with the aviation industry and that his decision to undertake the course was one supported by his partners. The support they provided was not financial. It was rather in that much of his flying training was done mid-week, commonly on a Wednesday morning, when he absented himself from the practice for that purpose. I was not told whether his other partners had similar absences which enabled them mid-week to pursue their interests in yachting; golf; fishing; or other recreational pursuits which are also commonly a source of clients for solicitors who seek to develop their practices by such associations.

9. So it was that the Applicant bore the entire cost of his flying training and, during the year of income ended 30 June 1984, he incurred expenses claimed as follows:-

Flying Lessons $2,162

Training Books 83

Travel from home to

aerodrome for training 293

Journals 54

Diner's Club Membership 35

The claim so made to deductions was wholly disallowed and was objected to in all respects. However, the claim to Diner's Club Membership was not pursued before me.

  1. In the year of income ended 30 June 1984 the share of partnership profit attributable to the interest of the Applicant was $49,016 but, by reason of a Deed of Assignment entered into in November 1981 in favour of a company which was trustee for a "No. 2 Trust" in the name of the surname of the Applicant, the share of that income derived by the Applicant was only $24,998: the other 49% of the income so generated being derived by the trustee.
  2. Resolution of the Applicant's problem is to be found in a characterization of the actions concerned. The Applicant became an amateur pilot. Of itself that was not an income-earning activity. It was not to be distinguished in its character from other recreational activities, such as yachting, or golfing, or car racing or even that of being a good "club man". The course was undertaken with a view to attracting clients to his business. That circumstance does not distinguish it from the circumstances of many who use their recreational, sporting, political, cultural and social activities as a means of fostering the goodwill of potential clients. There was a suggestion in the evidence that the Applicant did not particularly like flying. I accept that there may have been some nervous excitement with his initial experiences as a passenger in light aircraft and as a student pilot and, indeed, perhaps even as a pilot, but I am certainly not persuaded by the evidence before me that the Applicant had such an aversion to flying that, in the interests of his business, he overcame it rather than pursue some more pleasing activity which might have been quite as productive of new clients as the course undertaken. But, in any event, I find the point to be without significance.
  3. The solicitor who fraternizes on the golf course or elsewhere with clients whom he dislikes, doing so only in order to foster or preserve their goodwill as clients, is no more entitled to a deduction than the person who finds fraternization in that manner with his clients a matter of great personal satisfaction.
  4. Unquestionably the activities of a taxpayer as a pilot can be income-producing activities and, in appropriate circumstances, can constitute a business. Unquestionably, a person can contemporaneously pursue income-earning activities as a pilot and as a solicitor or other professional person. But that is not the case contended for by the Applicant. Rather the argument for the Applicant is that flying status as a pilot, or rather the acquisition of the status of a pilot, was an incident of his professional practice as a solicitor. I decline to accept that. Coming to be recognized as the solicitor-colleague of other flyers and of persons interested in aviation is no more part of the practice of a solicitor than a taxpayer being recognized as a colleague by bookmakers on the basis of the taxpayer's punting activities; the golfer being recognized as a solicitor-colleague by fellow golfers; the yachtsman by fellow-yachtsmen; the racing driver by followers of the car racing fraternity; and so on.
  5. That is not to say that expenditure by solicitors in the way for example of entertaining clients and prospective clients has never been deductible. I make no comment in that regard for the simple reason that the case was not presented on that basis. There is no claim that his flying attracted the clients or served their interests. Accordingly, I conclude that the expenses which were incurred by the Applicant in learning to fly, and in relation to flying itself, are not deductible.
  6. However, there remains another question. How are the volumes acquired through flying training and continuing status as a pilot to be assessed? In some instances some of the material is used by the Applicant, just as other solicitors would use sets of the publications of the Australian Standards Authority in matters involving building disputes; or sets of fees prescribed by the Commonwealth Department of Health in the course of advising medical practitioners or their insurers. I accept that there was a quite limited use of the material provided. Accordingly, some allowance should be made, but there must be an apportionment.
  7. Such an apportionment is called for, firstly, because such expense as was incurred was wholly claimed and yet it was incurred, to the extent to which it was incurred at all, "in the course of" generating all of the assessable income generated by the participation of the Applicant in the partnership. However, as the Applicant had assigned 49% in that interest to others, it follows that to that degree the expenditure which was incurred was partly directed to the generation of assessable income in the hands of others. Applying the principles laid down in Ure v. F.C. of T. (81 A.T.C. 4100), I would proportionally adjust the amount otherwise allowable.

17. Secondly, even if there had been no question of assignment involved, I could not accept that the expense incurred was equally related to the practice in respect of all subscriptions. On that account the amount to be allowed must be substantially reduced. Thirdly, even where I am satisfied that there was an actual use and application of the materials in the course of practice, as I am satisfied was the case in relation to the Air Navigation Orders, I am sure that it is far from being exclusively used for professional purposes. In all the circumstances, I think an allowance of $10 is appropriate.

15. Accordingly, the determination of the Tribunal will be that the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection under review be varied and that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1984 be reduced by $10.
