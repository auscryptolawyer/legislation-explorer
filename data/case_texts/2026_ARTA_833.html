Statement of Reasons

Contents

WHAT IS THIS CASE ABOUT?

  1. This case concerns whether and, if so, to what extent the general anti-avoidance provisions in Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth)[1] apply to two complex series of arrangements.
  2. The two series of arrangements are identified in these reasons as: (1) the Cleary Hoare Practice Trust or Practice Trust schemes; and (2) the Income Earning Trusts or IET schemes.
  3. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) upheld the application of Part IVA to both series of schemes.[2] The applicant, Mr Grant, appealed the AAT’s decision to the Federal Court, which, constituted as a Full Court, allowed the appeal; set aside the AAT’s decision; and remitted the proceeding to this Tribunal for hearing and determination of the following questions:
    (a) whether the applicant had assessable amounts in respect of the Cleary Hoare Practice Trust taxable income for the 1997 to 2002 tax years pursuant to Part IVA . . .; and

    (b) whether the applicant had assessable amounts in respect of the Income Earning Trusts taxable income for the 1997 to 2000 tax years pursuant to Part IVA . . . [3]

  4. Mr Grant does not dispute that the arrangements constitute schemes for Part IVA purposes. Nor does he make any submission regarding whether it would be concluded that the schemes were entered into for the dominant purpose of obtaining tax benefits. Rather, Mr Grant contends that he did not obtain a tax benefit in each case or, alternatively, that any tax benefit was less than the amount determined by the Commissioner.
  5. Mr Grant bears the burden of proving the assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect and what amounts should have been assessed. Thus, the question for the Tribunal to determine is whether, in respect of each scheme and each relevant income year, Mr Grant has proved that he has not obtained a tax benefit in respect of each scheme for each income year or whether the amount of the tax benefit in each case was less than the amount determined by the Commissioner.

BROAD OUTLINE OF THE FACTS

  1. The Federal Court summarised the facts as follows:
    14. Mr Grant is a solicitor. Between May 1992 and 30 June 1999 he was a partner at the law firm Cleary Hoare. The firm was run by the Cleary Hoare Practice Trust . . . Mr Grant, along with Messrs Hart, Cleary and Hoare, was a trustee of that trust, and the legal practice was under their control pursuant to the terms of the relevant trust deed. Mr Hart practised as a solicitor in tax matters. Mr Grant did not.

    15. Towards the end of the 1997 financial year, Mr Hart and others, being the other principals of Cleary Hoare, entered into two tax planning arrangements which involved a series of new trusts and transactions with no pattern of previous income distributions, each known as the “New Venture Income scheme” or NVI Scheme.

    16. The CH Practice Trust was a unit trust. The units were divided into ordinary units and special units. Trust entities associated with the firm’s principals were the ordinary unitholders; in Mr Grant’s case, Wood Duck Pty Ltd (Wood Duck) as trustee for the Sensitive New Age Guy Trust (SNAG Trust). Mr Grant was also a special unit holder of the [Practice Trust].

    17. Separately, during the 1997 to 2000 income years, Cleary Hoare Corporate Pty Ltd (CHC) (as trustee for the CHC Discretionary Trust) carried on a business of marketing and facilitating tax planning arrangements and products, called “promotional activities”, as well as “employee welfare funds and non-complying super funds”. During those years, a series of so called “income earning trusts”, called the IETs, earned income in the form of fees in respect of that business.

    18. The IET business used Cleary Hoare to prepare documents and provide legal services in respect of those schemes.

    19. Between 1997 and 2000, there were a total of ten IETs that earned income. Each IET was a discretionary trust operated by a corporate trustee.

    20. In relation to six of the IETs, Mr Grant was not a director of the relevant corporate trustee. And three of the IETs were created after Mr Grant left the firm after having been told by Messrs Hart and Collie that if he did not resign then he would be excluded from the [Practice Trust] and IETs.

    20. In July 1999, Mr Grant commenced practice in his own firm, Grants Lawyers, and subsequently Merthyr Law. He had no further involvement with Cleary Hoare or the IETs.

THE AMENDED ASSESSMENTS AND THE AAT DECISION

  1. Mr Grant had lodged tax returns for the 1994 to 2002 income years on the basis that he had tax losses available against which assessable income could be offset, and that he did not personally receive income distributions from the Practice Trust or through the IET schemes.
  2. The Commissioner’s assessments for the 1997 to 2002 income years which were the subject of the AAT proceedings were premised on the Commissioner’s view that the asserted accrued losses in respect of the 1994 and subsequent income years were not available to Mr Grant and included in Mr Grant’s assessable income the tax benefits the Commissioner determined that Mr Grant had obtained, in accordance with Part IVA, as detailed below.
  3. Mr Grant objected to the assessments and upon his objections being disallowed, applied to the AAT for review of the objection decisions. The AAT decided that:
    (a) accrued personal losses were available to be offset against Mr Grant’s assessable income in the 1997 and subsequent years (until exhausted);

    (b) Mr Grant’s entity, the trustee of the Sensitive New Age Guy Trust (the SNAG Trust), not Mr Grant, had become presently entitled to the part of the Practice Trust income distributed to the SNAG Trust.

    (c) Mr Grant did not otherwise have assessable income relating to the Practice Trust as he received only loans, not income distributions, from the trust;

    (d) in relation to the IET schemes, loans were made to Mr Grant’s entities – the Lombard Trust and SNAG No 2 – which were forgiven following Mr Grant’s departure from Cleary Hoare;

    (e) the Commissioner’s Part IVA determinations in respect of the Practice Trust schemes and the IET schemes should be upheld.

SCOPE OF THE REMITTER

  1. Aside from the AAT’s decision regarding the Part IVA determinations, in determining Mr Grant’s appeal the Court did not disturb the AAT’s findings. Thus, the Court made clear that, aside from the tax benefit question, all other issues are beyond the scope of the remitter, and the AAT’s findings in respect of such issues are not to be re-opened. That includes the assessment of penalties “applied to the corrected shortfalls in tax payable by Mr Grant”.[4]
  2. The issues which are not to be re-opened include those relating to the following findings of the AAT as summarised by the Court:
    The Tribunal accepted Mr Grant’s submission that at least some of the amounts received by the entities associated with him as part of the Practice Trust Scheme and IET Schemes were loans which were released when he departed from Cleary Hoare in 1999, and that these entities also made loans to Mr Grant. The Tribunal also accepted that Mr Grant did not earn income by way of distributions from the [Cleary Hoare Practice Trust] or [Sensitive New Age Guy Trust] in the years 1994 to 1999, and the accrued personal losses were still available to him in the 1997 tax year.[5]
  3. Thus, the AAT’s determination that the accrued personal losses were available was not disturbed on appeal and is not to be re-opened in the proceeding before this Tribunal. Nor is the finding that it was the SNAG Trust, not Mr Grant, that was entitled to income from the Practice Trust in the 1997, 1998 and 1999 income years, or the finding that loans were made to Mr Grant, not income distributions. Similarly, the findings that loans were made to the Lombard Trust and the SNAG No 2 in connection with the IET schemes, which were forgiven following Mr Grant’s departure from the firm, are not to be re-opened.
  4. Subject to these limitations, the hearing before this Tribunal is not a continuation of the AAT hearing. The remitter obliges the Tribunal to consider the remitted questions afresh. Neither party is bound by how they conducted their case before the AAT.[6]

STATUTORY FRAMEWORK[7]

Meaning of “tax benefit”

  1. For Part IVA purposes, under s 177C(1)(a) the obtaining of a tax benefit by a taxpayer in connection with a scheme is stated to be a reference to:
    an amount not being included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of a year of income where the amount would have been included, or might reasonably be expected to have been included, in the assessable income of the taxpayer of that year of income if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out . . .

    (Emphasis added.)

  2. The bolded words in s 177C(1)(a) above require a reasonable expectation of events. In that regard, on the highest authority it is established that:
    A reasonable expectation requires more than a possibility. It involves a prediction as to events which would have taken place if the relevant scheme had not been entered into or carried out and the prediction must be sufficiently reliable for it to be regarded as reasonable.[8]

Burden of proof

  1. The applicant bears the burden of proving the assessments are excessive and what amounts should have been assessed.[9]
  2. In that regard, I apply the following principles as previously summarised by this Tribunal’s predecessor:
    (a) Facts may be found based on oral evidence alone. There is no barrier to a fact being found on the uncorroborated evidence of an applicant. There is no requirement that direct evidence by oral testimony or affidavit may only be accepted if corroborated.

    (b) However, self-serving statements should be closely scrutinised.

    (c) Where a taxpayer has received funds or incurred costs that are not clearly or fully explained in contemporaneous records, the taxpayer may face a challenge in satisfying the burden of proof without corroborating evidence.

    (d) However, even in such cases, oral or written evidence of the taxpayer is not to be regarded as prima facie unacceptable. While it will often be prudent to put forward corroborating evidence, taxpayers are not obliged to call all material witnesses or produce all material documents.

    (e) The standard of proof is the balance of probabilities. An applicant that succeeds ‘in weighing down [the] scales ever so slightly’ in the applicant’s favour will discharge the burden of proof it bears.

    [26] . . . the observations of Logan J in Anglo American Investments Pty Ltd (Trustee) v Commissioner of Taxation noting that informality can and often does attend the formation of legal relations in small business. As his Honour noted:

    Even more this is so where the relevant corporate actors are or are represented by the same individual acting in different capacities or by individuals who are close family members or business associates. Sometimes the only documentary manifestation of that legal relationship may be a transaction recorded in a ledger or perhaps just an annually prepared profit and loss account and accompanying annotations. There may then, in a taxation appeal, be related oral evidence of the individual(s) concerned that the transaction was as so recorded informally...

    [27] That is not to say there is any special or lesser standard of proof for a small business. Where informality is present, ‘much can depend on the credibility one affords the accounts given by participants and, where they exist, representations in business records created under their supervision or with their approval’.

    [28] The Tribunal can only make findings of fact based on the evidence put before it and, where necessary, its relevance explained. Mere assertions will not suffice . . .

    [29] Further, the process of finding facts needs to consider the evidence led through the lens of what was, or could be expected to be, capable of being led. As already noted, that does not mean an applicant is obliged to produce every relevant document or potential witness. However, where relevant evidence that could be expected is not produced, that is a relevant factor for the Tribunal to consider.

    (Footnotes omitted.)[10]

  3. In the context of determining whether a taxpayer obtained a tax benefit, it is not sufficient for the taxpayer to show that the Commissioner’s counterfactual is not reasonable. A taxpayer will usually seek to demonstrate the absence of a tax benefit by identifying on the evidence a counterfactual or postulate which shows what the taxpayer would reasonably be expected to have done if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out. The taxpayer’s postulate must be a reasonable one, measured by reference to the substance of the scheme and any result or consequence for the taxpayer achieved by the scheme.[11]
  4. The prediction required must be based on objective inquiry and determination, not subjective intent.[12] That does not mean that evidence of the taxpayer is irrelevant. A taxpayer may give evidence regarding the steps the taxpayer says would have been taken in the absence of the scheme. That evidence may be useful to the extent that it reveals facts or matters that bear upon the objective determination of the reasonableness of the alternative postulate.[13]
  5. However, mere assertion or speculation unsupported by foundation facts will not assist the taxpayer.[14] Production of contemporaneous documentation to evidence the objective circumstances will be far more probative than mere assertions, along with consideration of the logic of the taxpayer’s counterfactual having regard to the surrounding circumstances established by the evidence.[15]
  6. Although a separate determination of whether Mr Grant has discharged the burden of proof in respect of the tax benefit issue is required for each scheme for each income year, the parties largely approached these issues by identifying what were said to be relevant factors applicable in relation to all of the income years relevant to each of the two types of schemes. I adopt the same approach unless there are circumstances identified that are relevant to only one or more income years or which apply or manifest differently across the income years.

Is evidence of events after the relevant income year potentially relevant to consideration of the reliability of a counterfactual?

  1. The Commissioner submits that:
    To the extent that Mr Grant seeks to rely on events after the income years under review, that cannot assist his counterfactual. Events after the relevant income years do not provide a foundation for a counterfactual, because the foundational facts are properly facts that occurred prior to the relevant income years, rather than after the relevant income years.
  2. For the reasons stated in Collie and Commissioner of Taxation,[16] I do not accept this submission.
  3. In addition to those reasons, I note that the Full Court referred to Mr Grant’s evidence about how he managed his affairs after 2004, stating that it “had to be considered”.[17] The Commissioner submitted that these comments were “not informative of the proper approach” to weighing Mr Grant’s evidence regarding how he managed his affairs after 2004.
  4. In that regard, the Commissioner noted the AAT was criticised for dismissing that evidence out of hand. Noting that was the context in which the Full Court’s observations were to be understood, the Commissioner submitted that the comments were not to be taken as an endorsement of ex post facto evidence for the purposes of an assessment of a counterfactual for Part IVA purposes. I am unable to understand that submission. That seems to me to be precisely what the comments indicate. I am also unable to understand why, as a matter of principle, evidence of a subsequent pattern of conduct could not inform the likelihood of a postulate regarding earlier circumstances being reliable, subject, of course, to weight and other relevant circumstances.
  5. Accordingly, I take into account Mr Grant’s submissions regarding circumstances arising after the relevant income years to the extent they may assist in assessing the reliability of his counterfactuals. That is, on the basis that what occurred in the years after the relevant income years may be informative of what may be reasonably expected to have occurred if the schemes had not been entered into.

What if the applicant’s alternative postulate would also be impugned under Part IVA?

  1. For the reasons discussed in Collie and Commissioner of Taxation,[18] Mr Grant cannot succeed by proving his counterfactuals give rise to a reasonable expectation if, under his counterfactuals, the contested income would be included in his assessable income by any provision of the income tax law including the provisions in Part IVA.[19]
  2. The Commissioner submits that under his counterfactuals the contested amounts would be included in Mr Grant’s taxable income by the operation of Part IVA. Mr Grant submits that this issue should not be considered by the Tribunal as it has been raised too late and with too little precision and detail by the Commissioner. In any case, Mr Grant says if the Commissioner’s submission is considered on its merits it should not be accepted.

THE SCHEMES AND PART IVA DETERMINATIONS

  1. The assessments for the 1997 to 2000 income years were made on the basis that Mr Grant obtained a tax benefit in each year as a consequence of Mr Grant and his associates at Cleary Hoare, and related trusts and corporate entities they controlled, carrying out a scheme known as the New Venture Income Scheme (NVI scheme).
  2. The Practice Trust schemes and the IET schemes are variants of the NVI scheme.
  3. Mr Grant reported minimal taxable income in each of the income years in which the schemes were implemented.

The Practice Trust schemes

The steps in the Practice Trust schemes

  1. The Practice Trust schemes were the means by which the respective shares of the Cleary Hoare Practice Trust’s income were passed through discretionary trusts controlled by Mr Hart, Mr Hoare and Mr Grant.[20]
  2. The steps in the schemes are not in dispute. They are the same schemes that were before the AAT. The schemes are summarised below in Mr Grant’s recitation of facts which the Commissioner adopted.
  3. The details of the Practice Trust schemes are set out in the AAT’s reasons, as follows:
    (a) 1997 Practice Trust scheme – at [131] and illustrated diagrammatically at [132];

    (b) 1998 Practice Trust scheme – at [133] and [135];

    (c) 1999 Practice Trust scheme – at [67] and [137].

  4. I adopt those descriptions of the Practice Trust schemes which are accepted by both parties.
  5. The Full Court summarised the Practice Trust schemes as involving:
    (a) the distribution of income by the SNAG Trust (that income having been distributed by the CH Practice Trust to the SNAG Trust) to a separate trust; and

    (b) a series of subsequent transactions that involved:

    (i) income distribution to further trusts, including an entity with accumulated losses;

    (ii) subscription for units in a separate trust, and the making of gifts to other trusts; and

    (iii) ultimately, the making of loans to entities associated with the principals of Cleary Hoare, including (in Mr Grant’s case) the SNAG Trust.

  6. The loans in the final step were made by an entity controlled by Messrs Hart, Hoare and Grant. For example, in the 1997 Practice Trust scheme, the loans were made by Lake Mylor Pty Ltd as trustee for the Zebra Capital Trust, each to an entity controlled by one of those gentlemen. The loans to the SNAG Trust were released after Mr Grant’s departure from Cleary Hoare, as recorded in the financial statements for the SNAG Trust as at 30 June 1999.

The Part IVA determinations (Practice Trust schemes)

  1. The Commissioner determined that Mr Grant has obtained tax benefits in the following amounts and that those amounts would be included in Mr Grant’s taxable income for the relevant income years:
    (a) 1997 income year - $216,228;

    (b) 1998 income year - $201,693;

    (c) 1999 income year - $182,878.

  2. These are the amounts distributed by Wood Duck Pty Ltd, a company controlled by Mr Grant and in its capacity as trustee of the SNAG Trust, to beneficiaries of that trust, following the distribution of practice income from the Practice Trust to the SNAG Trust.

The IET schemes

  1. The IET schemes concerned income generated by a group of trusts known as the Income Earning Trusts under the control of Messrs Hart, Hoare, Grant and Collie. The IETs, through Cleary Hoare Corporate Pty Ltd as trustee for the CHC Discretionary Trust, carried on activities involving the implementing of tax avoidance arrangements for clients. Those arrangements involved the income of clients being passed through an NVI scheme. A feature of the arrangements was that, in the course of a complex series of transactions, amounts were directed through entities with available tax losses and/or tax exempt entities.
  2. Additionally, and relevantly for this matter, the income generated by the IET activities of marketing and implementing tax avoidance arrangements for clients was also passed through NVI schemes. That is to say, it was not only clients’ income that was passed through the schemes, but also the income earned by the IETs themselves from the business of implementing tax avoidance arrangements. It is that income from the IETs’ activities that is the subject of the dispute regarding the IET schemes.

The steps in the IET schemes

  1. The details of the IET schemes are set out in the AAT’s reasons, as follows:
    (a) 1997 IET scheme – at [142] and [144];

    (b) 1998 IET scheme - at [145] and [147];

    (c) 1999 IET scheme – at [67] and [149]; and

    (d) 2000 IET scheme – at [150] and [152].

  2. I adopt those descriptions of the IET schemes which are accepted by both parties.
  3. The Full Court summarised the IET schemes as follows:
    The IET Schemes commenced in 1997, and involved income earned by the IETs being subject to a series of transactions similar to those described above [ie in the summary of the Practice Trust schemes], but:

    (a) without any earlier income distribution to the SNAG Trust (or to any other trust related particularly to Mr Grant); and

    (b) rather than loans to the SNAG Trust, loans were made to two other trusts related to Mr Grant, the Lombard Trust (in 1997) and the SNAG No.2 Finance Trust (in 1998 to 2000).

  4. The loans in the final step were made by an entity controlled by the Cleary Hoare principals. For example, in the 1997 IET scheme, the loans were made by Lake Mylor Pty Ltd as trustee for the Zebra Capital Trust. Similar loans were made to Mr Hart’s and Mr Hoare’s entities. The loans to the Lombard Trust and the SNAG No 2 Trust were released after Mr Grant’s departure from Cleary Hoare, as recorded in the financial statements for those trusts. For 1997, 1998 and 1999, the amounts loaned to Mr Grant’s entities comprised around 25% of the total amount that passed to the entities of the four gentlemen. Coinciding with his departure from the firm, the percentage for Mr Grant’s entities in respect of the 2000 income year was much lower, around 2.45%.[21]

The Part IVA determinations (IET schemes) and alternative tax benefit calculations

  1. The Commissioner determined that Mr Grant obtained tax benefits in the following amounts and that those amounts would be included in Mr Grant’s taxable income for the relevant income years:
    (a) 1997 income year - $275,481;

    (b) 1998 income year - $797,329;

    (c) 1999 income year - $910,166;

    (d) 2000 income year - $150,000.

  2. The Commissioner relies on these amounts on the basis that Mr Grant’s entities would have received one-quarter of the income earned by the IETs in the relevant income years. That calculation results in amounts in excess of the alleged tax benefit amounts set out immediately above and in the Part IVA determinations. However, the Commissioner relies on the amounts determined by the Part IVA determinations and does not argue the tax benefit amounts should be increased.
  3. Alternatively, the Commissioner relies on lesser amounts based on the amounts the Commissioner identifies as having been paid, as loans, into bank accounts held by the trustee of Mr Grant’s trusts, as a proportion of the total of the amounts paid into bank accounts of entities associated with Messrs Hart, Hoare, Grant and Collie. That calculation generates the following alternative tax benefit calculations submitted by the Commissioner:
    (a) 1997 income year - $275,288;

    (b) 1998 income year - $771,288;

    (c) 1999 income year - $900,775;

    (d) 2000 income year - $115,827.

  4. In relation to this alternative calculation, there is a controversy between the parties regarding the IET income to which the proportions of the total loans made to entities associated with Messrs Hart, Hoare, Grant and Collie, represented by the total of the loans to entities associated with Mr Grant, should be applied to derive any tax benefit.
  5. The Commissioner adopted amounts advised by the trustee of the respective IETs in response to statutory requirements to provide information when those amounts were higher than the income returned by the trust. Mr Grant says the amounts returned should be adopted.

FACTS AND EVIDENCE

Agreed facts

  1. The Commissioner adopted the recitation of the relevant facts in Mr Grant’s opening submissions.[22]
  2. This detailed recitation of relevant facts – so far as it relates to the circumstances leading up to the assessments - is reproduced in the Appendix to these reasons with any necessary qualifications stated. I adopt this recitation of facts (as so qualified) as findings in the proceedings.

Evidence of Mr Grant

  1. Mr Grant provided three witness statements and was cross-examined.
  2. Mr Grant’s primary witness statement dated 30 June 2025 largely consolidates evidence before the AAT and addresses matters that are summarised in the agreed facts set out above. His evidence in relation to the factual background is largely unchallenged by the Commissioner. That is to say, the Commissioner does not say the evidence should not be accepted as honestly given but rather that it provides an insufficient foundation for the Tribunal to accept the reliability of Mr Grant’s counterfactuals.
  3. One important exception relates to Mr Grant’s evidence that he was effectively forced out of the Cleary Hoare practice in 1999 because he was not contributing to the generation of the IET income. Recalling that it was found to be loans, not distributions, that Mr Grant’s entities received from the IET schemes, that evidence provides part of the foundation on which Mr Grant asserts that, if not for the IET schemes, Mr Hart and Mr Collie would not have caused or allowed IET income to be distributed to Mr Grant.
  4. The Commissioner says that Mr Grant’s evidence that his retirement from Cleary Hoare was provoked by the threat of Mr Hart and Mr Collie to restructure the firm in a way that would exclude Mr Grant is difficult if not impossible to reconcile with contemporaneous documents executed by Mr Grant in connection with the Practice Trust and his departure from the firm. The Commissioner says Mr Grant’s evidence in this respect is not credible and should not be accepted.
  5. Mr Grant’s supplementary witness statement dated 3 September 2025 corrects certain errors in respect of trust distributions as noted below.
  6. Mr Grant’s third witness statement, dated 27 October 2025, sets out for each relevant income year the personal losses he had available; the trust income he maintains was available to be distributed; his other income and deductions; and how Mr Grants says he would have caused trust income to be distributed consistent with his asserted strategy of avoiding the accumulation of personal assets and ensuring no recipient of trust distributions incurred a tax liability at a marginal rate greater than the prevailing company tax rate.
  7. This third statement also addresses the expert advice of Mr Newman and Mr Laxon. As noted below, there are differences in the approach of the two experts. Mr Grant says he does not consider the differences to be particularly significant, and he would have followed advice of Mr Newman in those years in which Mr Newman acted for him – 1999 and 2000. In the years in which he did not engage Mr Newman, or if he had not done so, Mr Grant says he would have followed the advice of Mr Laxon if advice of that kind had been given. He says the opinion of Mr Laxon is consistent with the steps he took from 2004 onwards.
  8. Mr Grant’s third statement also addresses some relatively minor discrepancies in the numbers in the expert reports.
  9. In addition to his witness statements and oral evidence, there is a significant amount of documentary evidence contained in the hearing book.

Expert evidence

  1. In accordance with the ruling set out in Grant and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 2362, Mr Grant relied upon expert reports by two accountants, Mr Chris Newman and Mr Paul Laxon. Both accountants are professionally qualified with long experience. I accept that they are qualified to give expert evidence of the kind they have provided. Although both witnesses were cross-examined, the Commissioner did not submit otherwise.
  2. The experts’ reports opined as to the type of advice regarding distributions of trust income that they would have provided to Mr Grant in the relevant years.
  3. The experts were briefed with information concerning income said to be available to be distributed, available tax losses and persons and entities able to potentially receive income distributions, including Mr Grant’s de facto spouses at relevant times and corporate beneficiaries. They were not otherwise briefed with information regarding Mr Grant’s financial circumstances. They were not provided with information regarding Mr Grant’s living or other expenses or lifestyle and associated costs.
  4. The evidence of Mr Newman and Mr Laxon regarding the types of distributions they would have recommended was broadly similar. They both opined that to the extent that it would be offset by the accumulated losses, they would have advised that income be distributed to Mr Grant. The Commissioner also accepts that would have occurred.
  5. However, the advice differed as to the strategy to be applied to the distribution of the balance of the income. Mr Newman’s evidence was that he would have recommended distributions be made in such a way that “across the board” each taxpayer’s marginal tax rate would not exceed the prevailing company tax rate. Mr Laxon’s evidence was that he would have recommended distributions such that each taxpayer’s average tax rate did not exceed the company tax rate.
  6. Based on these strategies, each expert set out their evidence regarding the distributions they would have recommended each year and what tax would have been payable in accordance with their advice in each income year.
  7. There is no suggestion that the evidence of either accountant is not credible or not reasonably held, or otherwise not reliable evidence. I accept that the witnesses provided honest evidence regarding the advice they would have provided within the limits of the information with which they were briefed and consistent with their obligations as expert witnesses.

HAS MR GRANT PROVED HE DID NOT OBTAIN A TAX BENEFIT IN THE AMOUNTS DETERMINED BY THE COMMISSIONER IN RELATION TO THE PRACTICE TRUST SCHEMES IN THE 1997, 1998 AND 1999 INCOME YEARS?

Mr Grant’s counterfactuals (Practice Trust schemes)

  1. Mr Grant postulates that but for the Practice Trust schemes he would have:
    [F]irst utilised Accrued Personal Losses as an offset against income, and then caused associated discretionary trusts to not accumulate income but distribute income in each relevant year to:

    (a) himself and then his de facto partner, such that each of their income tax rate[s] did not exceed the corporate tax rate in each of those years; and

    (b) any remaining income available to a corporate beneficiary.

  2. Ms Noble was Mr Grant’s de facto spouse from 1993 to 1997. Ms McFadden was Mr Grant’s de facto spouse from 1998 to 2007.[23]
  3. In summary terms, Mr Grant mainly relies upon what he says are objective factors providing a foundation for conclusions that, for asset protection reasons, he would have avoided receiving significant income personally and, broadly speaking, would have received and acted on professional advice of the type reflected in the expert opinions of the accountants.
  4. Mr Grant says that, for the 1999 and 2000 income years, he likely would have received and acted upon the advice of Mr Newman who was his accountant for those years. The advice that would likely have been received would be consistent with Mr Newman’s expert opinion. For the other years – 1997, 1998, 2001 and 2002 – Mr Grant submits he would have received and acted on advice of the kind indicated by Mr Laxon.

The Commissioner’s counterfactuals (Practice Trust schemes)

  1. The Commissioner’s counterfactual for each of the 1997 to 1999 income years is that the SNAG Trust would or might reasonably be expected to have distributed the amounts identified by the Commissioner as the tax benefit to Mr Grant personally.

The circumstances identified by the parties (Practice Trust schemes)

  1. The circumstances identified as relevant to the reliability of Mr Grant’s counterfactuals may be grouped as relating to the following:
    (a) Mr Grant’s assertion that for asset protection reasons he would have avoided receiving income and accumulating assets personally, which gives rise to issues concerning:
    (i) Mr Grant’s indebtedness and other circumstances said to objectively support the assertion;

    (ii) the availability of other persons and entities to which income could have been distributed by relevant trusts.

    (b) Mr Grant’s access to funds for living and other expenses;

    (c) what is said to be supportive patterns of income distribution before and after the relevant years;

    (d) taxation advice which Mr Grant says he would have been likely to have received and acted upon, in relation to which the expert reports of the accountants is central to Mr Grant’s case;

    (e) whether Part IVA would apply to Mr Grant’s counterfactuals;

Asset protection

  1. The circumstances to which Mr Grant points in his submissions as objectively supporting a conclusion that it would not reasonably be expected that the contested income would have been included in his assessable income include his personal indebtedness in the amount of $250,000 of “old debt” related to the Cleary Hoare firm and his contingent liability under the guarantee he had provided of the Practice Trust’s credit facility in the amount of $300,000. He also guaranteed substantial commercial rental liabilities.
  2. I do not accept that those particular potential liabilities provide a strong foundation for a conclusion that Mr Grant would have avoided receiving income personally for asset protection reasons. Whether the existence of specified liabilities provides an objective rationale for avoiding receiving income personally invites consideration of what might occur if the liabilities were to be called upon. It is implicit in the submission that Mr Grant would have entered into the strategy on the footing that, if the debts were called upon, an option would be that Mr Grant, a practising solicitor, would default in payment of the liabilities.
  3. It is one thing to say that a professional person exposed to potential liability for professional negligence claims might seek to protect their assets. However, to say that Mr Grant would have been motivated to avoid receiving income personally by the existence of these liabilities which he freely entered into strikes me as grasping for factors on which to base the submission rather than objective factors in support of it. Further, the liabilities are relatively modest for a professional person.
  4. I accept that the existence of the two liabilities referenced in Mr Grant’s submissions, and the contingent lease liabilities, form part of Mr Grant’s overall financial situation which may inform an asset protection strategy. But that, in my view, is as far as these liabilities go as a foundation for Mr Grant’s counterfactuals.
  5. However, the drastic decline in the legal firm’s circumstances that occurred the early 1990s is, in my view, in a different category. The extent of the firm’s rapid decline is evidenced by the departure of eight partners and the decline in staff numbers from over 120 to less than 20 in a two-year period.
  6. Even though the proceedings concern a period some years later, I accept that living through that upheaval, financial strain and uncertainty would leave an enduring impact. That is to say, the circumstances surrounding what Mr Grant's submissions described as the near collapse of the firm provide an objective foundation for a strategy of Mr Grant avoiding the accumulation of assets personally. The remaining question is to what extent these considerations support a conclusion that it is not reasonably likely that Mr Grant would have received income in excess of the amounts contemplated by his counterfactuals.
  7. In that regard, the Commissioner submitted:
    40. Mr Grant not wanting to earn income or accumulate assets personally after the events that followed two partners of his firm being charged with criminal offences, did not place him in a different position from any other businessperson in Australia who is at risk of exposure to liability. Such caution does not require that a taxpayer personally earn no or only nominal income. A person may insulate themselves from that risk by separating their business undertakings and personal assets. This does not require a taxpayer to conduct its affairs in a way that involves never personally earning income.

    41. Rather than take the approach that he did of diverting Practice Trust and IET income away from himself, Mr Grant could instead have earned income personally, paid tax on that income, and then diverted it to an entity other than himself (once he had met his living expenses), which he then could have caused to purchase assets and hold wealth. That strategy would have sufficed to both insulate Mr Collie’s and his family’s assets from the claims of potential creditors and allowed him to meet his and his family’s living expenses.[24]

  8. I accept the Commissioner’s submission that asset protection does not require that no or minimal income be derived personally. To the extent that income is expended in meeting living costs and other expenses paid to third party suppliers for value, it is unlikely that it would be exposed to recovery in bankruptcy proceedings. However, the same cannot be said of diverting income derived by Mr Grant to an associated entity or trust which potentially would be exposed to recovery. The Commissioner’s submission in that respect is in my view unrealistic.
  9. I accept that Mr Grant had arranged his affairs such that there were beneficiaries to whom income could be distributed, consistent with his counterfactuals, including his de facto spouse at the relevant time – discussed further below – and companies. The latter included A Tough Road to Hoe Pty Ltd which was owned by Mr Grant’s father and Wood Duck Pty Ltd in which Mr Grant held a 50% ownership interest.

Access to funds for living and other expenses

  1. Mr Grant does not assert that he would have received no income from the Practice Trust personally under the Practice Trust schemes. His counterfactuals contemplate Mr Grant and his de facto spouse in the relevant years would have had access to the income the counterfactuals predict would have been distributed to them personally.
  2. However, that is not sufficient to support the reliability of the prediction that no other income would have been distributed to Mr Grant if the schemes had not been entered into. That is because the evidence does not enable me to form a picture of Mr Grant’s lifestyle during the relevant years.
  3. However, the AAT accepted that Mr Grant did not receive any distributions from the Practice Trust or the SNAG Trust during the relevant income years. It follows that, whatever his lifestyle expenses may have been, he was able to fund them from loans from the entities to which his share of the Practice Trust income was directed or from other sources.

Patterns of income distribution?

Earlier distributions?

  1. The Commissioner does not contest the first part of Mr Grant’s counterfactuals which would have the SNAG Trust distribute income to Mr Grant that is sufficient to be offset against his personal tax losses and that the Practice Trust income for the 1997 income year would be fully offset by losses.
  2. However, the Commissioner does not accept that part of Mr Grant’s postulate which would have distributions made to Mr Grant’s de facto spouses. In that regard, the Commissioner notes that Mr Grant never caused any trust to make distributions to either of his de facto spouses prior to the implementation of the schemes.
  3. Mr Grant’s response, which I accept as it is consistent with the undisturbed finding of the AAT regarding the existence of the losses, is that he believed he had access to tax losses in the preceding years. Accordingly, there was no cause for Mr Grant to contemplate distributions to a de facto spouse in that period.
  4. As Mr Grant put it in submissions, he in fact caused distributions to be made, just of a different kind. That may be so, but it does little to assist Mr Grant’s case.

Subsequent distributions?

  1. In respect of the 2003 and subsequent years, Mr Grant referred to distributions made by the Grants Lawyers Trust and later a further practice trust, the SG Practice Trust.
  2. In his 30 June 2025 witness statement, Mr Grant stated that in 2003 distributions of $12,000 and $8,000 were made to Mr Grant and Ms McFadden respectively. The balance of the Grants Lawyers Trust income of $137,207.16 was distributed to a number of trusts, including an amount of $61,228.16 distributed to The Genius 2002 Trust. Mr Grant gave unchallenged evidence that each of the trusts had available tax losses.
  3. However, in a subsequent statement dated 3 September 2025, Mr Grant corrected that evidence. He stated that the evidence in the 30 June 2025 statement was based on the Grant Lawyers Trust financial statements but that on further investigation he found that the distribution of $8,000 was not made to Ms McFadden and that the total amount of $69,228.16 ($61,228.16 plus $8,000) was paid to The Genius No. 2 Trust. As the financial statements for The Genius 2002 Trust indicate, the trust had losses against which the income of $69,228.16 could be fully offset.
  4. In respect of the 2004 to 2019 income years, Mr Grant deposed that he acted upon advice by ensuring that income from the Grant Lawyers Trust and the SG Practice Trust was distributed to himself, his sister, his de facto spouse and other entities in a way that the marginal tax rate applicable to any beneficiary did not exceed the prevailing corporate tax rate.
  5. However, Mr Grant does not identify any evidence that distributions of income were made by any trust with which he is associated to Ms Noble or Ms McFadden. The upshot is that, while Mr Grant asserts that he would have caused income to be distributed to his de facto spouse at the relevant time, there is no evidence other than Mr Grant’s assertion that he ever did so before, during or after the relevant income years. Nor did he support the assertions regarding the pattern of distributions in this period by reference to specific amounts distributed to individual beneficiaries or demonstrate that such distributions were consistent with the asserted strategy of limiting the tax exposure to the corporate tax rate. Reference to the financial statements and tax returns identified in closing submissions did not assist in that regard.[25]

Taxation advice

  1. Mr Grant gave evidence that he relied on taxation advice regarding the distribution of income by his entities. He submitted that it should be accepted that he would have received and acted upon advice of the kind indicated by the expert accountants, Mr Newman and Mr Laxon.
  2. A number of barriers stand in the way of accepting that submission.
  3. The expert opinions were based only on taxation considerations. They were not informed by instructions regarding the extent or nature of the expenditure that Mr Grant may have been required to or wish to meet. Nor were they informed by any information regarding Mr Grant’s relationship with his de facto spouses at the relevant times other than the existence of the relationships and their status as beneficiaries of the relevant trusts. Instructions were not provided to the experts regarding the extent of the capacity of corporate beneficiaries to make loans to Mr Grant to meet living or other expenses.
  4. Similarly, aside from the broad asset protection considerations, Mr Grant’s submission regarding the particular distributions that it is said would have been made, based on the taxation advice, is founded on an unstated premise that his decisions regarding distributions would be driven entirely by taxation considerations, devoid of any emotional or other considerations aside from asset protection.
  5. That assumes a particular importance in respect of the income that the experts say they would have advised be distributed to Mr Grant’s de facto spouse in the relevant years. The issue is not relevant in respect of the 1997 income year. That is because Mr Grant had available sufficient losses to fully offset any distributions that would have been made in respect of both schemes. It is an issue in respect of the 1999 to 2000 income years because Mr Grant’s counterfactuals contemplate distributions to Ms McFadden in respect of those years.
  6. There is no significant evidence regarding Mr Grant’s relationship with Ms McFadden beyond the existence of the relationship and a formal separation agreement governing their financial affairs when the relationship ended.[26] I accept that it was a serious relationship. However, it does not follow that Mr Grant would necessarily cause substantial income to be directed to Ms McFadden.
  7. The issue would also arise in respect of the 1998 year if the Tribunal does not accept that no distributions would have been made for Mr Grant’s benefit from the IETs in that year, because in that event Mr Grant’s counterfactual and submissions contemplate distributions to Ms Noble.
  8. It is not clear to me why distributions would be made to Ms Noble in respect of the 1998 income year when the relationship ended in the 1997 calendar year.[27] Commonly trust distributions would be determined towards the end of an income year when the extent of the income available for distribution is clearer by which time this relationship would have ended.
  9. However, whether the counterfactual posits distributions to Ms Noble or Ms McFadden, there is, as already indicated, no particularised evidence of distributions that Mr Grant caused to be made to any spouse or de facto spouse before, during or after the relevant income years, including in the period 2004 to 2019. Mr Grant gave general evidence in respect of that period but did not identify any distribution that was made to a spouse in that period. While a distribution to Ms McFadden appears to have been contemplated in the 2003 income year, as discussed above, no such distribution occurred. Mr Grant could not offer any explanation for why the apparently contemplated distribution to Ms McFadden did not occur.

Would Mr Grant’s counterfactuals attract Part IVA (Practice Schemes)?

  1. Mr Grant submits that the Tribunal should not address this question as it would be procedurally unfair. The issue was not identified in the Commissioner’s Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions and was first raised in the Commissioner’s Opening Submissions and without detailed submissions regarding the steps in the alleged scheme or addressing the factors relevant to determining whether it would be concluded that the scheme was entered into or carried out with the dominant purpose of obtaining a tax benefit.
  2. The Commissioner’s Opening Submissions were filed on Wednesday 5 November 2025 in advance of the hearing that commenced on 10 November 2026. I agree that it is unsatisfactory for an issue of this importance to have been raised at such a late stage.
  3. On the other hand, Mr Grant could have, but did not, seek additional time to respond to the submission or other directions. Nor is there any suggestion that responsive evidence could have been filed. Rather, Mr Grant chose to respond by raising the procedural fairness issue and arguing that in any case Part IVA would not apply to Mr Grant’s counterfactuals because it would be concluded that at least an equivalent purpose to obtaining a tax benefit would be the protection of Mr Grant’s assets.
  4. In those circumstances, I have considered the Commissioner’s argument that Part IVA would have applied to Mr Grant’s counterfactuals. In that respect, at the hearing I queried how the burden of proof that Mr Grant bears to prove the assessments are excessive would apply in respect of the hypothetical application of Part IVA to Mr Grant’s counterfactuals.
  5. Ms Derrington, who appeared for Mr Grant, submitted that the burden would be on the Commissioner to establish that Mr Grant’s counterfactuals would be Part IVA schemes. That was said to be on the basis that it is for the Commissioner, having submitted that these alternative facts in the counterfactuals give rise to an alternative consequence, to establish that the alternative facts as asserted by Mr Grant give rise to a different legal conclusion.[28]
  6. I do not accept that submission. If it were accepted, a taxpayer would effectively reverse the burden of proof by putting forward a counterfactual that might itself be impugned under Part IVA and requiring the Commissioner to prove that Part IVA would apply to the taxpayer’s postulate. The submission is also inconsistent with the Full Court’s finding in the Hart case that “the appellant has not discharged the onus of demonstrating that the alternative transactions he has put forward would not have been subject to Part IVA”.[29]
  7. Indeed, the further observations of the Full Court in Hart have some resonance in respect of Mr Grant’s submissions that he would have made distributions to his de facto spouse and others:
    It is true that, as a matter of logic flowing from the objective position of [Mr Hart’s trust] and the identify of its potential range of discretionary beneficiaries, one could speculate about the possibility of distributions being made, for example, to children, that might not, without more, be capable of being impugned by Part IVA. But the appellant’s case rose no higher than speculation.
  8. Mr Grant’s case differs from Hart in that Mr Grant put forward with precision the distributions that he says would have been made and relies on evidence in that regard. The difficulty for Mr Grant is that the evidence is not sufficient to support the reliability of his counterfactuals.
  9. While Mr Grant relies on the distributions he says would have been made to, among others, his de facto spouse, the evidence in support of that contention is primarily Mr Grant’s own assertions and the evidence of the accountants as to the advice they would have given. As already mentioned, other than Mr Grant’s broad statement, there is no evidence that was brought to my attention of Mr Grant ever having caused distributions to have been made to a de facto spouse over the period in excess of 20 years referenced in his submissions. Nor is there any evidence or analysis of evidence to make good Mr Grant’s broad assertion as to his practice in relation to distributions said to be consistent with his counterfactuals.[30]
  10. The evidence of the accountants suffers, as indicated earlier, from the lack of foundation facts beyond the amounts said to be available for distribution, the accumulated tax losses and the beneficiaries available as potential recipients of distributions. Consequently, and relevant to the potential impugning of his counterfactuals under Part IVA, the evidence is directed only to tax outcomes, including ensuring that a significant part of the income that has as its ultimate source Mr Grant’s professional work does not form part of his assessable income. The advice the experts say would have been given does not on its face take into account other, non-tax factors that may be relevant to the exercise of a trustee’s discretion.
  11. The Commissioner says that in assessing the potential impugning of Mr Grant’s counterfactuals under Part IVA the advice of the accountants would be objective evidence and not excluded as subjective evidence of intention which is irrelevant to Part IVA considerations. Even if that is not correct, an objective mathematical exercise would reveal that distributions made in conformity with either Mr Newman’s or Mr Laxon’s advice were undertaken to achieve a tax outcome that includes ensuring a significant amount of income is not taxed in Mr Grant’s hands.
  12. Having regard to the evidence available as discussed above and those considerations, I am not persuaded that it would not be concluded that a scheme involving distributions made in the amounts the accountants would have recommended would not have been carried out by Mr Grant for the dominant purpose of obtaining tax benefits and would not have resulted in such benefits.

Conclusion: Mr Grant has not discharged the burden of proving he did not obtain tax benefits in relation to the Practice Trust schemes

  1. For the reasons I have indicated, although put forward with greater precision than the taxpayer in Hart, I am not persuaded that Mr Grant’s counterfactuals in respect of the Practice Trust schemes rise beyond speculation.
  2. The distributions that it is asserted would be made, particularly in respect of his de facto spouses, are not supported by any identified pattern of distributions of that kind. That is explicable in respect of the absence of such distributions before and during the relevant years by the accumulated tax losses against which Mr Grant’s income would be offset. It is not explicable in respect of the subsequent period of over 15 years.
  3. Additionally, the evidence of both accountants, and Mr Grant’s evidence that he would have acted on the advice received, suffers from the absence of the type of information on which accounting advice regarding the making of distributions would be expected to be based. In particular, Mr Grant’s financial circumstances including the expenditure that he would make during each of the relevant periods to meet living and lifestyle expenses; acquire assets; repay loans on which the viability of the counterfactuals rested; and obligations to repay debts to third parties relating to his professional practice and the IET schemes.
  4. None of this is to reject that asset protection would have played a part in the counterfactuals. I have taken that into account as a significant factor. Rather, it is to say that the evidence does not support Mr Grant’s counterfactuals sufficiently to conclude that they constitute a reliable prediction of what would have occurred but for the schemes.
  5. Additionally, for the reasons indicated above, Mr Grant has not proved that his Practice Scheme counterfactuals would not be impugned by Part IVA.

HAS MR GRANT PROVED HE DID NOT OBTAIN A TAX BENEFIT IN THE AMOUNTS DETERMINED BY THE COMMISSIONER IN RELATION TO THE IET SCHEMES IN THE 1997, 1998, 1999 AND 2000 INCOME YEARS?

Mr Grant’s counterfactuals (IET schemes)

  1. Mr Grant’s primary postulate is that if the IET schemes had not been carried out, neither he nor any associated entities would have received any income from the IETs. At most, Mr Grant or associated entities would have received loans requiring repayment which, on his departure from Cleary Hoare, would have been released.
  2. In summary terms, that submission is mainly based on the proposition that Mr Grant was not involved in generating the IET income and therefore Mr Hart and Mr Collie would have been unlikely to cause income to be distributed to Mr Grant or his trusts, rather than make loans as in fact occurred as the AAT found. Further, that for that reason Mr Grant was forced out of Cleary Hoare and the releasing of loans made to his entities as part of the IET schemes occurred in that context.
  3. Alternatively, if the Tribunal concludes that Mr Grant’s trusts would have received income from the IETs, Mr Grant says his trusts would have received no more than the amounts that were loaned to his trusts.
  4. Further, under this alternative, Mr Grant says that (consistent with his counterfactuals in respect of the Practice Trust schemes) his trusts would have distributed the income first to Mr Grant and his de facto spouse at the time such that their effective tax rates did not exceed the then prevailing corporate tax rate, with the remaining income being distributed to corporate beneficiaries.

The Commissioner’s counterfactuals (IET schemes)

  1. The Commissioner’s counterfactual for each of the 1997 to 2000 income years is that the IETs would or might reasonably be expected to have distributed the amounts identified by the Commissioner as tax benefits to a discretionary trust controlled by Mr Grant which would then have distributed those amounts to Mr Grant personally.

The circumstances identified by the parties

Mr Grant’s contribution to the IETs and his departure from Cleary Hoare

  1. Mr Grant’s primary counterfactuals in respect of the IET schemes are that, absent the schemes, Mr Hart and Mr Collie would not have caused IET income to be distributed to Mr Grant.
  2. I accept Mr Grant’s evidence that it was Messrs Hart and Collie, and not Mr Grant, who devised and marketed the schemes. I have no reason not to accept Mr Grant’s evidence in that regard.
  3. I do not accept that Mr Grant played no other role in the conduct of the IETs. I accept that he played a more limited role as he asserts in his evidence, but he was a director of the corporate trustees of various participating entities. He signed documents in that capacity. He was in any case effectively a part-owner of the business along with Messrs Hart, Collie and Hoare.
  4. Additionally, it would not make commercial sense for Messrs Hart and Collie to cause substantial unsecured loans to be made to Mr Grant if he were not contributing in some not insignificant way to the IET operations. Why would they tie up substantial amounts in loans to a business partner that could have been made available to the other principals? That is not to suggest, contrary to the undisturbed AAT finding, that the loans were not repayable. Rather, it is to indicate that Mr Grant could have been expected to receive some return on account of being effectively a part-owner of the business and contributing, albeit to a more limited extent than others, to the business.
  5. Further, Mr Collie could have been but was not called to provide evidence regarding the circumstances giving rise to these arrangements nor regarding the circumstances of the departure of Mr Grant.
  6. However, I do not find it necessary to decide the controversy between the parties regarding whether Mr Grant was “forced out” of the firm or could have been forced out. Whether or not that is the case, the fact is that Mr Grant left the firm with effect from 30 June 1999. It is incongruous to suggest, as Mr Grant does, that if the schemes were not entered into he would not have received income because he had left the firm when his entities received loans totalling some $655,500 in that very year including an amount of $90,000 on 31 May 1999 - only a month before his departure and subsequent to execution of Heads of Agreement on 14 April 1999 concerning his retirement from the firm[31] - and a further $150,000 loan on 6 July 1999 only some six days after leaving the firm.[32]
  7. It is also incongruous to suggest that the remaining loans were forgiven as part of the separation arrangements. The Deed of Mutual Release releasing the loans was not executed until some two years later on 4 August 2001 and gave effect to releases of all loans associated with the IET schemes, not just those owed by Mr Grant’s entities. Further, the deed acknowledges that it “was always the intention of the Releasors that, ultimately, they would settle accounts among themselves.”[33]

Conclusion: Mr Grant has not discharged the burden of proving he did not obtain tax benefits (IET schemes)

  1. For those reasons, I do not accept Mr Grant’s primary counterfactuals that absent the IET schemes he would have received no income from the IETs. Mr Grant was effectively one of four owners of a business in which he participated and which was profitable. I do not accept that he would have received no return from his ownership interest and participation in the business.
  2. In respect of the alternative counterfactuals that Mr Grant would have caused trust distributions to have been made in accordance with the accountants’ evidence, the parties largely relied upon the same submissions made in respect of the Practice Trust schemes.
  3. For the reasons given in respect of those schemes, I do not accept that Mr Grant has proved that his alternative counterfactuals premised on obtaining and adopting advice of that kind are reliable predictions of what would have occurred but for the IET schemes.

Quantification of tax benefits

  1. If I reached the conclusion that Mr Grant had not proved his primary or alternative counterfactuals, the parties were at odds regarding how the tax benefit should be quantified.
  2. The Commissioner’s primary submission is that Mr Grant’s share of the IET income would be one-quarter of the total income of the IETs, on the basis that he was effectively one of four owners of the business. In calculating the total income of the IETs from which to derive Mr Grant’s one-quarter share, the Commissioner referred to two sources: the IETs’ tax returns and responses provided by Mr Collie on behalf of the corporate trustees to compulsory information notices issued by the Commissioner. Where the amounts in those responses were higher than the amounts that were returned, the Commissioner submitted the higher amounts should be adopted. Where that approach arrived at amounts higher than in the Part IVA determinations, the Commissioner was content to adopt the amounts in the determinations.
  3. Mr Grant submitted that the Commissioner should be confined to the amounts in the assessments. No authority was cited for that proposition other than a comment by the member in the course of the AAT hearing that ordinarily the net income of a trust is the amount stated in the trust’s tax return. No reliance is placed on conclusive evidence provisions.
  4. I do not accept Mr Grant’s submission in this regard. I can identify no basis on which the income of the IETs should not be calculated by reference to the actual income of the IETs. It is to be expected that the responses of Mr Collie, a lawyer of longstanding to compulsory notices, would have been carefully prepared.
  5. However, this conclusion is not determinative in view of my conclusion as to the next aspect on which the parties differ regarding quantification of the tax benefit. As indicated, the Commissioner’s primary submission is that the income which it is reasonable to conclude Mr Grant would have received, should be calculated as one-quarter of the total IET income calculated in the manner just outlined. Alternatively, the Commissioner says Mr Grant’s share could be calculated by applying to the total IET income the proportion of the loans made to Mr Grant’s entities in each income year as a proportion of the total of the loans made to the entities of the four owners.
  6. Mr Grant rejects both of these propositions and says that, if the counterfactuals based on Mr Grant receiving no IET income or amounts limited to those reflecting the accountants’ evidence are rejected, the income that he would have received but for the schemes should be limited to the amounts of the loans his entities received. I accept this submission. It is grounded in the reality that the loan amounts are the amounts that found their way to Mr Grant or his entities from the IET income. Additionally, that the amounts received as loans are less than one-quarter of the total IET income is consistent with the more limited role played by Mr Grant in the IET business.

SHOULD THE TRIBUNAL MAKE A COMPENSATING ADJUSTMENT IN RESPECT OF THE 2002 INCOME YEAR?

  1. A controversy arose between the parties as to whether, depending on the Tribunal’s conclusions regarding the schemes, a compensating adjustment should be made under

    s 177F of the ITAA 1936 by the Tribunal in respect of the 2002 income year or left to Mr Grant to make a request to the Commissioner and, if dissatisfied with the outcome, exercise appeal or review rights under s 177F(7).
  2. There may be, as the Commissioner put it in oral submissions, a nice question regarding whether consideration of the making of a compensating adjustment would be within the terms of the remitter and in any case it would require an extension of Mr Grant’s grounds of objection against the relevant assessments.
  3. This issue arose because the Commissioner’s assessments were made in accordance with his earlier contention that Mr Grant’s accumulated personal losses were exhausted in the 1994 to 1996 income years and therefore no longer available to be offset against income in the 2001 and 2002 income years. The Commissioner abandoned that contention in light of the AAT’s undisturbed findings in respect of the losses remaining available at the commencement of the 1997 income year.
  4. However, the losses would be exhausted in the 1997 and 1998 income years on the parties’ counterfactuals. Mr Grant argues that, because that would occur only because of the Commissioner’s determinations, a compensating adjustment should be made in respect of the 2002 income year.[34]
  5. It is not altogether clear whether or in what way Mr Grant would say the issue arises under the particular conclusions I have reached - which do not align with either parties’ submissions - or, if so, what discretionary considerations arise in those circumstances. Additionally, the issue was not raised earlier in Mr Grant’s Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions.
  6. In those circumstances, the fairer approach for both parties is not to seek to resolve the compensating adjustment issue at this time. It will be open to Mr Grant to request that the Commissioner make a compensating adjustment and provide submissions and supporting materials he considers appropriate in the context of the Tribunal’s conclusions and, as noted earlier, exercise appeal or review rights if dissatisfied with the Commissioner’s decision.

DISPOSITION OF THE REVIEW

  1. The parties are agreed that the matter should be remitted to the Commissioner to give effect to the Tribunal’s conclusions in respect of the remitted questions and any flow-on effect upon the penalty assessments. I have adopted that course.
  2. The Tribunal’s decision reflects the rejection of Mr Grant’s counterfactuals but acceptance of his alternative submission regarding the calculation of the tax benefits in respect of the IET schemes based on the amounts loaned to his entities. In other words, the assessable amounts are the amounts that are set out in the Part IVA determinations in respect of the Practice Trust schemes and the amounts loaned to Mr Grant in respect of the IET schemes.
  3. In making assessments to give effect to the Tribunal’s conclusions in respect of the remitted questions and the AAT’s decision regarding the accumulated personal losses, the Commissioner will need to allow deductions for the accumulated losses of $347,488 as at 1 July 1996, commencing in the 1997 income year, until they are exhausted. As the parties agreed, consequential amendments to the assessments of administrative penalties will require consideration.

14. The law firm Cleary and Hoare (the Firm) commenced under a partnership (the Partnership) in 1981 between Donald Cleary (Mr Cleary) and John Hoare (Mr Hoare). The Cleary and Hoare Office Unit Trust (CHOUT) was a service trust that (by its trustee, Chol Corporation (No.1) Pty Ltd (Chol)) employed the Firm’s administrative staff and provided administrative services to the Firm.

15. Mr Grant commenced articles of clerkship at the Firm in 1982 and was admitted to legal practice as a solicitor in 1987. In late 1989, Mr Grant was a salaried partner of the Firm and was offered the opportunity to become an equity partner and acquire a 10% equity interest in the Firm.

16. On 1 January 1990, Mr Grant accepted that offer by purchasing 10% of the equity for $180,000 (borrowed from the National Australia Bank (NAB)) and acquiring 10% of the units in CHOUT. On about 1 July 1990, Mr Grant purchased a further 2% of the equity in the Firm for $32,000, borrowing those monies from Com-Law (No. 51) Pty Ltd (an entity controlled by Raymund Grant, Mr Grant’s father).

17. After becoming a partner of the Partnership, Mr Grant gave guarantees to NAB in respect of monies borrowed by the Partnership and CHOUT of up to $2,216,430 (the Old Debt).

The Charges

18. In 1990, two partners of the Firm were charged with criminal offences (the Charges). After the Charges were laid, large clients left the Firm and eight of the Firm’s partners also departed through to 1992. In that period, the Partnership and CHOUT traded at a loss, but continued to operate with the support of the NAB.

19. By 1992, staff numbers at Cleary Hoare had fallen from over 120 to fewer than 20. Consequently, the Firm no longer needed to occupy three levels at the Colonial Mutual Building at 300 Queen Street, Brisbane (CML) that the Firm then occupied under a lease.

20. In 1992, CHOUT (by its trustee, Chol) executed a lease for a tenancy of 9 years in respect of two levels of CML for a rental of $440,000 per annum (Lease), and Mr Grant gave a guarantee in respect of the Lease.

21. On 1 August 1993, the Firm vacated the Lease premises, notwithstanding that the Lease still had 8 years left to run. CHOUT’s contingent liability in respect of the Lease was in excess of $3,520,000 (i.e., 8 years x $440,000 per year).

22. Moreover, the Firm’s adverse circumstances left Mr Grant with substantial personal losses of approximately $347,000 by the 1994 tax year (Accrued Personal Losses).

23. Owing to the events described at [18]-[22] above, Mr Grant asserts that he sought to avoid earning income or accumulating assets personally into the future.

Michael Hart and the Restructure

24. In 1990, Mr Hart joined the Firm as a salaried partner and practised in tax law. Prior to that time, the Firm had no practitioner who practised in taxation.

25. In 1992, Mr Cleary, Mr Hoare and Mr Grant (the Partnership’s remaining equity partners) discussed with Mr Hart a new structure for the Firm, and subsequently agreed to seek to restructure the Firm such that it would be operated by a new unit trust and Mr Hart would become the managing principal of the Firm.

26. Between December 1992 and January 1993, Mr Hart, Mr Cleary, Mr Hoare and Mr Grant sought and obtained permission from the Queensland Law Society to conduct the Firm under a unit trust structure.

27. On 29 January 1993, the Cleary Hoare Practice Trust (Practice Trust) was established, and on that day acquired the goodwill, business name and work in progress of the Firm (the Restructure).

28. Following the Restructure, the principal solicitors of the Firm (CH Principals) were Mr Hart, Mr Cleary, Mr Hoare and Mr Grant. The trustees of the Practice Trust were originally Mr Cleary, Mr Hoare and Mr Grant. But by mid-1994, Mr Cleary ceased to be a trustee and Mr Hart was appointed.

29. Ordinary units in the Practice Trust were owned by discretionary trusts associated with the CH Principals (CH Principals Discretionary Trusts). The relevant discretionary trust for Mr Hart was the Outlook Practice Trust (Outlook Trust). In relation to Mr Grant, the relevant discretionary trust was the Sensitive New Age Guy Trust (SNAG Trust), which was established on 29 January 1993. At all relevant times, Wood Duck Pty Ltd (Wood Duck) was the trustee of the SNAG Trust, and Mr Grant controlled the SNAG Trust and Wood Duck.

30. As a consequence of the Restructure, the Practice Trust (by its trustees) operated the Firm’s legal practice. That effectively separated the business of the Firm from the CH Principals in their personal capacities.

31. Between 1994 and 1999, the Practice Trust made income distributions to the SNAG Trust as a holder of ordinary units in the Practice Trust. Neither the SNAG Trust nor the Practice Trust distributed any income to Mr Grant personally.

Financing from NAB following the Restructure

32. In 1994, following negotiation with the NAB after the Restructure was implemented, the Practice Trust obtained a credit facility from the NAB for $350,000. Mr Grant provided a guarantee for that facility and assumed liability for $250,000 of the Old Debt (the Applicant’s

Portion). Each of Mr Hoare and Mr Cleary also personally assumed liability for a portion of the Old Debt.

Mr Collie and Comlaw

37. Prior to 1992, Ian Collie (Mr Collie) was known to Mr Hart. He lived in Melbourne and had been engaged in legal practice in tax law. In 1992, Mr Hart suggested to Mr Collie that he should relocate to Brisbane and establish a business.

38. In December 1992, Mr Collie moved to Brisbane and the Comlaw Trust (Comlaw) was established. Comlaw was a unit trust in which the units were issued one half to the Practice Trust and one half to the Canowindra Trust (representing Mr Collie’s related interests).

39. The trustee of Comlaw was Lake Morundah Pty Ltd, which subsequently changed its name to Comlaw Consultants Pty Ltd (Comlaw Consultants). Its directors were, at relevant times, Mr Hart, Mr Collie and Mr Hoare. Mr Grant was not a director. Nor did he have any involvement in the management or operation of Comlaw Consultants.

40. Comlaw’s operated its business until 1996. It operated separately from the Firm and was run by Mr Collie and Mr Hart. In the course of Comlaw’s business, Mr Collie and Mr Hart caused the Annesley Trust to be established.

41. Though originally contemplated as a vehicle that would take up property development opportunities, Comlaw started earning income through the provision of tax-planning products (Comlaw Tax Products) that were developed by Mr Collie and Mr Hart.

42. Mr Collie and Mr Hart promoted Comlaw Tax Products to accountants and tax advisers, and to (other) prospective clients (Comlaw Promotional Activities). Mr Grant asserts that he was not involved in Comlaw Promotional Activities, nor did Mr Grant’s practice within the Firm involve the provision of tax planning products or tax advice, or services related to implementing Comlaw Tax Products.

Background to the NVI Scheme and the IET Business

44. In about mid-1996, Mr Hart and Mr Collie showed the NVI Opinion to Mr Grant and said to him that the NVI scheme involved an entity’s income being set off against accumulated losses in a separate entity and that the NVI Opinion supported the view that the NVI scheme was lawful.

Mr Grant’s Departure from the Firm

69. Mr Grant asserts that on or about early 1999, Mr Collie and Mr Hart said to Mr Grant that they were unhappy that he (Mr Grant) did not work in the IET Business, and Mr Collie said that it was not fair that he and Mr Hart were generating all the intellectual property of the IET Business (an apparent reference to the IET Tax Products, from which income was generated for the IET Business).

70. Mr Grant asserts that in or around early April 1999, Mr Hart, Mr Collie, and Mr Hoare said to Mr Grant that he should tender his resignation from the Firm, or they would restructure to exclude him from the Practice Trust and the IETs. Feeling he had no choice in the matter, Mr Grant agreed to resign effective 30 June 1999, and executed a Heads of Agreement dated 14 April 1999 in that regard. The Commissioner disputes the circumstances of Mr Grant’s departure from the firm.

71. Mr Grant asserts that between 14 April 1999 and 30 June 1999, Mr Grant and Mr Hart orally agreed that in consideration for Mr Grant having:

(a) previously paid to acquire an equity interest in the Firm;

(b) stayed and worked in the Firm following the Charges and the Firm’s downturn, when other partners had left;

(c) been part of the Restructure, and the Firm’s return to prosperity; and

(d) having been a director of NVI scheme entities, at the request of Mr Collie and Mr Hart;

any loans made to Mr Grant’s related entities by any Practice Trust Scheme or IET Scheme entities should be forgiven. As noted in the AAT’s reasons for decision, the release of those loans on 30 June 1999 was recorded in the financial statements of the SNAG Trust, the Lombard Trust and SNAG No. 2.

72. On 30 June 1999, Mr Grant resigned from the Firm and commenced practice in his own firm. From 1 July 1999 onwards, Mr Grant had no involvement in the Practice Trust, the IET Business or the IETs, other than to finalise his resignation. The finalisation of Mr Grant’s resignation included resigning on 21 July 1999 as a director of each of CHCPL, LMPL, Iapetus Pty Ltd and Xenaberry Pty Ltd.

73. On 4 August 2001, a deed was executed that recorded that any loans made to the SNAG Trust, the Lombard Trust or SNAG No.2 by relevant Ancillary Trusts within the Practice Trust Schemes or the IET Schemes were released.
