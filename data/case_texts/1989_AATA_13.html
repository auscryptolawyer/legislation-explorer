REASONS FOR DECISION

20 January 1389 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant in this reference is a well-presented man aged 40 years and of youthful appearance. That is in keeping with his evidence that he has never smoked cigarettes; is only a occasional drinker; and is a regular jogger. For many years he has worked for his present employer as a tow-truck operator but not in the accident scene arena. His employer throughout the relevant years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1983 inclusive was a person bearing the same surname as the Applicant. The evidence in that regard did not go beyond that simple fact. It was not suggested in cross-examination that there was any other association between the employer and the Applicant other than master and servant. He married in 1965 and two children were born of the marriage. The elder, a girl born in 1966, gave evidence. In 1980, with the assistance of a gift from his parents of $10,000, he and his wife managed to purchase a home. The purchase price was $47,500. The purchase was funded with a first mortgage of $28,000; by drawings on savings; and by realization of some assets. The Applicant has continued to reside in that home since that date but his wife left him during 1984. His elder daughter - as a nineteen year old - left home during 1985.

  1. The evidence of father and daughter well satisfies me that the father was something of a "Steptoe". He had a habit of collecting bits and pieces, ranging from old cars to coins. I accept that many of the items to be later mentioned were found by him in checking out cars which came his way when they were abandoned by former owners. I also accept that, in relation to items such as cars and other bric-a-brac capable of being restored, he spent much of his spare time effecting such restoration. I am satisfied that it was commonplace for two or three old cars to be about his home either in course of or awaiting renovation: something which sometimes did not eventuate. In relation to the elder daughter I accept that her father, in order to encourage her to qualify for her Higher School Certificate, promised her a car and, with that end in mind, in particular he renovated an old sedan which became hers to use when she obtained her Higher School Certificate. Having commenced full-time studies as a TAFE student she had little need for the car and it was sold to a tradesman who had attended at the family home to carry out repairs. I accept that later, after the relevant time, another car was provided for the daughter by her father.
  2. The evidence of the Applicant was that his only source of assessable income was his employment; that he was paid in cash by his employer; that most of the moneys he received were entrusted to his wife who was generally responsible for banking and such matters; and that he believed his group certificates from his employer to be in all respects correct. I find that he was not a person who was concerned to, or who attempted to, reconcile his group certificate to all of the individual payments made during the course of a financial year.

Although to some the amount involved ($13,425.67) would not be large, I am satisfied that the claim is massive to a person such as the Applicant. That is so whether or not the assessments properly lie.

  1. The Applicant objected to the assessments and his objections were disallowed. He then requested an independent review. Fortunately for him, his request was made when it was for he was not required, as a pre-requisite to his challenges, to pay fees of $960 - $240 for each year of assessment - in order to open the way to a single, brief hearing before a single member of the Tribunal. Now, if he could raise $960 within sixty days, he would enjoy the same right to an independent review as the wealthy.
  2. When he was assessed as liable to pay in all $6,644.29 by way of additional tax for incorrect returns, no explanation was offered him as to why the amounts which were assessed should have been selected. That information only came to light at the hearing when the Commissioner's representative advised that, of the "penalties" provided for by the Parliament ($13,562.76 -being 200% of tax avoided), the Commissioner had exercised his discretion and remitted penalties (in accordance with his common practice) to an amount equal to 20% per annum of tax avoided for near the period of alleged avoidance ($3,628) plus 40% of the tax avoided ($2,714) - a total imposed of $6,342.
  3. The importance to the Applicant of issues relating to additional tax is such that the failure of the tax agent to expressly raise the question of review of penalties in the objections is quite remarkable and almost certainly reflects negligence on the part of the agent. I say that because that omission has prompted the Commissioner's representative to contend that the Tribunal has no power to review the Commissioner's exercise of discretion as to remission of additional tax. Nonetheless the Commissioner's representative acknowledged that, if the increases in tax were set aside, the imposition of additional tax would wholly fall; and, if the increases in tax were reduced, the impositions of additional tax would be reduced appropriately.
  4. If the investigation officers had any information suggesting that the Applicant had sources of income not acknowledged to the Commissioner in the returns, that circumstance was not disclosed to the Tribunal. As a result of the investigation the Commissioner's officers took account of the Applicant's claims to have sold property - not in the course of business and not being property acquired for any profit-making purpose. They prepared a "source and application of funds" statement. In doing so, they recorded an estimate of "cash expenditure" on sums of $80, $90, $100 and $110 a week in the relevant years. The basis for doing so was not established in evidence. Instead it was argued for the Commissioner that, as the Applicant had not advanced any evidence in support of the contention that the estimate was incorrect, or indeed contended that the estimate was incorrect, in that respect the Applicant had failed to discharge the burden of proof he bears. The estimates for "cash expenditure" so made were in addition to taking into account

No details were provided in support of those calculations in relation to cheque expenses but the dates and amounts brought to account under savings account withdrawals were identified by reference to a pass-book. Once again, the Applicant neither presented any evidence to suggest those elements of the calculations were incorrect. Nor did he contend them to be incorrect. I am satisfied that, whether they be correct or not, the Applicant had no understanding of their significance in the operation of a "source and application of funds" statement.

10. When the Applicant was first interviewed by an investigating officer at his home he was invited to explain the suspected understatement of income. He was given an opportunity to explain non-assessable sources of funds. When he later called at the local Australian Taxation Office, as he was required to do for the purposes of interview, he presented a sheet listing items which he had sold; the prices for which he had sold them, as best he then recalled them; and approximately - very approximately - the year in which they had been sold. That was in November 1985. He at all times acknowledged to the Commissioner and before me that he could not identify the purchasers of the items (with the exception of the purchaser of the Toyota sedan provided for the daughter) or produce any documents to support his claims. In his words, he "had no evidence", as if his own testimony was of no account. The same view seems to have been held by the Commissioner in relation to some matters. Written records are, of course, very important. Few complicated transactions could be effectively implemented without them. But written records are not essential to ensure the successful completion of many transactions. Further, it needs to be remembered that written records - including those maintained by sophisticated computers - are no more reliable than the persons responsible for their composition. Errors may be as easily recorded in writing as expressed orally. Lies may be as readily told in writing as spoken. For those reasons no record is credible of itself. It is no more worthy of credit than the person who made it. But the existence of a record may add greatly to the weight to be attributed to the evidence of a person. The absence of such records is a serious problem for the Applicant. The list of items sold and of prices referred to is as follows:-

The sales so described could account for $34,905 plus the sale price of miscellaneous crockery. In raising the assessments the Commissioner gave credit for such sales to a total of $19,040. The difference ($15,865) is substantially accounted for by the claim to have sold a full set of florins for cash to an unidentified dealer.

  1. Nothing was advanced on behalf of the Commissioner to suggest that any element or transaction alleged by the Applicant was improbable other than the circumstance that no large receipt of moneys which might seem to have been derived from such a source was reflected in the records in the only deposit account to be evidenced. Nor was anything advanced for the Commissioner to suggest that a "full set" of florins would not have had at material times a realizable value of $15,575. Nor was the Applicant cross-examined as to how he came upon the figure of $15,575, if not by reason of experience of some such sale. Rather the case for the Commissioner rested on the asserted improbability of such a sale having occurred without it being reflected in deposits to the one deposit account produced in evidence.
  2. The Applicant denied having had any other sources of assessable income than those acknowledged in his return of income. He was cross-examined. He could not explain how the "discrepancy" arose. But that is hardly surprising because I am satisfied he did not understand the nature of a "source and applications of funds" statement. His contention was that his only income was as returned, and that all other moneys to be accounted for were to be explained by his sales of cars, coins and oddments.
  3. On the other hand the Commissioner, by his representative, pointed to several suspicious factors and asserted that, in consequence of all the foregoing, the Applicant had not discharged the burden of proof which he bears. At one stage it was in fact suggested that it could not be discharged without the production of corroborative evidence, seemingly in documentary form. (cf. ante.)
  4. In face of that conflict, it is necessary to consider whether the suspicions and doubts which exist suffice to bring the Tribunal to a conclusion that the sworn testimony of the Applicant, which has not been seriously challenged as to its honesty, is to be rejected. I am not persuaded that it should be.
  5. If the Applicant had unassessed sources of assessable income, it seems that they would have had to be:-
    • (a) from a second job, or equivalent - If so, I am of opinion that the Applicant must have been quite deliberately lying in the course of his evidence;
    • (b) a scale of activity in the way of doing up old cars and other items such as to constitute it as a business rather than as a hobby such as the Applicant asserted and the Commissioner accepted -It is not suggested that that was so; or
    • (c) receipt of payments from his employer in excess of those attributed to him on group certificates. That possibility needs to be more closely analysed.

16. The Commissioner's representative suggested that that might have been the case because the Applicant was "related" to his employer. The submission was only made in the course of closing argument and then based only on the circumstance that the surnames of the Applicant and his employer were the same. I am not persuaded that that circumstance warrants an inference of kinship. However, even if one assumes that, because of kinship or otherwise, the employer had made greater payments to the Applicant than had been acknowledged in the group certificates, two possibilities must be considered. The first is that that was done with the knowledge of the Applicant. If that was so, then I have no doubt that he must have been lying in his evidence before me. On the other hand, if such greater payments were made to the Applicant without his knowledge, there must have been some circumstance which prompted the employer to put himself at risk of substantial penalties viz-a-viz the Commissioner. There may have been such a circumstance but there was no suggestion made of it in the course of the hearing.

  1. Examination of the income tax returns made following the hearing brought to attention the circumstance that the gross remuneration for the Applicant in two successive years (1981 and 1982) was identical. That, to say the least, was unusual for any person employed at the Applicant's end of the pay scale in the early 1980's. But nothing was put to him about it. Nor was there any suggestion made that the wages said to have been paid to him by his employer were less than those due to a tow-truck operator such as the Applicant in those years of income. As the Applicant has not had an opportunity to explain those matters I am not prepared to draw any inference against him in that regard.
  2. To sum up, I have concluded that, judged on the basis that the Applicant bears the onus of satisfying me on the balance of probabilities that the assessment is excessive, I am persuaded that he has not been deliberately deceitful before me and that he was not the unwitting "beneficiary" of "black money" from his employer. I am persuaded that the amended assessments were excessive and should not have issued.

14. For those reasons, the order of the Tribunal will be that the objections of the Applicant should be allowed.
